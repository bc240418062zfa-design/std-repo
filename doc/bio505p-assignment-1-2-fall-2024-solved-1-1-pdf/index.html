RNA Purification: An Overview

a. Importance of RNA Purification in Molecular Biology
RNA purification is a critical step in molecular biology as it ensures the isolation of high-quality
RNA necessary for various downstream applications, such as gene expression analysis, RNA
sequencing, and reverse transcription PCR (RT-PCR). Pure RNA is essential to avoid
contamination that could interfere with enzymatic reactions, leading to inaccurate results or
experimental failure. High-quality RNA helps researchers understand gene regulation and
cellular processes accurately.

b. Challenges Associated with RNA Purification
RNA purification presents unique challenges due to the instability of RNA molecules and their
susceptibility to degradation by ubiquitous ribonucleases (RNases). Key challenges include:

   •   RNase Contamination: RNases are abundant in the environment, making RNA highly
       prone to degradation.
   •   Sample Quality: Poor sample storage or handling can compromise RNA integrity.
   •   Complexity of Biological Samples: Extracting RNA from tissues with high lipid or
       protein content can be difficult.
   •   Co-contaminants: DNA and proteins can co-purify with RNA, reducing the purity.

c. General Overview of Steps in RNA Purification Process
The RNA purification process generally involves the following steps:

   1. Cell Lysis: Cells are lysed to release RNA while inhibiting RNases using specialized
      reagents or buffers.
   2. Separation of RNA from Other Biomolecules: RNA is separated from DNA, proteins,
      and lipids using organic extraction (e.g., phenol-chloroform) or column-based methods.
   3. Precipitation: RNA is precipitated using ethanol or isopropanol to concentrate it.
   4. Washing and Resuspension: The RNA pellet is washed with alcohol to remove
      impurities and resuspended in RNase-free water or buffer.
   5. Quality Assessment: Purity and integrity are evaluated using spectrophotometry or gel
      electrophoresis.

Effective RNA purification ensures high-quality RNA for reliable and reproducible molecular
biology experiments.

Procedures for RNA Purification Methods

1. Kit Method (2 marks)

Commercial RNA purification kits simplify the isolation process and ensure high RNA quality
(Qiagen, n.d.). The general procedure is:

   1. Cell Lysis: Add lysis buffer (provided in the kit) to the sample to disrupt cells and release
      RNA while inactivating RNases.
   2. Binding RNA: Mix lysate with ethanol or binding buffer and apply it to a silica column
      or beads where RNA binds.
   3. Washing: Wash the bound RNA with wash buffers to remove contaminants like proteins
      and DNA.
   4. Elution: Elute purified RNA using RNase-free water or elution buffer.

These kits minimize contamination and are efficient for various sample types (Thermo Fisher
Scientific, n.d.).

2. Prizel Method (2 marks)

The Prizel method, also known as phenol-chloroform extraction, is widely used in research
(Chomczynski & Sacchi, 1987). The steps include:

   1. Homogenization: Add TRIzol reagent (phenol-based) to the sample and homogenize to
      lyse cells.
   2. Phase Separation: Add chloroform, mix, and centrifuge to separate into three layers:
      aqueous (RNA), interphase (DNA), and organic (proteins).
   3. RNA Precipitation: Transfer the aqueous layer to a new tube, then add isopropanol to
      precipitate RNA. Centrifuge to form a pellet.
   4. Washing: Wash the pellet with 70% ethanol to remove impurities.
   5. Resuspension: Dissolve the RNA pellet in RNase-free water.

This method is effective but requires careful handling to prevent contamination.

3. Manual Method (3 marks)

Manual RNA extraction is a cost-effective method suitable for resource-limited settings
(Chomczynski & Sacchi, 1987). The process involves:

   1. Lysis: Add guanidinium thiocyanate-based lysis buffer to disrupt cells and denature
      RNases.
   2. RNA Separation: Use phenol-chloroform extraction or salt precipitation to separate
      RNA from DNA and proteins.
   3. Precipitation: Add ethanol or isopropanol to precipitate RNA and centrifuge to collect
      the pellet.
   4. Washing: Wash the RNA pellet with 70% ethanol to remove residual contaminants.
   5. Resuspension: Dissolve the purified RNA in RNase-free water or buffer for storage.

While labor-intensive, this method provides high-quality RNA when performed correctly.

Collection of Samples for Karyotyping

Karyotyping is a cytogenetic test used to analyze chromosomal structures. Samples for
karyotyping can be collected from various sources depending on the clinical need:

   1.   Peripheral Blood
   2.   Bone Marrow
   3.   Amniotic Fluid
   4.   Chorionic Villus Sampling (CVS)
   5.   Skin or Tissue Biopsy
   6.   Tumor Samples
