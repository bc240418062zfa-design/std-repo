Ciona intestinalis: A Study of Its Classification, Life Cycle,
and Ecological Significance

1. Introduction

Ciona intestinalis, commonly called the sea squirt, is a marine invertebrate belonging to the
subphylum Urochordata (Tunicata). Despite their sedentary adult life, these animals possess key
chordate features such as a notochord and dorsal nerve cord during their larval stage. This unique
combination makes Ciona intestinalis a valuable model for understanding early chordate
evolution and marine ecosystem functioning.

2. Taxonomic Classification

2.1 Scientific Classification

   •   Class: Ascidiacea

   •   Family: Cionidae

   •   Species: Ciona intestinalis

3. Morphology and Anatomy

3.1 External Features

Ciona intestinalis has a soft, cylindrical, translucent body covered by a cellulose-like tunic called
tunicin. It attaches to hard surfaces like rocks or ship hulls via its base. The body includes an oral
siphon for water intake and an atrial siphon for expelling filtered water.

4. Life Cycle

4.1 Reproduction and Fertilization

Ciona intestinalis is hermaphroditic and reproduces via external fertilization. Eggs and sperm are
released into the water through the atrial siphon. Once fertilized, embryos develop rapidly.

4.2 Larval Stage

The embryo develops into a free-swimming tadpole larva that possesses all key chordate
features— a notochord, dorsal nerve cord, tail, and a brain-like structure. This stage lasts only a
few hours to promote dispersal.

4.3 Metamorphosis and Adult Stage

After swimming, the larva settles on a substrate and undergoes metamorphosis. It absorbs its tail
and notochord, reconfigures its body structure, and becomes a sessile adult that remains attached
to the surface.

5. Ecological Significance

5.1 Water Filtration and Nutrient Cycling

As a filter feeder, Ciona intestinalis removes plankton and organic particles from seawater,
contributing to water purification and nutrient cycling. One individual can filter several liters of
water per hour.

5.2 Role in Marine Habitats

Aggregations of Ciona intestinalis form microhabitats for microorganisms and juvenile marine
animals. This enhances biodiversity and stabilizes benthic ecosystems in coastal environments.

5.3 Invasive Potential and Biofouling

Introduced globally via ship ballast water, Ciona intestinalis often becomes invasive. It can
outcompete native species and cause biofouling problems on marine infrastructure and
aquaculture farms.

6. Scientific and Human Relevance

6.1 Model Organism in Research

The genome of Ciona intestinalis is compact and fully sequenced, making it ideal for
developmental and genetic studies. It provides insight into vertebrate evolution, particularly the
origin of the notochord and nervous system.

6.2 Biomedical and Industrial Applications

Compounds derived from its tunic have shown antimicrobial and antifouling properties. These
bioactive substances are being studied for pharmaceutical and marine engineering applications.

7. Conclusion

Ciona intestinalis is a key species linking invertebrates and vertebrates through its evolutionary
traits. Its biphasic lifecycle, efficient filtration, and adaptability give it both ecological
importance and scientific value. While beneficial in research and ecosystems, its invasive
behavior requires careful monitoring in non-native habitats.
