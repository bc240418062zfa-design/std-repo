Define holism with example.

A word is defined in terms of some of the very words which help us to define. This
view is similar to ‗meaning holism‘ (Quine, 1960) – the meaning of a
word/phrase/ sentence depends on its relationship with other words, e.g. part of
what makes up the meaning of ‗tall‘ is that it is opposed to the meaning of ‗short‘.
Holist theory tends to be functional in the sense that it is some aspect of the uses of
a piece of language which makes for its meaning. If you call something ‗tall‘, you
should be willing to call it ‗not short‘.

Meaning holism may be correct in a way, it does not provide a basis for the
scientific study of linguistic meaning. That is why; it has not been appreciated by
formal semanticists (Portner, 2005, p. 3-5)
Define mood and its type subjunctive mood and imperative mood.

Mood is the quality of verb which conveys a writer‘s attitude or feelings towards a
subject (Saeed, 2004). Mood also shows the feelings of a speaker at time of

speaking. For example, ‗it may rain tomorrow. ‘In this example, the speaker‘s
attitude is uncertain about the rain. There are different types of moods:

Subjunctive moods contrast with indicative moods because there is an element of
uncertainty in subjunctive moods. These are used for wishes, beliefs, exhortations,
etc. e.g. ‗May you live long! (Saeed, 2004)

Imperative moods make direct commands or requests. For example, show me the
money.
What is Core vocabulary?

Each language has a core vocabulary of more frequent and basic words are widely
used in foreign language teaching. The core vocabulary could be used to trace
lexical links between languages to establish family relationships among them. The
implication of this approach is that the membership of core vocabulary will be the
same or similar for all languages. Swadesh originally proposed a 200-word list
which was later narrowed down to the 100-word list (see Saeed, 2014: 77).

Examples of Core Vocabulary Words

   •   Pronouns: I, you, it, they
   •   Verbs: go, do, make, have
   •   Adjectives: good, big, small, happy
   •   Prepositions: in, on, at, with

Repetition concern with linguistic.

The three concerns with repetition in linguistic studies discussed in the handouts
are:

Lexical Cohesion: Repetition is a tool for connecting ideas in a text. It helps
maintain coherence by repeating key words or phrases, which links parts of the text
together.

Avoidance of Redundancy: While repetition can help with clarity, it’s important
to avoid unnecessary repetition that could disrupt the flow of communication.
Using alternative linguistic devices like substitution or ellipsis can help avoid this.

Stylistic Effect: Repetition can also serve a stylistic function, adding emphasis or
rhythm to the speech or writing. However, too much repetition can make the text
monotonous, so it should be used carefully for impact without overdoing it.

Reasons behind introducing conceptual and procedural distinctions in
relevance.

The conceptual and procedural distinctions in relevance were introduced by Diane
Blakemore (1987) to account for differences in the meanings of regular content
words and discourse connectives. Conceptual meaning refers to the content or
concepts conveyed by words (like "dog" or "red"), whereas procedural meaning
refers to words that guide how the information should be processed (such as "but,"
"so," "therefore"). This distinction helps explain how words like "but" or "so"
influence the way listeners interpret the context of a conversation.

These distinctions are important because they clarify how language can
simultaneously carry information and guide the listener on how to use that
information within the conversation, reducing cognitive effort for comprehension .
What is Linguistic botanizing?

Austin organized a group of philosophers whose work is known as ordinary
language philosophy. The prevailing method was ‗linguistic botanizing‘; to see the
distinctions made by ordinary language on the assumption that the way people
speak makes many subtle distinctions, worthy of philosophical investigation. These
philosophers were drawn into thinking about questions such as ‗what saying or
stating involves‘and ‗what else speakers do with language‘.
Objections of Grice model.
Two main objections to Grice's Cooperative Principle are:

Assuming Everyone is Always Cooperative: Grice’s model assumes that people
always cooperate in conversations, but in real life, people sometimes want to speak
independently or avoid certain topics. This means not everyone follows the rules
all the time.

Cultural Differences: People from different cultures may understand and use
conversation rules in different ways. What might be acceptable in one culture (like
being vague or indirect) could be seen as violating the rules in another culture.

These objections highlight that Grice's model doesn't always fit how real
conversations happen across different cultures.
