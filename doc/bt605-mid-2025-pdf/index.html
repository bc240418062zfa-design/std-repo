           BT605-Biosafety and Bioethics
                   Mid Term-Spring (2025)
1. Types of Patient Advance Directives

      Living Will: Specifies medical treatments a person wishes or refuses (e.g., DNR order).
      Durable Power of Attorney for Healthcare: Appoints a person to make decisions if the
       patient is incapacitated.
      Do Not Resuscitate (DNR): Legal order to not perform CPR.
      Example: A cancer patient signs a living will refusing life support.

2. Constituting Components of Elements of Acts

      Statutory authority for regulation.
      Defined rights and duties.
      Enforcement mechanisms (e.g., courts).
      Example: Patient Self-Determination Act includes patient rights and institutional
       responsibilities.

3. Ethics in Genetic Screening

      Privacy concerns about sharing results outside family.
      Religious and social objections.
      Emotional and financial stress.
      Risk of discrimination based on results.

4. Current Issues in Environmental Ethics

      Climate change and greenhouse gas emissions.
      Biodiversity loss.
      Acid rain, ozone depletion.
      Human overpopulation and bioaccumulation.

5. Biosafety vs. Biosecurity

      Biosafety: Prevents accidental exposure to pathogens.
      Biosecurity: Prevents intentional misuse of pathogens.
      Biosafety includes lab containment; biosecurity includes access control.
      Both work together to reduce risk in biotech labs.

6. Biohazards vs. Bioweapons

      Biohazards: Naturally occurring agents harmful to health (e.g., viruses).
      Bioweapons: Deliberately engineered agents used in warfare.
      Biohazards are public health threats; bioweapons are military threats.
      Example: Anthrax as biohazard vs. weaponized anthrax.

7. Burden of Genetic Information

      Potential for insurance/employment discrimination.
      Misinterpretation of results.
      Emotional and familial stress.
      Ethical concerns in privacy and data handling.

8. Patient Rights & Refusal of Treatment

      Patients can refuse any treatment.
      They must be informed of consequences.
      Right to second opinion.
      Should inform caregiver and respect facility policies.

9. WHO Pathogen Group Examples

      WHO Collaborating Centre for Biosafety (e.g., CDC USA).
      Health Canada’s Office of Laboratory Security.
      Victorian Infectious Diseases Reference Laboratory, Australia.

10. Human Impact on Biodiversity

      Deforestation and habitat destruction.
      Pollution (air, water, soil).

      Climate change altering ecosystems.
      Overexploitation of species (e.g., overfishing)

11. Genetic Screening vs. Genetic Testing

      Screening: Performed on populations to identify risks (e.g., newborn screening).
      Testing: Diagnostic for individuals with symptoms/family history.
      Screening is preventive; testing is confirmatory.
      Testing is usually more detailed than screening.

12. Ethical Codes

      Promote public interest and well-being.
      Ensure honesty and transparency in research.
      Require humane treatment of animals and protection of environment.
      Include mentorship, fair publication, and conflict-of-interest disclosure.

13. Growth of Bioethics

      Emerged in response to medical advances and ethical dilemmas.
      Interdisciplinary field combining philosophy, law, medicine.
      Encourages ethical healthcare decisions.
      Now includes genetics, environmental, and reproductive ethics.

14. Patient Rights

      Right to informed consent and treatment options.
      Right to privacy and respectful care.
      Right to refuse treatment.
      Right to continuity and quality care.

15. Biosafety

      Involves practices and containment to prevent accidental release of bioagents.
      Ensures safe lab procedures and waste disposal.
      Includes use of biosafety levels (BSL 1–4).
      Aims to protect personnel, community, and environment.

16. Ethical Issues of Nanotechnology

      Long-term health/environmental impacts unknown.
      Use in surveillance and privacy invasion.
      Equity in access to nanomedicine.
      Need for regulation and public engagement.

17. Factors Increasing Atmospheric CO₂

      Burning fossil fuels (coal, oil, gas).
      Deforestation and land-use change.
      Industrial processes and emissions.
      Agriculture and animal husbandry (e.g., methane from livestock).

18. Five Explorers of Atmospheric CO₂

      Researchers studying:
          o Deforestation impact.
          o Fossil fuel emission tracking.
          o Ocean acidification.
          o Climate modeling (e.g., IPCC).
          o Satellite monitoring (e.g., NASA’s OCO-2).

19. Victim Trafficking

      Involves exploitation (e.g., labor, sexual).
      Often linked with human rights abuses.
      Requires legal, ethical, and policy response.
      Victims face long-term trauma and health issues.

20. Why Animals Are Used in Biotechnology

      Model organisms for research (e.g., mice, rabbits).
      Production of biologics (e.g., antibodies, insulin).
      Study of genetic diseases.
      Testing of drugs and vaccines.

21. Biodiversity Affected by Human Activity

      Habitat destruction (urbanization, deforestation).
      Pollution of ecosystems.
      Introduction of invasive species.
      Overhunting and overfishing.
      Climate change altering species distribution.

22. WHO Classification of Pathogens

      Risk Group 1: Low risk (e.g., non-pathogenic E. coli).
      Risk Group 2: Moderate risk (e.g., Staphylococcus aureus).
      Risk Group 3: High individual, low community risk (e.g., M. tuberculosis).
      Risk Group 4: High individual and community risk (e.g., Ebola virus).

23. Cybernetics and Its Types

      Study of communication/control in machines/living beings.
