What is Sociolinguistics? Sociolinguistics is the study of the relationship
between language and society, examining how language use varies based on
social factors like culture, context, and identity.
Sociolinguistics and Linguistics: Sociolinguistics complements linguistics,
which focuses on language structure and grammar, by exploring language use
in social contexts.
Sociolinguistics and the Sociology of Language: Sociolinguistics overlaps
with the sociology of language, which examines language as a social
institution, but focuses more on language use and variation.
Sociolinguistics and Other Disciplines: Sociolinguistics draws from
anthropology, sociology, psychology, and education to understand language
in social contexts.
Sociolinguistic Phenomena and an Imaginary World: Sociolinguistic
phenomena, like language variation and change, can be observed in imaginary
worlds, like fictional societies or virtual communities.
Sociolinguistic Phenomena and a Real but Exotic World: Sociolinguistic
phenomena can also be studied in real but exotic cultures, like indigenous
communities or minority languages.
Sociolinguistic Phenomena and a Real and Familiar World:
Sociolinguistic phenomena are most relevant in our everyday lives, in our own
language use and interactions.
Sociolinguistic Phenomena and We: Sociolinguistics helps us understand
our own language use and its relationship to our identity, culture, and social
context.
Sociolinguistic Phenomena and the Changing World: Sociolinguistics
examines how language adapts to changing social contexts, like globalization
and migration. Sociolinguistics explores the diversity of language varieties,
including dialects, registers, and styles.

What Are Linguistics Items? Linguistic items refer to language elements
like words, phrases, and grammatical structures.
The Terms- Variety and Lect: Variety refers to a language variant, while
lect refers to a specific language or dialect.
Types and Significance of Varieties of Language: Varieties of language can
be regional, social, or functional, and understanding them is crucial for
communication and social cohesion.
Attitude towards Language Varieties: Sociolinguistics examines how
speakers' attitudes towards language varieties affect language use and identity.
What Are Speech Communities? Speech communities are groups of people
who share a common language or dialect and social norms of communication.
Speech communities can be defined by geographic location, social identity,
or language use.
Intersecting Communities: Speech communities can intersect or overlap,
leading to complex language use and identity negotiations.
Rejecting the Idea of Speech Communities: Some scholars argue that
speech communities are too simplistic a concept, and language use is more
complex and dynamic.
Networks and Repertoires
    - Networks refer to the social connections and relationships between
       speakers
    - Repertoires refer to the range of language varieties and styles that
       speakers have access to and use in different contexts
LANGUAGE CONTACT AND VARIATION
    - Language contact occurs when speakers of different languages or
       varieties interact and influence each other's language use
    - Variation refers to the different forms and uses of language that emerge
       from language contact and change
Sociolinguistics      Constraints on language Contact: Sociolinguistic
constraints refer to the social factors that influence language contact and
variation, such as social status, power dynamics, and cultural norms
Wave Model of Language Contact and Change: The wave model describes
how language change spreads through a population, with new forms and uses
of language emerging and spreading like waves

Spatial Diffusion by Gravity: Spatial diffusion refers to the spread of
language variations through geographic space, with language change
spreading from central to peripheral areas
Access to the Codes: Access to the codes refers to the ability of speakers to
use and understand different language varieties and styles
Rigidity of the Social Matrix
    - The social matrix refers to the social structures and norms that shape
       language use and variation
    - Rigidity refers to the degree to which the social matrix restricts or
       allows language change and variation
Variables and Variants
    - Variables refer to the social and linguistic factors that influence
       language use and variation
    - Variants refer to the different forms and uses of language that emerge
       from language contact and change
Types of Variables and Variants
    - Synchronic variation refers to the different language varieties and
       styles used by speakers at a given time
    - Diachronic variation refers to the change and development of
       language over time
    - Example: A synchronic variant may be the use of a local dialect, while
       a diachronic variant may be the emergence of a new language variety
       over time.
Language Varieties: Language varieties refer to the different forms and uses
of language that emerge from language contact and change
Difference Between a Language and a Dialect:
    - A language is a system of communication with a shared grammar and
       vocabulary
    - A dialect is a variety of a language with its own distinct grammar,
       vocabulary, and pronunciation
Some Examples of Dialects:
    - Regional dialects (e.g. Southern American English)
    - Social dialects (e.g. African American Vernacular English)
    - Historical dialects (e.g. Old English)

Standard Languages: Standard languages are varieties of language that are
widely accepted and used in formal situations. Example: Standard American
English is a standard language used in formal situations, while a local dialect
may be used in informal situations.
The Delimitation of Languages: The delimitation of languages refers to the
process of defining and distinguishing between languages and dialects
The Family Tree Model: The family tree model describes the historical
relationships between languages, with languages branching off from a
common ancestor
