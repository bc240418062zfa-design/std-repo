        1. Factors that affect antigen and antibody tests (3):
              • Sample quality and handling: The condition and handling of the sample
                  can affect test results. For example, contaminated or degraded samples
                  can lead to false negatives or false positives.
              • Test sensitivity and specificity: The test’s ability to detect the antigen or
                  antibody accurately can impact results. A test with low sensitivity may
                  miss detecting the antigen, while a test with low specificity may detect
                  non-specific substances.
              • Interfering substances or cross-reactivity: Substances in the sample
                  can interfere with the test or react with the test reagents, leading to
                  inaccurate results.
        2. Antibody formation (5):
             • Antigen presentation to B cells: Antigens are processed and presented
                 to B cells, which recognize them through their surface-bound antibodies.
             • Activation and proliferation of B cells: Activated B cells multiply and
                 differentiate into plasma cells, which produce antibodies.
             • Differentiation into plasma cells: B cells mature into plasma cells,
                 which specialize in antibody production.
             • Class switching and affinity maturation: B cells can switch to
                 producing different antibody classes (e.g., IgG, IgA) and undergo affinity
                 maturation to produce high-affinity antibodies.
             • Antibody production and secretion: Plasma cells produce and secrete
                 antibodies, which enter the bloodstream and other bodily fluids.
        3. MHC and its classes (5):
              • Major Histocompatibility Complex (MHC): A group of genes that play a
                  critical role in the immune response.
              • MHC Class I: Presents intracellular antigens to CD8+ T cells (cytotoxic T
                  cells), which kill infected cells.
              • MHC Class II: Presents extracellular antigens to CD4+ T cells (helper T
                  cells), which coordinate immune responses.
              • MHC Class III: Involved in the complement system and inflammation.

     •   MHC genes are highly polymorphic: Many variations exist, allowing for
         greater diversity in immune responses.
4. Usage of monoclonal antibodies (5):
     • Cancer treatment (e.g., rituximab): Targets cancer cells for destruction.
     • Autoimmune disease treatment (e.g., adalimumab): Blocks
        inflammatory pathways.
     • Infectious disease treatment (e.g., palivizumab): Neutralizes
        pathogens.
     • Diagnostic tests (e.g., flow cytometry): Identifies and characterizes
        cells.
     • Research applications (e.g., immunohistochemistry): Studies protein
        expression and localization.

5. Lymphocyte recirculation (3):
     • Lymphocytes continuously circulate between lymphoid organs and
        blood: Allows for immune surveillance and rapid response.
     • Regulated by adhesion molecules and chemokines: Controls
        lymphocyte migration and localization.

6. Classes of antibodies:
      • IgA: Found in mucosal surfaces, provides local immunity.
      • IgD: Expressed on mature B cells, activates antibody production.
      • IgE: Involved in allergic reactions and parasite immunity.
      • IgG: Most common antibody class, provides long-term immunity.
      • IgM: First response to infections, activates complement system.

7. Physical barriers of innate immune system:
     • Skin and mucous membranes: Prevents pathogen entry.
     • Respiratory tract cilia and mucus: Traps and removes pathogens.
     • Gastrointestinal tract acidity and enzymes: Kills pathogens.
     • Blood-brain barrier: Restricts pathogen entry into the central nervous
         system.
     • Eye and ear protective mechanisms: Prevents pathogen entry.
8. Anaphylaxis:
Anaphylaxsis is the form of immune response in which exaggerated response is
generated against Allergen. The molecules which produced such response are
called as anaphylatoxins.

              •   Severe, systemic allergic reaction.
              •   IgE-mediated release of histamine and other mediators.
              •   Symptoms include hives, itching, swelling, and anaphylactic shock.

         9. Affinity maturation: repeat.3
                • Process of increasing antibody affinity for antigens.
                • Occurs through somatic hypermutation and selection.
                • Results in higher antibody efficacy and specificity.
         10. Characteristics of antibodies:
               • Specificity for antigens: Recognizes specific epitopes.
               • Affinity for antigens: Strength of antibody-antigen binding.
               • Valence (number of binding sites): Determines antibody functionality.
               • Isotype (class) and subclass: Defines antibody function and
                   localization.
               • Idiotype (unique epitope recognition): Determines antibody specificity.

11 : Heptan definition:

Heptan is a hydrocarbon molecule with seven carbon atoms (C7H16). It is a volatile organic
compound and a solvent.

   12 : Immunology and antigen difference:

Immunology: Study of the immune system, including its functions, disorders, and
responses to pathogens and antigens.

Antigen: A substance that triggers an immune response, recognized by immune cells (e.g.,
proteins, peptides, carbohydrates).
