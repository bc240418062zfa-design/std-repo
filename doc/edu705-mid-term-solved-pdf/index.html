Q. Methodology explain
The methodology of a research study outlines the procedures and techniques used to collect,
analyze, and interpret data. It typically includes the research design (qualitative, quantitative, or
mixed methods), sampling methods (random, stratified, convenience), data collection tools
(surveys, interviews, experiments), and data analysis techniques (statistical analysis, thematic
analysis, content analysis). The methodology ensures the research is systematic, valid, and reliable,
providing a clear framework for replicating the study and verifying results. It also addresses ethical
considerations, limitations, and the rationale behind the chosen methods, aligning them with the
research objectives and questions.

Q. Feature of abstract
A key feature of an abstract is its conciseness, offering a succinct summary of the main points of
a larger work such as a research paper, thesis, or report. It typically includes the purpose of the
study, the research methods employed, the main results or findings, and the conclusions drawn.
The abstract allows readers to quickly ascertain the paper's relevance and significance, providing
a snapshot that helps them decide whether to read the full document. It should be clear, precise,
and free of unnecessary details, ensuring that even those unfamiliar with the full text can
understand the essence of the work.

Q. What is the choice of tense in the citing statement?
The choice of tense in citing statements depends on the context of the reference. Present tense is
used when discussing the current relevance or established facts from a study, such as "Smith (2020)
argues that social media influences consumer behavior." Past tense is appropriate for referring to
specific experiments or studies conducted in the past, for example, "Johnson (2018) found that
increased exercise improves mental health." Present perfect tense is used to discuss past research
with ongoing relevance, like "Researchers have shown that climate change affects biodiversity
(Green et al., 2015)." Selecting the appropriate tense helps clarify the relationship between past
research and its current implications.

Q. Where is 'Hedging' used in the reporting results? Explain.
Hedging is used in reporting results, particularly in academic and scientific contexts, to convey
uncertainty, caution, and modesty about findings. It allows authors to acknowledge limitations,
avoid overstatements, and respect the scientific process by using language that softens or qualifies
statements. Phrases like "may," "might," "could," "suggests," and "is likely to" help present
findings accurately, indicating that results are not absolute or universally applicable. For example,
"The results suggest a potential link between diet and heart health" is more accurate than making
a definitive claim. Hedging ensures claims are appropriately cautious, inviting critical discussion
and further inquiry.
