Question no : 02 Mutation!

A mutation is a change in the DNA sequence of an organism. It can occur naturally or
be induced by external factors.

Types of Mutations

1. *Point mutation*: A single nucleotide base is changed.

2. *Frameshift mutation*: Insertion or deletion of nucleotides changes the reading frame.

3. *Chromosomal mutation*: Changes in the number or structure of chromosomes.

4. *Gene mutation*: Affects a single gene or a small group of genes.

Causes of Mutations

1. *Errors during DNA replication*

2. *Environmental factors* (e.g., radiation, chemicals)

3. *Viral infections*

4. *Genetic recombination*

Effects of Mutations

1. *Neutral*: No significant effect on the organism.

2. *Beneficial*: Increases fitness or provides a new trait.

3. *Deleterious*: Harmful or reduces fitness.

4. *Lethal*: Causes death or prevents reproduction.

Examples of Mutations

1. *Sickle cell anemia*: A point mutation in the hemoglobin gene.

2. *Cystic fibrosis*: A frameshift mutation in the CFTR gene.

3. *Antibiotic resistance*: Mutations in bacterial genes confer resistance.

Mutations are an essential part of evolution, as they provide the raw material for natural
selection to act upon.

3 Recessive Epistasis!

Recessive epistasis occurs when two or more genes interact to produce a phenotype, and
one gene masks the effect of the other gene(s) only when the masking gene is homozygous
recessive.

Conditions for Recessive Epistasis

1. Two or more genes are involved.

2. One gene (the “masking” gene) has a recessive allele.

3. The masking gene is homozygous recessive (rr).

4. The effect of the other gene(s) is masked or hidden.

Example of Recessive Epistasis

1. _Seed color in peas_: Gene A controls seed color (A = yellow, a = green). Gene B controls
the expression of gene A (B = expresses A, b = masks A).

2. When gene B is homozygous recessive (bb), it masks the effect of gene A, and the seeds
are always green, regardless of the genotype at gene A.

4 Physical Mapping

1. *Definition*: A physical map is a representation of the physical distance between genes
or markers on a chromosome.

2. *Unit of measurement*: Base pairs (bp) or kilobase pairs (kbp).

3. *Techniques*: Fluorescence in situ hybridization (FISH), chromosome walking, and DNA
sequencing.

4. *Information*: Provides information on the physical location of genes and their relative
distances.

Genetic Mapping

1. *Definition*: A genetic map is a representation of the recombination frequency between
genes or markers on a chromosome.

2. *Unit of measurement*: Centimorgans (cM) or map units (m.u.).

3. *Techniques*: Linkage analysis, pedigree analysis, and association studies.

4. *Information*: Provides information on the genetic linkage between genes and their
relative distances based on recombination frequencies.

Key differences:

- *Physical vs. Genetic distance*: Physical maps measure physical distance, while genetic
maps measure genetic distance based on recombination frequencies.

- *Units of measurement*: Physical maps use base pairs or kilobase pairs, while genetic
maps use centimorgans or map units.

- *Techniques*: Physical mapping involves techniques like FISH and DNA sequencing,
while genetic mapping involves linkage analysis and pedigree analysis.

some common molecular techniques used to study proteins:

Separation and Identification Techniques

1. *SDS-PAGE (Sodium Dodecyl Sulfate-Polyacrylamide Gel Electrophoresis)*: Separates
proteins based on size and charge.

2. *Western Blotting*: Detects specific proteins using antibodies.

3. *Mass Spectrometry (MS)*: Identifies proteins based on mass-to-charge ratio.

Structural Analysis Techniques

1. *X-ray Crystallography*: Determines 3D structure of proteins.

2. *Nuclear Magnetic Resonance (NMR) Spectroscopy*: Analyzes protein structure and
dynamics.

3. *Cryo-Electron Microscopy (Cryo-EM)*: Visualizes protein structure at high resolution.

Functional Analysis Techniques

1. *Enzyme-Linked Immunosorbent Assay (ELISA)*: Measures protein activity and
interactions.

2. *Protein-Protein Interaction (PPI) Assays*: Studies protein interactions using techniques
like yeast two-hybrid and co-immunoprecipitation.

3. *Biochemical Assays*: Measures enzyme activity, protein stability, and other functional
properties.

Intronic sequences!

Introns are non-coding regions of a gene that are removed by RNA splicing before the
mature messenger RNA (mRNA) is formed. Intronic sequences are the DNA sequences that
make up these non-coding regions.

Characteristics of Intronic Sequences

1. _Non-coding_: Intronic sequences do not code for amino acids.

2. _Intervening sequences_: Introns are located between exons, which are the coding
regions of a gene.

3. _Removed by splicing_: Intronic sequences are removed from the pre-mRNA transcript
during RNA splicing.

4. _Variable length_: Introns can vary greatly in length, from a few hundred to tens of
thousands of base pairs.

5. _Regulatory elements_: Introns can contain regulatory elements, such as enhancers or
silencers, that influence gene expression.
