Short Questions

   1. Difference Between Animal and Vegetal Hemisphere:

         o   The animal hemisphere of an egg is the region that contains the majority of the
             cytoplasm, where smaller cells are formed during cleavage. It has less yolk
             compared to the vegetal hemisphere.

         o   The vegetal hemisphere is the region that contains a higher concentration of
             yolk, making it denser. It generally forms larger cells during cleavage and is
             involved in providing nutrients to the developing embryo.
   2. Difference Between Sex and Reproduction:
         o   Sex refers to the biological differences between males and females, specifically
             their roles in the production of gametes (sperm and eggs). It is typically a
             biological characteristic related to mating.

         o   Reproduction is the broader biological process of producing offspring. It can
             occur via sexual reproduction, where two gametes fuse, or asexual
             reproduction, where offspring are produced without gamete fusion.

   3. 3-Stage Cleavage in Sea Urchin:

         o   First Cleavage: This occurs in the animal-vegetal axis and is meridional
             (vertical). It splits the zygote into two equal blastomeres.

         o   Second Cleavage: This occurs perpendicular to the first cleavage and is also
             meridional, resulting in four blastomeres.

         o   Third Cleavage: This is an equatorial cleavage that divides the cells further,
             resulting in the 8-cell stage. The cells are typically smaller in the animal
             hemisphere and larger in the vegetal hemisphere.

Long Questions

   1. Explain Protostome:

         o   Protostomes are a group of animals that undergo a specific pattern of embryonic
             development. In protostomes, the blastopore (the first opening during
             gastrulation) becomes the mouth, while the anus forms later. They exhibit spiral
             cleavage, where the cells divide at an angle to one another, and their fate is often
             determined early (determinate cleavage). Examples of protostomes include
             arthropods, mollusks, and annelids.
   2. Composition of Yolk:

            o   Yolk is a nutrient-rich substance that provides essential energy for the developing
                embryo. It primarily contains lipids (fats), proteins, carbohydrates, and
                vitamins. The yolk's composition can vary between species. In some species, yolk
                is concentrated in one hemisphere of the egg (vegetal hemisphere), influencing
                the type of cleavage and the mode of development (e.g., determinate or
                indeterminate).

     3. Difference Between Cytokinesis and Karyokinesis:

            o   Karyokinesis refers to the division of the nucleus during cell division, which
                occurs during mitosis or meiosis. It ensures that each daughter cell receives an
                equal set of chromosomes.
            o   Cytokinesis is the process of dividing the cytoplasm and other cellular
                components, leading to the formation of two distinct daughter cells. Cytokinesis
                usually follows karyokinesis and ensures the complete division of the cell into
                two separate entities.

            o   Key Difference: Karyokinesis is nuclear division, while cytokinesis is the
                division of the cytoplasm and cell membrane.

Karyokinesis
Karyokinesis is the division of a cell's nucleus during the process of cell division. It ensures the
accurate distribution of the cell's genetic material (DNA) into two daughter nuclei. This process
is typically divided into phases: prophase, metaphase, anaphase, and telophase.

Cytokinesis

Cytokinesis is the division of the cell's cytoplasm, resulting in the formation of two separate
daughter cells. It occurs after karyokinesis and involves the formation of a cleavage furrow in
animal cells or a cell plate in plant cells to separate the two cells.

Both processes are essential for mitosis and meiosis.

Gastrulation
Gastrulation is a crucial phase in embryonic development where the single-layered blastula
reorganizes into a multilayered structure called the gastrula. This process involves cell migration
and differentiation, leading to the formation of three germ layers:
     1. Ectoderm: Develops into the skin, nervous system, and sensory organs.

     2. Mesoderm: Forms muscles, bones, blood, and internal organs like the heart and kidneys.

     3. Endoderm: Gives rise to the lining of the digestive tract, respiratory system, and
        associated organs like the liver and pancreas.

Gastrulation sets the foundation for organogenesis and the overall body plan of the organism.

Steps in Fertilization

     1. Sperm Capacitation: Sperm undergoes physiological changes to become capable of
        fertilizing an egg.
     2. Acrosome Reaction: Enzymes from the sperm's acrosome digest the egg's outer layers
        (zona pellucida).
     3. Sperm-Egg Binding: The sperm binds to receptors on the egg's surface.

     4. Plasma Membrane Fusion: The sperm and egg membranes fuse, allowing the sperm
        nucleus to enter the egg.

     5. Cortical Reaction: The egg releases cortical granules to prevent polyspermy (entry of
        additional sperm).

     6. Nuclear Fusion: The sperm and egg nuclei merge to form a zygote with a complete
        diploid set of chromosomes.

Significance of Fertilization

     1. Restores Diploidy: Combines haploid gametes to form a diploid zygote.

     2. Genetic Variation: Creates a unique genetic combination, promoting diversity.

     3. Initiates Development: Activates the egg to begin cell division and embryogenesis.
