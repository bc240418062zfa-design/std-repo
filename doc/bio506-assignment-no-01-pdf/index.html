Question no 1:
 (Critical Scenario: Buffer Preparation Challenge)
Your lab receives an unstable plant extract requiring precise pH conditions for
carbohydrate isolation. You are asked to prepare 200 mL of 20 mM sodium phosphate
buffer at pH 7.0.

Part 1:
Explain how you would calculate and prepare the buffer using appropriate salt
combinations?

Solution:

Data:
Preparation of 20 mM Sodium Phosphate Buffer with a pH of 7.0

Use the Henderson-Hasselbalch equation to prepare 200 mL of 20 mM sodium phosphate buffer
with a pH of 7.0:

pH = pKa + log([A⁻]/[HA])

With phosphate buffer, we have pKa₂ ≈ 7.2.

Rearranged:

7.0 = 7.2 + log([A⁻]/[HA])

Thus,

log([A⁻]/[HA]) = -0.2

Therefore,

[A⁻]/[HA] = 10^(-0.2) which is approximately equal to 0.63.

Assuming total concentration = [A⁻] + [HA] = 20mM,

yields [A-] = 0.63 × [HA].

Solving yields,

[HA] + 0.63[HA] = 20 → 1.63[HA] = 20 → [HA] = 12.27 mM, [A-] = 20 - [HA] = 7.73 mM.

Now to calculate moles for 0.2L,

Moles HA = 12.27 × 10⁻³ × 0.2 = 2.45mmol

And for A-:

Moles A- = 7.73 × 10⁻³ × 0.2 = 1.55mmol.

Weigh 2.45 mmol of NaH₂PO₄ and 1.55 mmol of Na₂HPO₄ to achieve the calculated values. The
next order of steps would be to dissolve into ~150 mL of distilled water, calibrate solution pH
values if needed and then dilute to a 200mL solution. Make sure everything is mixed uniformly
and then store under the appropriate conditions.

Part2:

Importance of Buffering in Amylose and Amylopectin Isolation

The Importance of Buffering in the Isolation of Amylose and Amylopectin

Buffering is crucial in the isolation of amylose and amylopectin because it helps maintain a
stable pH environment, which is essential for preserving the structure and solubility of these
starch components. Amylose is mostly linear, while amylopectin is highly branched, and their
solubility and behavior can be significantly affected by pH. If the pH fluctuates, especially
toward highly acidic or basic conditions, it can lead to hydrolysis of glycosidic bonds or
denaturation of associated proteins. This may result in the degradation or alteration of the
polysaccharide structures, interfering with their separation and accurate analysis. A stable pH
provided by buffering agents prevents such undesirable reactions, ensuring clear differentiation
between amylose and amylopectin during the isolation process. Therefore, buffering not only
protects the molecular integrity of these polysaccharides but also enhances the reliability and
reproducibility of the experimental results.

 Question no 3:
Carbohydrates Testing
Using your theoretical understanding, describe the chemical interaction of iodine with
different carbohydrate types (monosaccharides, disaccharides, and polysaccharides). Why
does iodine selectively produce a blue-black complex with starch but not with glucose or
sucrose? Use your understanding of molecular structure to justify your answer.

Answer:
Iodine interacts differently with various carbohydrate types due to differences in their molecular
structures. When iodine is used in carbohydrate testing, it reacts specifically with
polysaccharides like starch, but not with monosaccharides (e.g., glucose) or disaccharides (e.g.,
sucrose).

Monosaccharides and disaccharides are relatively small molecules with simple, linear or
slightly branched structures. They do not have the complex, coiled structures necessary to
interact with iodine in a significant way. Glucose and sucrose dissolve in water to form
individual molecules that do not provide a suitable environment for iodine molecules to be
trapped or form a visible complex. Therefore, no color change is observed when iodine is added
to these sugars.

Starch, a polysaccharide, has a unique helical structure, particularly in its amylose component.
Amylose consists of long, unbranched chains of glucose units that form a spiral (helix). When
iodine is added to starch, the iodine molecules fit inside the helical structure of amylose. This
forms a charge-transfer complex, resulting in a deep blue-black color. This reaction is both
specific and sensitive to the structural conformation of amylose.

Amylopectin, the branched form of starch, also interacts with iodine but produces a
reddish-brown color due to its branched structure, which does not allow full insertion of iodine
into helices.

Conclusion
 iodine forms a blue-black complex with starch due to the presence of helical structures in
amylose, which can trap iodine molecules. The absence of such helical structures in glucose and
sucrose explains the lack of a visible reaction.
