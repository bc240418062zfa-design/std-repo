1-Mitosis vs. Meiosis:
Mitosis is a process of cell division that results in two genetically identical diploid daughter cells, used for growth, repair, and asexual reproduction.
Meiosis is a type of cell division that reduces the chromosome number by half, producing four genetically diverse haploid cells (gametes) used in sexual reproduction.
2. Trisomy and Its Types:
Trisomy is a genetic condition where an individual has three copies of a chromosome instead of the usual two.
Trisomy 21 (Down syndrome): Three copies of chromosome 21.
Trisomy 18 (Edwards syndrome): Three copies of chromosome 18.
Trisomy 13 (Patau syndrome): Three copies of chromosome 13.
3. Events of Prophase I (Meiosis):
Leptotene: Chromosomes begin to condense.
Zygotene: Homologous chromosomes start pairing up (synapsis).
Pachytene: Crossing over occurs between homologous chromosomes.
Diplotene: Chromosomes start to separate, and chiasmata (crossing-over points) are visible.
Diakinesis: Chromosomes fully condense and prepare for metaphase.
4. Epistasis and Examples:
Epistasis is when one gene suppresses the expression of another gene at a different locus.
Example 1: In Labrador retrievers, the E gene (for pigmentation) can suppress the B gene (for black or brown fur), resulting in yellow fur regardless of the B allele.
Example 2: In humans, the presence of an allele for albinism (recessive) can mask the expression of other eye color genes.
5. Types of Chromosomes:
Metacentric: Centromere in the middle, resulting in two equal arms.
Submetacentric: Centromere slightly off-center, resulting in one short arm and one long arm.
Acrocentric: Centromere near one end, with a very short arm.
Telocentric: Centromere at the very end of the chromosome, resulting in only one arm.
6. DNA Mapping Steps:
Step 1: Identify the gene or genetic marker of interest.
Step 2: Create a genetic map using molecular markers (such as RFLPs, SNPs).
Step 3: Cross individuals that are heterozygous for the gene and analyze the offspring.
Step 4: Calculate recombination frequency to determine the distance between markers.
7. Polygenic Traits with Examples:
Polygenic traits are influenced by multiple genes and show continuous variation.
Example 1: Human height (controlled by many genes).
Example 2: Skin color (affected by multiple genes that control melanin production).
8. Staining Procedures:
Giemsa Staining: Used for staining chromosomes, providing a G-band pattern for karyotyping.
Fluorescence in situ Hybridization (FISH): Uses fluorescent probes to visualize specific genetic sequences.
Hematoxylin and Eosin (H&E): Common for tissue staining in histology to distinguish cell structures.
9. Law of Independent Assortment:
Mendel's Law of Independent Assortment states that genes located on different chromosomes are inherited independently of each other during gamete formation.
Example: In a dihybrid cross of pea plants (AaBb x AaBb), the alleles for seed color (A/a) and seed shape (B/b) assort independently into gametes, leading to a 9:3:3:1 phenotypic ratio in the F2 generation.
10. Chromosomal Aberration:
Chromosomal aberration refers to changes in the structure or number of chromosomes. Types include:
Aneuploidy: Abnormal number of chromosomes (e.g., trisomy, monosomy).
Structural aberrations:
Deletion: A segment of a chromosome is missing.
Duplication: A segment of a chromosome is duplicated.
Inversion: A segment of a chromosome is reversed.
Translocation: A piece of one chromosome is transferred to another chromosome.

11-Types of Chromosomes
Metacentric: The centromere is near the center of the chromosome, creating two arms of approximately equal length.
Submetacentric: The centromere is slightly off-center, making one arm slightly longer than the other.
Acrocentric: The centromere is closer to one end, resulting in one very short arm and one long arm.
Telocentric: The centromere is at the very end of the chromosome, and only one arm is present.
12-Purines vs. Pyrimidines
Purines: Larger, double-ringed structures. Examples: Adenine (A) and Guanine (G).
Pyrimidines: Smaller, single-ringed structures. Examples: Cytosine (C), Thymine (T), and Uracil (U) (in RNA).
13-Negative and Positive Epistasis
Positive Epistasis: One gene enhances the expression of another gene. Example: In the coat color of dogs, one gene (B) affects the intensity of color determined by another gene (A).
Negative Epistasis: One gene suppresses the expression of another gene. Example: The ee genotype for the yellow coat color in Labrador Retrievers can suppress the expression of the B allele, causing yellow fur regardless of the B gene's dominance.

15-Difference Between Meiosis and Mitosis
Mitosis:
A type of cell division that results in two genetically identical diploid daughter cells.
Occurs in somatic cells for growth, repair, and asexual reproduction.
Phases: Prophase, Metaphase, Anaphase, Telophase, followed by Cytokinesis.
Results in two cells with the same chromosome number as the parent.
Meiosis:
A type of cell division that results in four haploid cells (gametes) with half the chromosome number of the parent.
Occurs in reproductive organs (ovaries/testes) for sexual reproduction.
Phases: Meiosis I (separation of homologous chromosomes) and Meiosis II (separation of sister chromatids).
Introduces genetic variation through crossing over and independent assortment.
