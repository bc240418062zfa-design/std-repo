Phases of a Muscle Twitch (2 Marks)

A muscle twitch consists of three distinct phases:

   1. Latent Period:
      This is the initial delay after the stimulus is applied but before contraction begins (usually
      less than 5 milliseconds). During this time, the action potential travels along the
      sarcolemma and triggers calcium release from the sarcoplasmic reticulum.

   2. Contraction Phase:
      In this phase, the muscle fibers actively shorten as actin and myosin filaments interact via
      cross-bridge cycling. The tension increases and the muscle produces force.
   3. Relaxation Phase:
      Calcium ions are actively pumped back into the sarcoplasmic reticulum. Cross-bridges
      detach, tension declines, and the muscle returns to its resting state.

Summation and Tetanus Due to Increased Stimulation Frequency (3 Marks)

When a muscle fiber is stimulated before it has completely relaxed from a previous twitch, the
new stimulus adds to the force of the first—this is known as temporal summation or wave
summation. The increased frequency allows calcium ions to accumulate in the sarcoplasm,
resulting in a stronger contraction because the muscle cannot fully relax.

If the stimulation becomes frequent enough (i.e., multiple stimuli delivered at short intervals),
the individual twitches fuse into one continuous contraction called tetanus.

There are two types:

   •   Incomplete (Unfused) Tetanus: Muscle exhibits brief periods of partial relaxation
       between contractions.
   •   Complete (Fused) Tetanus: No relaxation occurs; a smooth, sustained contraction is
       maintained.

In tetanus, calcium reuptake is overwhelmed, and cross-bridge cycling continues uninterrupted,
leading to a maximum, steady tension. This physiological mechanism ensures smooth body
movements rather than jerky twitching.
