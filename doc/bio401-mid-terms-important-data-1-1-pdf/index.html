                             Subjective Questions

1. Types of Sampling
   Sampling types include Simple Random Sampling (each member has an equal chance),
   Stratified Sampling (population divided into subgroups or strata with proportional
   samples), Systematic Sampling (samples taken at regular intervals, such as every nth
   individual), and Cluster Sampling (population divided into clusters, and entire clusters
   are randomly chosen). Each method improves data representativeness depending on the
   population structure.
2. Systematic and Cluster Probability
   Systematic Sampling involves selecting every nth element, which is efficient but may
   introduce bias if there is a periodic pattern. Cluster Sampling involves dividing the
   population into clusters and randomly selecting some clusters, studying all elements
   within selected clusters. This method is efficient for geographically spread populations.
3. Comparison between Population and Sampling
   A population includes all individuals or items of interest, while a sample is a
   representative subset. Sampling is essential when studying an entire population is
   impractical. Populations yield parameters (e.g., population mean), while samples provide
   statistics (e.g., sample mean) that approximate the parameters with a margin of error.

4. Branches of Statistics
   Statistics branches into Descriptive and Inferential Statistics. Descriptive Statistics
summarize data (e.g., mean, median), while Inferential Statistics use sample data to draw
conclusions about a population (e.g., hypothesis testing). Descriptive statistics offer insight
    into data patterns, while inferential statistics extend insights to broader populations.
5. Quantitative and Qualitative Variables
   Variables can be Quantitative (numerical, measuring quantities, e.g., height) or
   Qualitative (categorical, describing attributes, e.g., color). Quantitative variables may be
   discrete (countable, e.g., number of students) or continuous (within a range, e.g.,
   weight). Variable types determine the choice of statistical tests.
6. Example of Nominal Scale
   Nominal scales classify data without a quantitative relationship. For example, nationality
   (e.g., American, Canadian) has categories with no inherent order. Nominal scales are used
   in qualitative data analysis to categorize and count observations.
7. How We Select a Sample in Cluster Sampling
   In Cluster Sampling, a population is divided into clusters (e.g., by region), then a few
   clusters are randomly selected. All members within selected clusters are sampled. This
   method reduces cost for geographically dispersed populations but may introduce cluster
   bias if clusters differ significantly.
8. Formula of Variance
   Variance measures data spread by averaging squared deviations from the mean. The
   formula is:
   Variance (σ²) = Σ(x - x̄)² / N where Σ(x - x̄)² represents the sum of squared deviations
   from the mean x̄, and N is the total number of observations. Variance is fundamental for
   calculating standard deviation.
9. Random and Biased Error
   Errors in data are either Random (unpredictable fluctuations) or Biased (Systematic),
   where results skew in one direction due to methodological flaws. Controlling for bias is
   crucial for accurate and reliable results.

10.Sampling Types and Why We Need Samples
   Different sampling types (random, stratified, systematic, cluster) help create
   representative samples, which are essential when studying every population member is
   impractical. Samples allow researchers to infer conclusions about the population
   costeffectively.
11. Types of Clusters
   Clusters represent natural groupings within a population, such as regions or schools. In
   Cluster Sampling, entire clusters are randomly selected, and all individuals within
   chosen clusters are studied. Proper cluster selection helps ensure data representativeness.
12. What is Data and Source of Data?
   Data is information collected for analysis. Primary data is gathered firsthand (e.g.,
   surveys), while Secondary data is from existing sources (e.g., publications). Primary
   data offers tailored insights, while secondary data is often more accessible.
13. Types of Distribution
   Common distributions include Normal Distribution (bell-shaped, symmetric), Binomial
   Distribution (two outcomes with fixed probabilities), Poisson Distribution (rare events
   over time intervals), and Uniform Distribution (equal probability for all outcomes).
14. Box and Whisker Plot
   A box and whisker plot displays data distribution, showing the median, quartiles, and
   outliers. The box spans Q1 to Q3, with whiskers extending 1.5 times the interquartile
   range (IQR), while outliers are plotted individually.
15. Pie Chart
   A pie chart is a circular chart divided into slices, with each slice representing a data
   proportion. It’s useful for showing parts of a whole but is limited for detailed
   comparisons, best for categorical data with a few categories.
16. Chebyshev's Inequality
   Chebyshev’s Inequality states that for any dataset, at least (1 - 1/k²) of data lies within k
   standard deviations of the mean, where k > 1. It’s valuable for datasets that don’t follow
   normal distribution, establishing bounds on data spread.

17. Definitions of Types of Sampling
   Sampling types include Random Sampling (equal selection chance), Systematic
   Sampling (fixed intervals), Stratified Sampling (population divided into subgroups with
   samples from each), and Cluster Sampling (whole groups sampled).
