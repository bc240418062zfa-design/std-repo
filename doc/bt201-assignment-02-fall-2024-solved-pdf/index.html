Impact of Invasive Species on Native Biodiversity in Ecosystems

Introduction

Invasive species, defined as non-native organisms introduced into ecosystems where they
become harmful, pose a significant threat to global biodiversity. Their presence disrupts
ecological balances, often leading to the decline or extinction of native species. This essay
critically evaluates the impact of invasive species on native biodiversity, contrasts eradication
and management strategies, and supports the discussion with real-world examples.

Effects of Invasive Species on Native Biodiversity

Invasive species affect native biodiversity through various mechanisms:

   1. Competition for Resources
      Invasive species often outcompete native species for essential resources such as food,
      water, and shelter. They can grow rapidly due to the absence of natural predators,
      displacing native organisms. For example, the zebra mussel (Dreissena polymorpha) in
      North American freshwater ecosystems competes with native mussels for space and
      nutrients, leading to declines in native populations.
   2. Predation and Parasitism
      Some invasive species prey on or parasitize native species, reducing their populations or
      driving them to extinction. A notable example is the introduction of the Nile perch (Lates
      niloticus) in Lake Victoria, which led to the extinction of hundreds of native cichlid fish
      species.
   3. Habitat Modification
      Invasive species can alter habitats, making them unsuitable for native species. For
      instance, invasive plants like kudzu (Pueraria montana) grow aggressively, smothering
      native vegetation and altering ecosystems in the southeastern United States.

   4. Introduction of Diseases
      Invasive species can introduce diseases to native populations. The American chestnut
      (Castanea dentata), once a dominant tree in North American forests, was nearly wiped
      out by chestnut blight, a fungal disease introduced with non-native Asian chestnut trees.
   5. Disruption of Ecosystem Services
      The alteration of ecosystems by invasive species can degrade services such as water
      filtration, pollination, and soil fertility. This impacts not only biodiversity but also human
      livelihoods.

Eradication Versus Management Strategies

To mitigate the impacts of invasive species, eradication and management are two primary
approaches. Both have their advantages, limitations, and contextual applicability.

Eradication Strategies

Eradication involves completely removing invasive species from an ecosystem. It is most
effective when the invasion is detected early and confined to a small area.

Advantages:

   •   Permanently eliminates the threat.
   •   Prevents further ecological and economic damage.

Limitations:

   •   Resource-intensive and costly.
   •   Can unintentionally harm non-target species or ecosystems.

Example:
The eradication of rats on South Georgia Island is a successful case. Introduced rats preyed on
native bird eggs and chicks, severely impacting seabird populations. Conservationists used
poisoned bait to eliminate the rats entirely, leading to the recovery of species such as the South
Georgia pipit and pintail duck.

Management Strategies

When eradication is impractical, management aims to control invasive species populations to
minimize their impact. This is often used for well-established invasions or large-scale areas.

Advantages:

   •   More feasible for large-scale invasions.
   •   Allows for adaptive approaches tailored to specific ecosystems.

Limitations:

   •   Requires ongoing effort and funding.
   •   May not fully restore ecosystems to their original state.

Example:
In Australia, the management of European rabbits (Oryctolagus cuniculus) is a prime example.
Rabbits caused extensive environmental degradation by overgrazing vegetation. Management
efforts included fencing, culling, and the introduction of diseases like myxomatosis and rabbit
hemorrhagic disease. While not eradicated, their population is controlled, reducing ecological
harm.
