    The ovipositor is a combination of different parts at the end of the abdomen in female
insects that lay their eggs inside objects. It helps them make a hole or slit in the object and

 •   Hypognathous (Hypo – Below: Gnathous – Jaw): Head perpendicular to longitudinal

 •   Prognathous : (Pro – infront: Gnathous – Jaw): Head extended longitudinally with

Nervous System of Insects:
   Insects have a bunch of ganglia (clusters of nerve cells) in their bodies. The biggest
ganglion, called the brain, is in their head. It’s connected to other ganglia through
commissures (bundles of nerve fibers). The brain has nerves that go to the eyes, antennae,
and other parts of the head. There’s also another ganglion, called the sub-esophageal
ganglion, below the esophagus.
   It connects to the brain and sends nerves to mouth parts and nearby head regions. The
ganglia in the thorax (body) can be separate or fused and they can have additional
connections from abdominal ganglia. All the ganglia send nerves to different body parts, like
wings, legs, and muscles. And there’s a small “sympathetic nerve system” that helps with the
nerve supply of the digestive and respiratory systems. So basically, insects have these ganglia
and nerves all over their bodies, allowing them to control and feel things.

Structure of Insects Nervous System:
   Insects have an intricate nervous system that helps them do all sorts of incredible things.
This system is composed of clusters of nerve cells called ganglia. The largest ganglion,
commonly referred to as the brain, is located in the insect's head. It is responsible for
coordinating various sensory and motor functions.

Connections & Role of Ganglia:
    The brain ganglion is connected to other ganglia throughout the insect's body by bundles
of nerve fibers known as commissures. These connections allow for communication and
coordination between different parts of the nervous system. The ganglia in the head send out
nerves to control the eyes, antennae, and other sensory structures.
    Another important ganglion, called the sub-esophageal ganglion, is situated beneath the
esophagus. It connects to the brain and controls the mouthparts and related regions.
The ganglia in the thorax, which is the middle part of the insect's body, can either be
separate or fused together. They send out nerves to control wing movement, leg coordination,
and muscle activity. In addition, these thoracic ganglia also connect to ganglia in the insect's
abdomen. This network of ganglia ensures that the different body parts function
harmoniously.

Sympathetic Nerve System in Insects:
    In addition to the main ganglia, insects also have a small sympathetic nerve system. This
system primarily focuses on providing nerve supply to the alimentary canal and tracheae,
working alongside the rest of the nervous system to ensure proper organ function.

Functions of Nervous System:
    The nervous system of insects allows them to respond to external stimuli, such as light,
sound, touch, and chemicals, enabling them to navigate their environment and find food. It
also plays a crucial role in their motor skills, allowing for precise and coordinated
movements.

   Metamorphosis is a biological process that involves a profound transformation in an
organism’s body structure as it progresses through different stages of its life cycle.

Types of Metamorphosis:
   There are three main types of metamorphosis: Ametabola, Hemimetabola, and
Holometabola.

    Ametabola refers to insects that undergo very little or no metamorphosis at all. They
hatch from eggs and look similar to the adult form throughout their life cycle. Examples of
Ametabola insects include silverfish and bristletails.
Hemimetabola or Paurometabola:

    Hemimetabola or Paurometabola, also known as incomplete metamorphosis, is when
the insect hatches from an egg and looks similar to the adult. It then goes through a series of
gradual changes, or molts, to reach maturity. Examples of Hemimetabola insects include
grasshoppers and dragonflies.
