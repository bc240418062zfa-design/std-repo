1. **How many compartments are there in mitochondria?**
  - Mitochondria have a double membrane structure consisting of an outer membrane and an inner
membrane. The inner membrane is folded into cristae, and the space between the two membranes is
called the intermembrane space. The innermost part is the matrix, which contains mitochondrial DNA
and enzymes. Therefore, there are four compartments: the outer membrane, intermembrane space, inner
membrane, and matrix.
2. **Define operon.**
  - An operon is a cluster of genes with a single promoter. It consists of a promoter, an operator, and two
or more structural genes. The operator lies between the promoter and the structural genes and binds
regulatory proteins that either activate or repress transcription.
3. **Phases of the cell cycle.**
  - The cell cycle consists of G1 (gap 1), S (synthesis), G2 (gap 2), and M (mitosis) phases. G1 phase
involves cell growth and preparation for DNA replication. The S phase is where DNA replication occurs.
G2 phase involves further growth and preparation for cell division. M phase is where mitosis takes place,
leading to the separation of duplicated chromosomes into two daughter cells. After M phase, the cycle
restarts with G1.
4. **What are phagocytes?**
  - Phagocytes are white blood cells that ingest and digest invading microorganisms. There are three
classes of professional phagocytes in mammals: macrophages, neutrophils, and dendritic cells. These cells
develop from hemopoietic stem cells and defend against infection. Macrophages also scavenge senescent
cells and cells that have died by apoptosis.
5. **Types of antibodies.**
 - There are five classes of antibodies: IgA, IgD, IgE, IgG, and IgM.
   - IgG: Most abundant, provides long-term protection, and can cross the placenta.
   - IgA: Protects mucous membranes and is found in secretions like tears and saliva.
   - IgM: First antibody produced in response to an infection, forms antigen-antibody complexes.
   - IgE: Involved in allergic reactions and defense against parasites.
   - IgD: Functions mainly as a receptor on B cells.
6. **Principle of vesicular transport.**
  - Vesicular transport involves the movement of proteins between different compartments via vesicles.
The process includes vesicle formation, loading cargo from one compartment's lumen, pinching off, and
fusing with another compartment to discharge the cargo. An example is the movement of soluble proteins
from the ER to the Golgi apparatus.

7. **What are cargo proteins?**
 - Cargo proteins are proteins transported within cells via vesicular transport mechanisms. They are
destined for specific locations such as organelles, the cell membrane, or for secretion outside the cell.
Examples include enzymes, receptors, hormones, and other functional proteins.
8. **Signaling in E. coli.**
  - Signaling in E. coli occurs through the lac operon, involving the binding of RNA polymerase to the
promoter region to initiate transcription. The operator region binds a repressor protein, which can
prevent transcription. The regulation of the lac operon depends on the presence or absence of lactose or
its metabolites.
9. **Features of the adaptive immune system.**
  - The adaptive immune system (AIS) is activated in peripheral lymphoid organs and can distinguish
between self and non-self, providing long-term protection against diseases. It involves antigens and can
even distinguish between optical isomers. If activated by self, it can be harmful.
10. **Cloning in sheep through nuclear transfer.**
  - Cloning involves isolating a somatic cell from the donor sheep, obtaining an enucleated egg cell from
another sheep, fusing the somatic cell with the enucleated egg cell, stimulating the fused cell to divide,
culturing the embryo, and transferring it to a surrogate sheep to carry to term.
11. **Replication models (semi-conservative, conservative, and dispersed).**
   - Semi-conservative replication: Each new double-stranded DNA molecule consists of one original
strand and one newly synthesized strand.
  - Conservative replication: One double-stranded DNA molecule remains completely original, and the
other is completely newly synthesized.
  - Dispersive replication: Each strand of a DNA molecule is a mix of original and newly synthesized
segments.

12. **Define epigenetics.**
 - Epigenetics is the study of heritable changes in gene expression that do not involve changes in the
DNA sequence.

13. **Functions of TH1 and TH2.**
  - TH1 cells mainly help activate macrophages and cytotoxic T cells, while TH2 cells mainly help
activate B cells.

14. **Two signaling functions of the N-terminal ER signal sequence of a soluble protein.**

  - The N-terminal ER signal sequence directs the protein to the ER membrane and serves as a start-
transfer signal that opens the pore.

15. **Types of MHC.**
  - MHC class I molecules present antigens derived from intracellular pathogens to cytotoxic T cells,
found on the surface of most nucleated cells.
  - MHC class II molecules present antigens derived from extracellular pathogens to helper T cells,
primarily expressed on antigen-presenting cells.

   PCR and its applications.

   •   PCR (Polymerase Chain Reaction) is a technique used to amplify a specific DNA sequence.
       Applications include:
         1. DNA amplification: Rapidly replicates specific DNA sequences for analysis or use.
         2. Gene expression analysis: Measures mRNA to determine gene expression levels.
         3. DNA sequencing: Generates enough DNA for sequencing.
         4. Disease diagnosis: Detects pathogens in samples.
         5. Genetic testing: Identifies genetic mutations, genotypes, or markers.
         6. Forensic analysis: Amplifies and analyzes DNA from crime scene samples.
         7. Environmental monitoring: Detects microorganisms in environmental samples.
         8. Food safety testing: Detects foodborne pathogens.
