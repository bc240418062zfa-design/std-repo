Question 1
What is the type of case studies
Answer
Single Instrumental Case Study: The researcher focuses on a specific issue and studies one example to explain it.
Collective/Multiple Case Study: The researcher focuses on the same issue but looks at multiple examples to explain it better.
Intrinsic Case Study: The researcher focuses on one unique or unusual case because it’s interesting on its own.

 Question 5
Difference between cross sectional study and longitudinal study
Answer
    1. Cross-Sectional Study:
Data is collected at one point in time.
It shows how things are at that moment (like a snapshot).
Example: Surveying people’s health in 2025.
Good for: Finding out how common something is, or comparing different groups.
Limitations: Can’t show changes over time or cause-and-effect.
    2. Longitudinal Study:
Data is collected over a long period.
It tracks how things change over time.
Example: Following people’s health from childhood to adulthood.
Good for: Understanding changes and causes over time.
Limitations: Takes time and is more expensive.
Question 6
 difference between index and scale
Answer
    1. Index:
Combines different things to make one score.
Example: Adding income, education, and job to make a score for social status.

    2. Scale:
Measures how much of something there is.
Example: A scale to rate how much you agree with a statement (1 to 5).
Main Difference:
Index = One score from many things.
Scale = Measures the amount of one thing.

Question 9
What are participants observation?
Answer
In participant observation, researchers can be:
    1. Complete participants – They join the group and act like regular members, without telling anyone they are studying them.
    2. Covert participants – They secretly observe the group without revealing they are a researcher.
    3. Overt participants – They openly tell the group they are a researcher while still taking part in the activities.
Question 10
Quantitative research
Answer
Quantitative research is a way of studying things by collecting numbers and data that can be measured. It helps researchers find patterns or trends using math and statistics.
 For example, surveys or experiments are often used to collect this data. The aim is to understand things in a clear, number-based way that can help make predictions or draw conclusions.
Question 11
Exploratory and co-relational hypothesis
Answer
Exploratory Hypothesis: Used when exploring a new topic to find patterns or ideas, without predicting specific outcomes.
Correlational Hypothesis: Suggests a connection between two things (like more study time and better grades) but doesn’t say one causes the other.
Question 12
Semi-structured and unstructured interview
Answer
Semi-structured Interview: Has some planned questions but allows flexibility to ask more based on answers.
Unstructured Interview: No fixed questions, just a free-flowing conversation.
Question 13
Ethical  research
Answer
Ethical research means doing studies in a fair and honest way. Key ideas are:
    1. Informed Consent: People know what the study is about and agree to join.
    2. Confidentiality: Personal details stay private.
    3. Minimizing Harm: Researchers try not to cause any harm or discomfort.
    4. Honesty: Results are reported truthfully.
    5. Fairness: Everyone is treated equally.
    6. Respect: People can leave the study anytime if they want.
Question 14
Difference between qualitative and quantitative research
Answer
    1. Data Type:
Qualitative: Uses words or descriptions.
Quantitative: Uses numbers and stats.
    2. Goal:
Qualitative: Understands feelings, experiences, and ideas.
Quantitative: Measures and counts things to find patterns.
    3. Methods:
Qualitative: Interviews, observations, and open-ended questions.
Quantitative: Surveys, tests, and structured questions.
    4. Analysis:
Qualitative: Looks for themes or meanings.
Quantitative: Uses math and statistics to analyze data.
    5. Outcome:
Qualitative: Provides in-depth understanding.
Quantitative: Gives clear, measurable results.
Question 15
FGD limitations
Here are the limitations of FGDs in simple terms:
    1. Dominant Voices: Some people may take over the discussion.
    2. Small Group: It might not represent everyone.
    3. Bias: The facilitator or group may influence responses.
    4. Time-Consuming: It takes a lot of time to organize and analyze.
    5. Shallow Answers: Doesn’t provide deep individual insights.
Question 16
Sequential sampling
Answer
Sequential sampling is when you collect data one step at a time and make decisions as you go. You don’t wait until you have all the data; instead, you stop or continue based on what you find along the way. It helps save time and effort. This method is used in things like checking product quality or testing new ideas.
Question 17
Steps for conducting an experiment
Answer
Here are the simple steps for conducting an experiment:

    1. Ask a question: What do you want to learn?
    2. Research: Find out what’s already known about the topic.
    3. Make a guess: Predict what will happen (hypothesis).
    4. Plan: Decide how to test your guess.
