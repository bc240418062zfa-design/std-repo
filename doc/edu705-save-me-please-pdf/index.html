           Glossary
                         A special Thanks to “Minha”
   Correlation
Interdependence between factors within a system.
   Deductive reasoning
An approach to research where the researcher predicts a relationship between the
independent and dependent variables, stating it as a hypothesis. The hypothesis is
then tested to see if it is true or false. Comes under the logic of reasoning.
   Dependent Variable
The outcome variable. In experimental research, this variable is expected to depend
on a predictor (or independent) variable.
   Research Question
A clear statement in the form of a question of the specific issue that a researcher
wishes to analyze.
   Sampling

The process of selecting a subgroup of a population that will be used to represent the
entire population.
   Semi-Structured Interview
A method of data collection in which the interviewer uses a pre-determined list of
topics or questions to gather information from a respondent. The interviewer,
however, may stray from the list to follow-up on things the respondent says during
the interview.

               Notes for Mids
      Purpose of the Thesis: it serves as a formal document where you present
      original research or scholarly work on a specific topic. It demonstrates your
      understanding, critical thinking, and ability to contribute new knowledge
      Writing Style and Audience:
         - Writing style refers to how you express ideas, use language, and
            structure sentences.
         - Consider your audience (readers) when choosing a style. Academic
            writing is typically formal, precise, and objective.
      Order and Component of Thesis
         - Title page: Includes title, author, institution, and date.
         - Abstract: Brief summary of the thesis.
         - Table of Contents: Lists chapters and sections.
         - Introduction: Sets context and outlines research question.
         - Literature Review: Surveys existing research.
         - Methodology: Describes research methods.
         - Results: Presents findings.
         - Discussion: Analyzes results.
         - Conclusion: Summarizes key points.
         - References: Lists cited sources.
         - Appendices: Additional material (e.g., data, charts).
         - Acknowledgments: Thanks to supporters.
         - Statement of Originality: Affirms original work.

Footnotes and Endnotes: footnotes appear at the bottom of pages, while
endnotes are at the end of chapters or the entire document. They provide
additional information or references.
Abstract: it summarizes your thesis briefly. It helps readers decide if they
want to read the full document.
Key Linguistic Feature of an Abstract: Tense: Abstracts often use the
present tense to discuss general truths or current relevance
Questions Related to Abstract Writing
   - "How long should the abstract be?"
   - "What should I include?"
   - "How do I make it engaging?"
Introduction Chapter in a Thesis: it sets the stage for your research. It
outlines the problem, research question, and significance.
Functions of the Introduction Chapter
   - Engagement: It captures the reader's attention and sets the tone for the
       entire work.
   - Context: It provides background information related to the topic.
   - Thesis Statement: It clearly defines the research problem or question.
   - Significance: It explains why the study is relevant and valuable.
   - Structure: It outlines the organization of the thesis or research paper.
Introduction: Establishing a Research Territory: In this section, you
demonstrate knowledge of the topic and its relevance to the field. You explain
where your study fits within existing literature and establish the context for
your research.
Indicating a Gap/ Niche: You highlight a gap or limitation in previous
research. By identifying this gap, you emphasize the need for further
investigation in your specific area.
Occupy the Niche/ Gap: This step involves presenting new information or
results that address the identified gap. You contribute to the field by filling
the gap with your research findings.
Statement of the Problem and the Context: The statement of the problem
defines the research issue or question. The context provides background
information related to the problem.

Aims of the Study: The aims outline the specific goals or objectives of your
research. They guide your investigation and provide direction.
Clarification of Key Constructs: You define essential terms, concepts, or
variables used in your study. This ensures clarity and understanding for the
reader.
Organization of the Thesis: You briefly describe how the thesis or research
paper is structured. This helps readers navigate through your work.
Key Linguistic Features in Establishing Significance of the Study: These
include language cues that emphasize the importance of your research. For
example, phrases like "critical gap" or "novel contribution" signal
significance.
Key Linguistic Features in Indicating the Gap: These features highlight
limitations or areas lacking sufficient research. Contrastive phrases (e.g.,
"however," "despite") often signal this.
Key Linguistic Features in Occupying the Gap in the Introduction: Here,
you present specific results or findings that address the gap. Use clear
language without interpretation or evaluation.
Active vs. Passive Voice Use
   - Active voice emphasizes the doer of the action (e.g., "We conducted
       the study").
   - Passive voice focuses on the action itself (e.g., "The study was
       conducted").
Key Linguistic Features: Adjectives and Pronouns
   - Adjectives add precision (e.g., "significant," "promising").
   - Pronouns (e.g., "we," "our") personalize the research.
Language Use in Making Comparisons: Comparative language (e.g.,
"compared to," "in contrast") highlights differences. Use it to discuss existing
research or your own findings
Introduction: Questions Answered: The introduction should address the
"what" and "why" of your research. It sets the stage for the reader to
understand your study
