Features or Characteristics of Class Chondrichthyes:

   Chondrichthyes is a class of fishes included in the division Gnathostomata, as they
possess jaws. The key characteristics of this class are:

   •   Tail fins with a large upper lobe.
   •   No swim bladder or lungs; buoyancy is controlled by large livers that excrete oil.
   •   Cartilage skeleton instead of bones.
   •   Paired appendages.
   •   No operculum (gill cover).
   •   Spiracle: A modified slit behind the eye that assists sharks in drawing water during
       respiration, playing a major role in bottom-dwelling sharks.
   •   Whale sharks are the second-largest vertebrates and the largest fish, with some
       measuring up to 15 meters in length.
   •   Mostly marine, carnivorous, and scavengers.
   •   Predatory fishes, feeding on other fishes, crustaceans, and mollusks.
   •   Some species filter food particles like plankton from the water current passing
       through the mouth, pharynx, and gills.

   •   Respiration through 5–7 pairs of gills. Some species must keep moving to breathe,
       while others can pump water over their gills.
   •   Gaseous exchange occurs through the water current passing over the gills.
   •   Biting mouthparts.
   •   Lateral line system: Detects motion and vibrations in water.
   •   Ampullae of Lorenzini: Electroreceptor organs that detect the electromagnetic
       fields produced by living organisms.
   •   Teeth are modified placoid scales, not attached to jawbones but embedded in tissue.
   •   Old teeth fall out and are continuously replaced by new ones forming behind them.

Examples: Sharks, ratfishes, rays, and skates.

Telencephalon and Diencephalon:

    In fish brains, the brain is generally divided into three regions: Prosencephalon,
Mesencephalon, and Rhombencephalon. The prosencephalon gives rise to two important
parts:

   1. Telencephalon
         o The telencephalon is the anterior part of the brain, primarily responsible for
            the reception and conduction of smell.
         o It is divided into two regions: Area ventralis and Area dorsalis.
         o The ventral area receives secondary olfactory fibers and is a constant
            structure in all bony fishes.
         o In adult fishes, the telencephalon may form through invagination and
            inversion, and plays a crucial role in reproductive behavior.
   2. Diencephalon
         o The diencephalon is primarily concerned with vision and is divided into three
            parts:
            (i) Epithalamus
            (ii) Hypothalamus
            (iii) Thalamus

Subclass Actinopterygii:
    Ray-finned fish. Their fins lack muscular lobes that is why called as ray-finned fishes.
They usually possess swim bladders, gas filled sacs along dorsal wall of the cavity that
regulate buoyancy. Paired fins supported by dermal rays. Base of fins is not muscular. Tail
fins almost equal to upper and lower lobes. Blind olfactory sacs.

Examples: Ray fined fishes

Subclass Sarcoptergyii:
    Lobe-finned fish. Have muscular lobes associated with their fins and usually use lungs for
gas exchange. Pneumatic sacs function as lungs or swim bladder. Paired fins with muscular
lobes. Some bony fishes are hermaphrodites and a number of species exhibit parthenogenesis.

Fertilization is usually external. Development is usually oviparous (egg laying) but can be
ovoviviparous or viviparous.

Examples: Lungfishes, coelacanths

Number of Chambers in a Fish’s Heart

   Fish have a two-chambered heart, consisting of:

   •   One atrium – a thin-walled chamber that receives deoxygenated blood from the
       body before passing it to the ventricle.
   •   One ventricle – a muscular chamber that pumps blood to the gills for oxygenation
       before it circulates through the body.

    This simple structure supports a single circulatory system, ensuring continuous blood
flow from the heart to the gills and then to the rest of the body.

Adaptations of Predatory Fishes:

   •   Fish that eat large animals as food are called predatory fish.
   •   They have acquired some general adaptations to take food.
   •   They have well-developed grasping and holding teeth, such as many sharks
       (Elasmobranchii), Barracuda (Sphyraena), Pike (Esox), and Gers (Lepisosteus).
   •   Their intestines tend to be smaller in size than herbivores.
   •   Many predatory fish such as bluegill (Pomatomus saltarix) and many deep sea fish
       actively hunt their prey.
   •   Other fish, such as the grouper (Epinephelus), often sit in wait till an animal passes
       and then dart out to grasp it.
   •   Angler fishes (Lophidae and Antennariidae) have developed an anterior ray of the
       first dorsal fin into a lure to attract their prey.
   •   The Southeast Asian archer fish (Toxotes jaculata) preys on insects by spitting on
       nearby trees.
   •   This fish has a well-developed aerial viewing system.
   •   Some predatory fishes hunt by sight.
   •   Other fish, especially many sharks (Squaliformes), nocturnal fish such as bullheads
       (Ictalurus) and Morays (Muraenidae), catch the prey by ensuring the position of the
       prey with the help of smell, taste, touch and lateral line organs.

Example: Barracuda (Sphyraena)

differences.
1. Morphometric characters: used for measureable structures

2. Meristic characters: used for any countable structures

3. Molecular characters: nuclear DNA and Mitochondrial DNA

4. Anatomical character: skeleton and characters of the soft anatomy
