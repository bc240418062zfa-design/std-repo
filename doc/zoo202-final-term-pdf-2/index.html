        Lesson 44
        Resptiles:
evolutionary Perspective

•   Class reptilia possess amniotic egg.

•   Amniotic eggs have extra embryonic membranes that protect the embryo from
    desiccation, cushion the embryo, promote gas transfer, and store waste
    materials

•   The amniotic eggs of reptiles and birds also have hard or leathery shells that
    protect the developing embryo, albumen that cushions and provides moisture
    and nutrients for the embryo, and yolk that supplies food to the embryo. All of
    these features are adaptations for development on land.

•   In amniotic egg, the embryo develops at the surface of a mass of yolk.

•   Amniotic egg consists of three layers, The amnion encloses the embryo in a
    fluid-filled sac and protects against shock and desiccation. The chorion is nearer
    the shell and becomes highly vascular and aids in gas exchange. The allantois is
    a ventral outgrowth of the gut and stores nitrogenous wastes (e.g., uric acid).
•   Even though the amniotic egg has played an important role in vertebrates’
    successful invasion of terrestrial habitats, it is one of many reptilian adaptations
    that have allowed members of this class to flourish on land
•   Living representatives of the class Reptilia include the turtles, lizards, snakes,
    worm lizards, crocodilians, and the tuatara

•   There are three classes of reptiles according to opening in temporal region

•   Reptiles in the subclass Anapsida lack openings or fenestrae region of the skull.
    The turtles represent this lineage today.

•   A second group of reptiles are diapsid. They have upper and lower openings in the
    temporal region of the skull.

•   Third group of reptiles are synapsid. They possess a single dorsal opening in the
    temporal region of the skull.

Cladistic interpretation of the amniotic lineage

•   Cladistic analysis shows that the birds (traditionally the class Aves) and the
    mammals (traditionally the class Mammalia) share a common ancestor with the
    reptiles.

     Lesson 45
Survey of the reptiles

General characteristics of reptiles
•   They have dry skin with epidermal scales
•   Skull with one point of articulation with the vertebral column (occipital condyle);
    respiration via
•   Lungs are present in reptiles
•   Osmoregulation occurs through metanephric kidneys
•   internal fertilization
•   Amniotic eggs
•   They have four orders named as; 1. Testudines, 2. Rhynchocephalia 3. Squamata, 4.
    Crocodilia

1. Order testudines (Chelonia)

•   Members of the order Testudines or Chelonia are the turtles
•   The approximately 225 species of turtles are characterized by a bony shell, limbs
    articulating internally to the ribs, and a keratinized beak rather than teeth.
•   The dorsal portion of the shell is the carapace, which forms from a fusion of
    vertebrae, expanded ribs, and bones in the dermis of the skin. Keratin covers the
    bone of the carapace.
•   The ventral portion of the shell is the plastron. It forms from bones of the pectoral
    girdle and dermal bone, and keratin also covers it

•   In some turtles, such as the North American box turtle (Terrapene), the shell has
    flexible areas, or hinges, that allow the anterior and posterior edges of the plastron to
    be raised.

•   Turtles have eight cervical vertebrae that can be articulated into an S-shaped
    configuration, which allows the head to be drawn into the shell.

•   Turtles have long life spans. Most reach sexual maturity after seven or eight years,
    and live 14 or more years.

•   All turtles are oviparous

•   Females use their hindlimbs to excavate nests in the soil. There they lay and cover
    with soil clutches of 5 to 100 eggs

•   Development takes from four weeks to one year, and the parent does not attend to
    the eggs during this time

•   The young are independent of the parent at hatching.

2.         Order rhynchocephalia
•    The one surviving species of the order is the tuatara (Sphenodon punctatus)

•    Tooth attachment and structure distinguish the tuatara from other reptiles.

•    It is oviparous and shares under- ground burrows with ground-nesting seabirds

•    Tuataras venture out of their burrows at dusk and dawn to feed on insects or,
     occasionally, small vertebrates.

  Lesson 46
Order squamata

•   Order squamata divided into three suborders.

1.Suborder Sauria—The Lizards
•   About 3,300 species of lizards are in the suborder Sauria

•   Lizards usually have two pairs of legs, and their upper and lower jaws unite
    anteriorly

•   Lizards vary in length from only a few centimeters to as large as 3 m. Many lizards
    live on surface substrates and retreat under rocks or logs when necessary

•   Others are burrowers or tree dwellers.

•    Most lizards are oviparous; some are ovoviviparous or viviparous
•    They usually deposit eggs under rocks or debris or in burrows. Geckos, commonly
    found on the walls of human dwellings in semitropical areas, are short and stout
•   Iguanas have robust bodies, short necks, and distinct heads. This group includes the
    marine iguanas of the Galápagos Islands and the flying dragons (Draco) of Southeast
    Asia
•   Chameleons are adapted to arboreal lifestyles and use a long, sticky tongue to
    capture insects
•    The only venomous lizards are the gila monster (Heloderma suspectum) and the
    Mexican beaded lizard

2. Suborder Serpentes—The Snakes
•    About 2,300 species are in the suborder Serpentes
•    Three hundred species are venomous
•    Snakes are elongate and lack limbs
•    The skeleton may contain more than two hundred vertebrae and pairs of ribs
•    Joints between vertebrae make the body very flexible
•    Snakes possess skull adaptations that facilitate swallowing large prey
•    Other differences between lizards and snakes include the mechanism for focusing
    the eyes and the morphology of the retina.
