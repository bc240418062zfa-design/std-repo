Types of active immunization vaccines:
Active immunization is the induction of immunity after exposure to an antigen, requiring
antigenic exposure. It occurs naturally or artificially, leading to antibody development and
memory response. The immune system generates antibodies slowly, providing long-term
immunity without pre-made antibodies in the host's serum.
Types: or Examples: Active immunization vaccines include live attenuated vaccines (e.g., MMR,
varicella), inactivated vaccines (e.g., polio, hepatitis A), subunit vaccines (e.g., hepatitis B,
HPV), conjugate vaccines (e.g., Hib), toxoid vaccines (e.g., tetanus, diphtheria), and mRNA
vaccines (e.g., COVID-19).

What is Opsonization?
Opsonization is the enhancement of the phagocytic process where pathogens are marked for
ingestion by phagocytes. Opsonins, such as immunoglobulins (IgG, IgM) and complement
proteins, bind to the bacterial surface, facilitating neutrophil or macrophage phagocytosis
through opsonin receptors like Fc and complement receptors.

The structure of TCR
The T-cell receptor (TCR) is a heterodimer surface receptor composed of two equal-length
chains (α and β). Each chain has a constant (C) and variable (V) region, with hypervariable
regions for specificity. TCRs have a short cytoplasmic tail, transmembrane region, and disulfide
bridge.

Characteristics of immunoglobulin:
Immunoglobulins (Ig) are antibodies produced by plasma cells, composed of two heavy and two
light chains forming a Y-shaped molecule. They have variable regions for antigen binding, and
constant regions for effector functions. Classes include IgG, IgA, IgM, IgE, and IgD, each with
specific roles and functions.

Parts of secondary lymphoid system?
 The secondary lymphoid system includes structures where lymphocytes become activated and
initiate adaptive immune responses. Key parts of this system are:
1. Lymph Nodes: A=Filter lymph fluid, b=Sites for antigen presentation and lymphocyte
activation.
2. Spleen: A= Filters blood, B= Red pulp: Removes old red blood cells, c= White pulp: Immune
responses to blood-borne antigens.
3. Tonsils: A= Located at the throat and nasal passages. B=First line of defense against inhaled
or ingested pathogens.
4. Appendix: A=Contains lymphoid tissue. B= Plays a role in gut immunity.

Complement System:
The complement system is a group of proteins that enhance the immune response. These proteins
can directly attack pathogens, promote phagocytosis, and contribute to the inflammatory
response. Among complement proteins, some proteins have the ability to bind with
immunoglobulin molecules while some are involved in binding on the membrane component of
various immune cells.

Endogenous & exogenous antigen difference?
Endogenous and exogenous antigens are two main types of antigens that the immune system
recognizes and responds to:
Endogenous Antigens:
    Origin: Generated within the body's cells.
    Produced within the cell, presented by MHC Class I, and targeted by CD8+ T cells.
    Examples: Viral proteins produced inside infected cells, tumor antigens from cancerous
     cells, and normal cellular proteins presented during routine maintenance.
Exogenous Antigens:
    Origin: Enter the body from the external environment.
    presented by MHC Class II, and targeted by CD4+ T cells.
    Examples: Bacteria, fungi, parasites, pollen, and toxins.

Activation of B-cell receptor?
B-cells are activated when there is high concentration of antigen for example in case of bacteria
the lipopolysacchrides antigens exist in greater quantity. B-cell activation occur after binding of
antigen to a surface molecule which is known as B-cell receptor. Afterward there is bypassing of
the early part of the biochemical pathway mediated by specific antigen receptor. Cross linking of
immunoglobulin receptor occur due to repeating determinants on antigenic structure. As a result
the transformation of B-cells occur into plasma cell for immunoglobulin secretion.

Active / passive immunization?
1= Active immunization: It is the induction of immunity after exposure of host to antigen. In this
case, antigenic exposure is mandatory. Active immunization can occur naturally by exposing to
microbe or other antigen when there is no prior exposure before the entry of antigen. In this form
of immunization, immune system develops antibodies against the microbe after its exposure.
This process of antibodies generation is considered slow.
2= Passive immunization: It is the induction of active humoral immunity. In this kind of
immunization, antibodies are mandatory. Passive immunization can occur naturally by transfer
of maternal antibodies to fetus through placenta. However, the artificial induction can be done by
injecting gamma globulins from other individuals or animals to host.

What are cytokines?
• Diverse group of non-antibody proteins • Act as mediators between cells • Initially these were
considered as products of immune cells • Also considered to act as mediator for immune cells •
Also produced from non-immune cells & mediators for them. • Used as biological response

modifier for treatment of various diseases • Cytokine is a general term but more specific term
used according to their cell of origin.

What are monoclonal antibodies?
The antibodies which are produced from a single antibody producing B-cell are called as
monoclonal antibodies. These antibodies also able to bind with single and unique antigen binding
sit (epitope). Monoclonal antibodies mainly consist of single subtype of IgG for example IgG1,
IgG2 & IgG3.
