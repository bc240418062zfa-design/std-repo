                       EDU705
Thesis Purpose

The overarching aim of a thesis, which is to demonstrate the student's ability to contribute to
research, solve theoretical issues, and apply appropriate methods.

Thesis Components

The sections or parts of a thesis, including title page, abstract, introduction, literature review,
methodology, results, discussion, conclusion, and appendices.

Writing Style

The tone, structure, and formatting choices made to ensure clarity, coherence, and appropriateness
for the academic audience.

Primary Audience

The immediate readers of the thesis, such as the academic community, supervisors, or examiners.

Secondary Audience

People outside the primary audience, such as policymakers or general readers interested in the
findings.

Title Page

The first page of a thesis that includes the title, author’s name, and institution.

Abstract

A concise summary of the thesis, presenting its aims, methodology, findings, and significance.

Table of Contents

An outline listing chapters, sections, and their corresponding page numbers.

Statement of Originality

A declaration that the work presented is the author’s original work and has not been plagiarized.

Acknowledgements

A section where the author expresses gratitude to individuals or institutions that contributed to the
research.

Footnotes

Notes placed at the bottom of a page, providing additional information or citations related to the
main text.

Endnotes

Similar to footnotes but located at the end of a chapter or thesis, often used to reduce page clutter.

Introduction Chapter

The opening section of a thesis that introduces the research problem, significance, and the overall
structure of the thesis.

Literature Review

A comprehensive analysis of existing research, identifying gaps and establishing the context for
the current study.

Research Gap

A specific area or question in a field that has not been sufficiently explored and forms the basis of
the study.

Niche

The unique position or contribution of the research within the broader field of study.

Signposting

Writing techniques that guide the reader through the structure and flow of the thesis.

Dedication

An optional section where the author honors someone by dedicating the thesis to them.

Hierarchy in Thesis

The structure or levels of headings, sections, and subsections within the thesis.

Appendices

Supplementary materials that support the thesis, such as data tables, transcripts, or ethical
approvals.

Research Territory

The broader context or field of study that the research addresses, highlighting its relevance and
scope.

Aims of Study

Clear, concise statements about what the research intends to achieve.

Key Constructs

The main concepts or variables used in the research, which are often socially or theoretically
defined.

Background Research

Previous studies and theories that provide context for the current research.

Problem Statement

A precise articulation of the research issue that needs to be addressed.

Gap Identification

Highlighting areas in existing literature where knowledge is lacking.

Occupying a Gap

Explaining how the research addresses the identified gap through its aims, methodology, and
contribution.

Linguistic Features

The language choices made in academic writing, such as tense, voice, and style, to convey meaning
effectively.

Present Perfect Tense

Used to discuss research conducted in the past but relevant to the present study.

Simple Present Tense

Used for established facts, generalizations, or current state of knowledge.

Simple Past Tense

Used to describe specific completed actions or past methodologies.

Paraphrasing

Rewriting ideas from a source in your own words while preserving the original meaning and
properly citing it.

Coherence

Ensuring ideas are logically connected and flow smoothly throughout the thesis.

Synthesizing

Integrating multiple sources and perspectives to create a cohesive argument or summary.

Reporting Verbs

Verbs used to discuss or critique others' work, such as "argued," "claimed," or "proposed," which
reflect the author’s stance.

Plagiarism

The unethical act of presenting someone else's work or ideas as one’s own without proper
acknowledgment.

Methodology

The section describing the research design, methods, and rationale behind choosing those methods.

Ethical Concerns

Considerations related to privacy, consent, and fair treatment of research participants.

Validity

The degree to which a research method measures what it claims to measure.

Reliability

The consistency and accuracy of a research method or instrument over time.

Data Collection

The process of gathering information using tools such as surveys, interviews, or observations.

Data Analysis

The methods used to interpret and make sense of the collected data.

Sampling

Selecting participants or data points for research based on specific criteria.

Triangulation

Using multiple methods, data sources, or theories to increase the reliability and validity of the
findings.

Research Paradigm

The underlying framework or philosophy guiding the research, such as qualitative, quantitative, or
mixed-method approaches.

Qualitative Data

Non-numerical data, such as interviews or observations, focusing on themes and meanings.

Quantitative Data

Numerical data, such as statistics or survey responses, focusing on measurable variables.

Findings

The results or outcomes of the research, presented as evidence supporting the research question.

Discussion Chapter

A section that interprets findings, relates them to existing research, and discusses their
implications.

Conclusion Chapter

The final section that summarizes findings, highlights contributions, and provides
recommendations or future research directions.
