   Mechanism of Enzyme Action
   Enzymes bind and chemically
   transform other molecules— they
   catalyze reactions.
   • The molecules acted upon by
     enzymes are called reaction
     substrates rather than ligands.

Enzymes
   • Enzymes are highly effective
     catalysts, commonly enhancing
     reaction rates by a factor of 105
     to 1017.
   • The distinguishing feature of an
     enzyme-catalyzed reaction is that
     it takes place within the confines
     of a pocket on the enzyme called
     the active site.

Enzymes
   • The surface of the active site is
     lined with amino acid residues
     with side chains that
     complement and bind the
     substrate and catalyze its
     chemical transformation.

Enzymes
   • Often, the active site encloses a
     substrate, sequestering it
     completely from solution.
   • The enzyme substrate complex,
     whose existence is central to the
     action of enzymes.

Enzymes

Enzymes
   • Enzyme-catalyzed reactions
     have three basic steps:
   • binding of substrate: E+S↔ES
   • conversion of bound substrate
     to bound product: ES ↔ EP
   • release of product : EP ↔ E+P

Reaction coordinate diagram for a chemical reaction.
The free energy of the system is plotted against the progress of the reaction
S→P. A diagram of this kind is a description of the energy
changes during the reaction, and the horizontal axis (reaction coordinate)
reflects the progressive chemical changes (e.g., bond breakage
or formation) as S is converted to P. The activation energies, G‡, for
the S→P and P→S reactions are indicated. G is the overall standard
free-energy change in the direction S→P.

   • When a reacting system is not at
     equilibrium, the tendency to
     move toward equilibrium
     represents a driving force
   • the magnitude of which can be
     expressed as the free-energy

   • Under standard conditions (298 K

   • for gases, at partial pressures of

   Mechanism of Enzyme Action
   (Contd.)

   Ground State
         &
   Transition State

Enzymes
   • The starting point for either the
     forward or the reverse reaction is
     called the ground state
   • The equilibrium between S and P
     reflects the difference in the free
     energies of their ground states.

Enzymes
   • The free energy of the ground
     state of P is lower than that of S

   • So G′⁰ for the reaction is negative
     and the equilibrium favors P.

   • The position and direction of
     equilibrium are not affected by
     any catalyst.

Enzymes

Enzymes
   • But there is an energy barrier
     between S and P: The energy
     required for
   • alignment of reacting groups
   • formation of transient unstable
     charges
   • bond rearrangements
   • and other transformations

Enzymes
   • This is illustrated by the energy
     “hill”
   • To undergo reaction, the
     molecules must overcome this
     barrier and therefore must be
     raised to a higher energy level
   • This is called the transition state.

Enzymes
   • It is simply a fleeting molecular
     moment in which events such as
   • bond breakage,
   • bond formation, and
   • charge development
   • have proceeded to the precise
     point at which decay to either
     substrate or product is equally
     likely.

Enzymes
   • The difference between the
     energy levels of the ground state
     and the transition state is the
     activation energy, G‡.

Enzymes
   • At the top of the energy hill is a
     point at which decay to the S or P
     state is equally probable (it is
     downhill either way)

      Enzymes
         • A substance that modifies the
           transition state to lower the
           activation energy is termed a
           catalyst; a biological catalyst is
           termed an enzyme.

END
