Real-World Case Study: Polydactyly in a Multigenerational Family (Lange et al.,
2014)

In a clinical genetics investigation of postaxial polydactyly, Lange et al. (2014) described a
multigenerational German family where several individuals carried a mutation in the GLI3 gene.
Despite inheriting the same mutation, family members displayed variable phenotypes: some had
a fully developed extra digit, others showed only a small, nonfunctional appendage, and a few
were completely normal in appearance. Genetic testing confirmed the presence of the mutation in
both symptomatic and asymptomatic individuals, demonstrating incomplete penetrance and
variable expressivity.

Question 1 (a). What does this case tell us about the penetrance of the
polydactyly gene?

The GLI3-related case reveals that polydactyly caused by a dominant mutation does not always
manifest, even in carriers. This phenomenon is known as incomplete penetrance, where some
individuals with a disease-causing allele do not show the trait at all. In the Lange et al. (2014)
family, several individuals with the mutant allele had a normal digit count, suggesting that
external modifiers or random developmental factors suppressed the trait's expression. This aligns
with the broader understanding that even dominant alleles may require specific cellular
environments or additional regulatory signals to fully express themselves (Cooper & Hausman,
2021). Hence, although the mutation is genetically dominant, its expression is probabilistic rather
than deterministic.

Question 1 (b). What does it suggest about the expressivity of the gene?

In the same GLI3 case, family members who did exhibit polydactyly did so to varying degrees:
one had a fully functional sixth digit, while another had only a skin outgrowth (Lange et al.,
2014). This illustrates variable expressivity—the range of phenotypic severity associated with a
single genotype. Unlike penetrance (which is about presence or absence), expressivity deals with
how strongly or in what form a gene is expressed. Modifier genes, differential enhancer
activity, and developmental timing differences can all influence how completely the GLI3

Question 1 (c). Give one possible reason why expressivity might vary among
individuals.

A major reason for variation in expressivity is the effect of modifier genes, which influence how
the primary mutation is expressed. For instance, the SHH (Sonic Hedgehog) signaling pathway
plays a crucial role in limb patterning. Individuals with the same GLI3 mutation may differ in
SHH enhancer strength or expression timing, leading to distinct outcomes (Lopez-Rios et al.,
2014). Other factors such as histone modifications or environmental exposures during limb bud
development may also change the intensity of gene expression, thereby influencing whether an
extra digit is partially formed, fully functional, or entirely absent.

Question 2. Describe one genetic condition (other than polydactyly) where
penetrance is incomplete and expressivity is variable.

A classic example is BRCA1 mutation–associated breast cancer. The BRCA1 gene, when
mutated, increases the risk of breast and ovarian cancer in women. However, not all women who
inherit a pathogenic variant develop cancer—penetrance is incomplete, estimated at 60–80 % by
age 70 (Antoniou et al., 2003). Among those who do develop cancer, the age of onset, type of
tumor, and progression rate vary widely, reflecting variable expressivity. Some women are
diagnosed in their 30s with aggressive triple-negative tumors, while others may not show disease
until postmenopause or never at all. Lifestyle factors like diet, childbirth history, and exposure to
hormones, as well as epigenetic changes, play a key role in modulating the phenotype (King et
al., 2003). This case demonstrates how the same deleterious mutation can lead to vastly different
clinical realities in different individuals.

Question 3. Explain how environmental factors can influence gene expressivity.

Gene expressivity is often shaped by environmental inputs, which interact with the genetic
background to modulate phenotypic outcomes. Some notable examples include:

   1. Temperature-sensitive mutations – The coat color in Siamese cats is a well-known
      case. A tyrosinase mutation results in melanin synthesis only in cooler parts of the body.
      The warmer core temperature denatures the enzyme, leading to paler fur, while the cooler
      extremities (ears, tail, paws) are darker (Lyons et al., 2005).
   2. Epigenetic regulation via diet – In mice, expression of the Agouti gene can be silenced
      by maternal intake of methyl-rich diets. Mice with the same genotype develop different
