Definition of Semantics and Pragmatics
   - Semantics: the study of meaning in language, focusing on the meaning
      of words, phrases, sentences, and texts.
   - Pragmatics: the study of how language is used in context to
      communicate effectively, considering factors like speaker intention,
      audience, and social norms.
The Borderline between Semantics and Pragmatics: The distinction
between semantics and pragmatics can be blurry, as meaning is often
dependent on context.
Sentence and Utterance
   - Sentence: a unit of language that expresses a complete thought or idea.
   - Utterance: a specific instance of a sentence being used in a particular
      context.
Language and Logic: Language and logic are closely related, as language is
used to express logical ideas and relationships.
The Explicit and the Implicit
   - Explicit meaning: the literal or obvious meaning of a sentence or text.
   - Implicit meaning: the implied or suggested meaning that goes beyond
      the literal interpretation.
Semantics in Linguistics: Semantics is a key area of study in linguistics,
exploring how meaning is created and conveyed through language.
Semantics in a Model of Grammar: A model of grammar that includes
semantics aims to explain how meaning is generated from the structure of
language.
Historical Semantics: Historical semantics examines how word meanings
change over time.

Semantics in Other Disciplines: Semantics is also relevant in fields like
philosophy, computer science, and anthropology.
Semantics and Semiotics: Semiotics is the study of signs and symbols, and
semantics is a key part of this field.
Reference and Sense
   - Reference: the relationship between a word or phrase and the thing it
       refers to.
   - Sense: the meaning of a word or phrase in a particular context.
Utterance, Sentence and Proposition
   - Utterance: a specific instance of a sentence being used.
   - Sentence: a unit of language that expresses a complete thought.
   - Proposition: the meaning or idea expressed by a sentence.
Utterance, Sentence and Proposition -Activities: These terms are related to
how language is used to communicate and convey meaning.
Literal and Non-Literal Meaning
   - Literal meaning: the obvious or explicit meaning of a sentence or text.
   - Non-literal meaning: the implied or suggested meaning that goes
       beyond the literal interpretation.
Semantics and Pragmatics: Semantics focuses on the meaning of language,
while pragmatics considers how language is used to communicate effectively.
Meaning Holism -Meaning within Language: Meaning holism suggests
that meaning is not just a matter of individual words or phrases, but of the
entire language system.
Idea Theory of Meaning: This theory posits that meaning is based on the
ideas or concepts that words and phrases represent.
Meanings Are out in the World: This perspective suggests that meaning is
not just a matter of language, but of the world and our experiences in it.
Meaning of Sentences and Truth Condition: The meaning of a sentence is
closely tied to its truth condition, or the circumstances under which it is true.
Putting Meaning Together from Pieces – Compositional Theory: This
theory suggests that meaning is composed of smaller parts, such as words and
phrases, that combine to create the meaning of a sentence or text.
KINDS OF MEANING

     - Linguistic/Conceptual Meaning: the meaning of words and phrases
        based on their linguistic structure and conceptual relationships.
     - Connotative/Referential Meaning: the emotional or associative
        meaning of words (connotative) versus their literal reference
        (referential).
     - Social Meaning: the meaning derived from social context, norms, and
        relationships.
     - Affective Meaning: the emotional or attitudinal meaning of words and
        phrases.
     - Reflective and Collocative Meaning: the meaning derived from the
        relationship between words and their contexts (collocative) and the
        reflection of meaning back on the speaker (reflective).
     - Thematic Meaning: the underlying theme or idea conveyed by a text
        or discourse.
   MEANING, THOUGHT AND REALITY
     - Sense and Concepts: the meaning of words and phrases is related to
        the concepts they represent.
     - Necessary and Sufficient Conditions: the conditions under which a
        word or phrase applies to a concept or object.
     - Prototypes: the central, typical instances of a concept or category.
     - Linguistic Relativity: the idea that language influences thought and
        perception.
     - The Language of Thought Hypothesis: the idea that thought is
        conducted in a mental language.
   What are Lexical Relations?
Lexical relations refer to the relationships between words and their meanings,
including:
      -   Synonyms (words with similar meanings)
      -   Antonyms (words with opposite meanings)
      -   Hyponyms (words with more specific meanings)
      -   Meronyms (words with part-whole relationships)
      -   Causative verbs and agentive nouns (words that express cause-and-
          effect relationships)

      - Colour terms (words that describe colours)
   Words and Lexical Items: Words are the basic units of language, while
   lexical items are the specific instances of words in context.
   Problems with Pinning Down Word Meaning
Word meaning can be difficult to define due to:
      - Homonymy (words with multiple meanings)
      - Polysemy (words with related meanings)
      - Contextual variation
      - Semantic change over time
   LEXICAL SEMANTICS: FIELDS AND COLLOCATION
      - Pragmatic and Syntagmatic Relations: the relationships between
         words based on their use in context (pragmatic) and their grammatical
         relationships (syntagmatic).
      - Semantic Fields: the areas of meaning that words and concepts occupy,
         such as "food" or "emotions".
      - Collocations: the common combinations of words that occur together,
         such as "strong coffee" or "happy birthday".
