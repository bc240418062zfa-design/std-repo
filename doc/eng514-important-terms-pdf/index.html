• Meaning-focused Input: Activities that involve listening or reading where learners focus on
understanding messages, not language forms, e.g., listening to stories or watching videos.

• Meaning-focused Output: Tasks requiring speaking or writing where learners concentrate on
conveying ideas, e.g., giving presentations or writing essays.

• Language-focused Learning: Explicit teaching or learning of language features, such as
vocabulary, grammar, and pronunciation, using deliberate practice like drills or exercises.

• Fluency Development: Improving ease and speed of language use across skills (listening,
speaking, reading, writing), often through repetitive, familiar activities.

• Comprehensible Input: Language input that learners can understand, often slightly above their
current proficiency level, facilitated through visuals or simplified language.

• Negotiated Syllabus: A course plan developed collaboratively by teachers and learners, tailored
to learners' needs and goals.

• Syntactic Knowledge: Understanding grammar and sentence structure, essential for
comprehending complex texts or spoken language.

• Discourse Knowledge: Awareness of how language is used in different contexts, such as
storytelling or academic lectures, to structure messages coherently.

• Pragmatic Knowledge: Knowing how language is used in social contexts, including speaker
intentions and implied meanings.

• Metacognition: Awareness and regulation of one’s learning processes, such as planning,
monitoring, and evaluating comprehension and performance.

• Interactive Listening: Listening that involves real-time interaction, like in conversations,
requiring active engagement and responses.

• One-way Listening: Listening where there’s no immediate interaction, e.g., listening to lectures
or radio broadcasts.

• Two-way Listening: Engaging in dialogue or conversations where both parties actively
participate in the exchange of ideas.

• Top-down Processing: Using background knowledge, context, and expectations to understand
a message, predicting content before fully processing details.

• Bottom-up Processing: Decoding the details of language, like sounds, words, and grammar, to
understand the message.

• Listening Grids: Structured tools used in listening exercises where learners fill in information
based on what they hear, improving focused comprehension.

• Semantic Processing: The cognitive process of understanding meaning, involving connections
between new information and existing knowledge.

• Schema Activation: Using prior knowledge and experiences to make sense of new information,
crucial for comprehension.

• Comprehension Structures: Mental frameworks or maps created by listeners to organize and
integrate information from texts or speech.

• Intonation Units: Segments of speech marked by pitch and stress patterns, aiding listeners in
identifying key ideas or transitions in spoken language.

• Phonological Phrase: A chunk of speech that forms a cohesive unit in pronunciation and
meaning, often a sentence or clause.

• Phonotactic Knowledge: Understanding allowable sound combinations in a language, aiding
in recognizing spoken words.

• Allophonic Variations: Slight pronunciation changes of sounds depending on context, such as
"gonna" instead of "going to."

• Cluster Reduction: Simplification of consonant clusters in speech, e.g., "friend" pronounced
as "frien'."

• Syntactic Parsing: Analyzing sentence structure in real time during listening to assign roles
(subject, object) to words for comprehension.

• Ellipsis Calculation: Inferring omitted parts of sentences, such as pronouns or verbs, based on
context, e.g., “She can do it better than I [can].”

• Propositional Models: Mental representations of ideas conveyed in a text or speech, helping in
understanding relationships between concepts.

• Formulaic Language: Pre-learned phrases and expressions, like idioms or greetings, used to
facilitate fluency and communication.

• Kinesic Signals: Non-verbal cues from body movements, such as gestures and facial
expressions, aiding in interpreting spoken messages.

• Baton Signals: Rhythmic gestures, like hand movements, used to emphasize or punctuate
spoken language.

• Directional Gaze: Eye movements that guide listeners’ attention to visual references or indicate
focus during communication.

• Guide Signals: Movements or gestures that enhance comprehension, like pointing to an object
while describing it.

• Inferencing: Drawing logical conclusions from incomplete information, essential for
understanding implied meanings.

• Parsimony Principle: Using the simplest explanation or schema necessary for understanding a
message, avoiding overcomplication.

• Prototype Theory: The idea that understanding relies on matching new information with typical
examples stored in memory.

• McGurk Effect: A cognitive phenomenon where conflicting auditory and visual speech inputs
lead to misinterpretation, showing the interplay of senses in listening.

• Articulatory Causes: The physical movements of speech organs (tongue, lips) that produce
specific sounds, important for accurate pronunciation.

• Psychoacoustic Effects: How listeners perceive sound qualities like pitch or timbre, influencing
speech recognition.

• Recognition of Words: The process of identifying spoken words through phonological,
contextual, and syntactic cues.

• Linguistic Commitment: The specialization of brain areas for processing specific language
features, developed over time with use.

• Automaticity in Processing: The ability to quickly and efficiently process language features
without conscious effort.

• Cognitive Commitment: Engaging mental focus on a task or topic, aiding deeper understanding
and retention.

• Neural Transmission: The brain’s processing of auditory signals into comprehensible speech
through neural networks.

• Phonological Rehearsal Loop: A mechanism in short-term memory that allows listeners to
retain and process spoken language.
