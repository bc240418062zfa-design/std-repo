### Practical 1: Preparation of Slides (Dry Mount)

Instructions:
- Dry mount slides are the easiest to make.
- Materials needed: Glass slide, cover slips.
- Works best for samples like pollen, hair, feathers, or dust particles.

Steps:
1. Place the sample on the slide.
2. Cover with a cover slip to protect the sample and lenses.

Note: These mounts are temporary unless the cover slip is sealed. For viewing complex structures, a prepared mount may be necessary.

Expected Results: You should see the outline and basic structure of the sample, such as the grains of pollen or the strands of hair.

### Practical 2: Preparation of Slides (Wet Mount)

Instructions:
- Wet mounts help in viewing intricate structures and observing natural color and mobility of live specimens.

Steps:
1. Place a few drops of liquid on the slide.
2. Add the specimen to the liquid.
3. Add more liquid to cover the specimen.
4. Apply the cover slip softly to avoid air bubbles.

Note: Wet mounts may dry out under microscope light. Add extra liquid if needed.

Expected Results: The sample should be visible with clear details, and live specimens may show movement.

### Practical 3: Observation of Wet Mounts of Human Cheek Cells Employing Bright and Dark Field Microscopy

Introduction:
- Observing human cheek cells under a microscope is a simple way to view human cell structure.

Procedure:
1. Swab the inside of your cheek with a toothpick.
2. Place the swab on a slide and add a drop of water.
3. Add methylene blue to stain the cells.
4. Apply a cover slip at an angle to avoid air bubbles.
5. Observe under a microscope with X-40 and X-100 magnification.

Tip: Use iodine as an alternative stain.

Expected Results: You should see circular structures with dark centers (nuclei) and the cell membrane under higher magnification.

### Practical 4: Measurement of Cell Size: Prokaryotic (Bacterial Cell) Cell Size

Components of Prokaryotic Cells:
- Plasma membrane, cytoplasm, DNA, ribosomes.
- Prokaryotes are simple, single-celled organisms without a nucleus.

Cell Size:
- Prokaryotic cells: 0.1 to 5.0 micrometers in diameter.
- Eukaryotic cells: 10 to 100 micrometers in diameter.
- Surface-area-to-volume ratio affects nutrient exchange efficiency.

Expected Results: Prokaryotic cells should appear very small under the microscope, often requiring higher magnification to distinguish their structures.

### Practical 5: Measurement of Cell Size: Eukaryotic (Human Cheek Cell) Cell Size

Estimating Cell Sizes:
1. Prepare a wet mount slide.
2. Observe human cheek cells under a microscope.
3. Draw the field of view and representative cells.
4. Record magnification, field diameter, estimated number of cells across the field, and individual cell length.

Note: Use microns (1/1000 mm) for measurement accuracy.

Expected Results: Human cheek cells should be visible as irregularly edged circles with a visible nucleus. The size can be estimated by counting how many cells fit across the field of view and calculating the cell length accordingly.

### Practical 6: Recording of Microscopic Observations with the Help of Camera Lucida

Instructions:
- The camera lucida helps draw microscope images by reflecting light rays with a prism and plane mirror.
- Components: Ring attachment, prism, mirror.

Steps:
1. Attach the camera lucida to the microscope.
2. Use the prism to project the image onto the plane paper.
3. Trace the image on the paper.

Expected Results: You should obtain an accurate and detailed drawing of the microscopic image.

### Practical 7: Liquid Handling: Proper Use of Pipettes

Instructions:
- Micropipettes are used to transfer small volumes of liquids.
- Four sizes: P10, P20, P200, P1000.

Steps:
1. Select the appropriate pipette size.
2. Set the desired volume.
3. Load a sterile tip.
4. Press the plunger to the first stop and immerse the tip in the liquid.
5. Release the plunger to draw up the liquid.
6. To dispense, press the plunger to the first stop, wait, then press to the second stop.

Expected Results: Accurate transfer of specified volumes of liquid with no air bubbles or inaccuracies.

### Practical 8: Liquid Handling: Proper Use of Micropipettes

Instructions:
- Always select the smallest pipette for the volume.
- Use blue tips for P1000 and clear tips for P200 and smaller.

Steps:
1. Set the volume by turning the central rings.
2. Load a sterile tip.
3. Press the plunger to the first stop and immerse the tip in the liquid.
4. Release the plunger to draw up the liquid.
5. Dispense by pressing the plunger to the first stop, then to the second stop.

Expected Results: Precise and accurate liquid measurements with no contamination.

### Practical 9: Hematoxylin and Eosin Staining (Harris’ Hematoxylin and Eosin)

Instructions:
    • H&E staining is widely used in medical diagnosis to view cell structures.

Materials:
    • Acid alcohol solution, ammonia water solution, lithium carbonate solution, eosin-phloxine B solution, hematoxylin solution.

Steps:
1. Deparaffinize and rehydrate sections.
2. Stain with Harris hematoxylin.
3. Differentiate in acid alcohol.
4. Blue in ammonia water.
5. Counterstain in eosin-phloxine solution.
6. Dehydrate, clear, and mount.

Expected Results: Nuclei should appear blue, while the cytoplasm will be pink to red.

### Practical 10: Gram’s Staining

Instructions:
    • Gram staining differentiates bacteria into Gram-positive and Gram-negative.

Steps:
1. Prepare a heat-fixed smear.
2. Stain with crystal violet.
3. Add Gram’s iodine.
4. Decolorize with acetone.
5. Counterstain with safranin.

Expected Results: Gram-positive bacteria will appear purple, and Gram-negative bacteria will appear pink or red.

### Practical 11: Handling of Centrifuge Machines

**Instructions:**
- Centrifuges separate components in a mixture using centrifugal force.
- Types include low-speed, high-speed, and ultracentrifuges.

**Components:**
- Rotor: Holds samples, can be horizontal, fixed-angle, or vertical.
- Motor: Drives the rotor.
- Imbalance detector: Shuts off if imbalance is detected.
- Tachometer: Indicates speed.
