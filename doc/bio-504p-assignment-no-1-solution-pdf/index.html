Question # 1:
Why Ninhydrin give a purple colored complex when reacts with α-amino acids
(-NH2) of proteins? (Marks 5)
Solution:
Reaction of Ninhydrin with α-Amino Acids
       Ninhydrin is a chemical reagent widely used for the detection of amino acids and proteins. It reacts
specifically with free α-amino groups (-NH₂) of amino acids to form a deep purple-colored complex
known as Ruhemann’s purple. This reaction is crucial in biochemical studies and forensic science
(e.g., fingerprint detection).

Structure of α-Amino Acids
  •   General Formula: H₂N–CHR–COOH
  •   All α-amino acids have a primary amine (-NH₂) group attached to the carbon atom adjacent to the
      carboxyl (-COOH) group.
  •   This α-positioned amine is key for the Ninhydrin reaction.

Chemical Nature of Ninhydrin
  •   Structure: Ninhydrin (triketohydrindene hydrate) is a powerful oxidizing agent.
  •   Function: It acts by reacting with the amino group of amino acids to yield a colored product.

Mechanism of Ninhydrin Reaction with α-Amino Acids
  1. Deamination and Decarboxylation:
        o   Ninhydrin reacts with the free α-amino group, removing the amine as ammonia (NH₃).
        o   Simultaneously, the carboxyl group is removed as carbon dioxide (CO₂).
  2. Formation of Aldehyde:
       o The carbon skeleton of the amino acid is converted into an aldehyde with one less carbon
         atom.
  3. Hydrindantin Formation:
       o During this process, Ninhydrin is reduced to hydrindantin, a colorless intermediate.

  4. Condensation Reaction:
        o   The released ammonia reacts with another molecule of ninhydrin and hydrindantin.
        o   This forms Ruhemann’s purple, the characteristic deep violet or purple color.

Why Purple Color Appears
  •   The purple color is due to the formation of Ruhemann's purple, a chromophore with a conjugated
      double bond system that absorbs visible light.
  •   The intense color is a result of the complex structure that stabilizes the excited electrons, allowing
      visible light absorption and purple color reflection.

Conclusion
     The purple color observed when ninhydrin reacts with α-amino acids is due to the formation of
Ruhemann’s purple, following a multi-step process involving deamination, decarboxylation, and
condensation. This reaction is both qualitative and semi-quantitative, making it a vital tool in
biochemical and forensic applications.

Question # 2:

Why Benedict’s test gives red color with reducing sugars? Give reasons.
(Marks 5)
Solution:
Benedict’s Test for Reducing Sugars
      Benedict's test is a widely used qualitative test to detect the presence of reducing sugars. It is based
on the ability of certain sugars to reduce copper(II) ions (Cu²⁺) to copper(I) ions (Cu⁺), resulting in a
color change that can be interpreted to indicate the presence and concentration of reducing sugars.

Principle of Benedict’s Test
      Benedict's reagent contains copper(II) sulfate (CuSO₄), sodium carbonate (Na₂CO₃), and sodium
citrate. The copper(II) ions (Cu²⁺) present in the reagent are blue in color. When a reducing sugar is
present in the test solution and heated with Benedict’s reagent, the reducing sugar donates electrons to
the copper(II) ions, reducing them to copper(I) ions (Cu⁺). The copper(I) ions then react with other
components in the solution, forming a red, orange, or yellow precipitate, depending on the amount of
reducing sugar present. The resulting color change is due to the formation of insoluble copper(I) oxide
(Cu₂O).

Why Benedict’s Test Gives a Red Color with Reducing Sugars
  1. Reduction of Copper(II) Ions:
     The key factor in the Benedict's test is the reduction of copper(II) ions (Cu²⁺) to copper(I) ions
     (Cu⁺). Reducing sugars have a free aldehyde group (-CHO) or a free ketone group (-C=O) capable
     of acting as reducing agents. The reducing sugar donates electrons to the Cu²⁺ ions, which are
     reduced to Cu⁺ ions.
  2. Formation of Copper(I) Oxide:
     The copper(I) ions formed in the reaction are highly reactive and combine with hydroxide ions
     (OH⁻) in the solution to form copper(I) oxide (Cu₂O), a red, insoluble compound. The more
     reducing sugar present, the more copper(I) oxide is formed, resulting in a deeper red precipitate.
  3. Color Gradation Based on Sugar Concentration:
     The intensity of the red color depends on the concentration of reducing sugar in the solution. If a
     small amount of reducing sugar is present, the solution may turn a green or yellow color due to the
     formation of copper(I) oxide in smaller quantities. With a higher concentration of reducing sugar, a
     more intense red color (indicating a greater amount of copper(I) oxide) is produced. This is why
     Benedict’s test is often used to semi-quantify the amount of reducing sugar in a sample.
  4. The Role of the Aldehyde or Ketone Group:
     The free aldehyde group in reducing sugars is essential for the reaction. The aldehyde group (in

     reduced to copper(I) ions. This oxidation-reduction process is fundamental to the reaction and
     leads to the formation of copper(I) oxide, which is responsible for the red color.
