  a) Label the names of Figure A, B, C, D, E, F, and G shown in the above
     picture. Write the possible methods used for sterilization of each separately
     along with principle of that sterilization technique and why it should be
     used for that particular object (1x7= 7 Marks).
  b) Write down the name of object shown in Figure H and write its function in
     the lab. (1+2=3 marks)

Solution (a):
     The following are the names of the laboratory equipment shown in Figure A to G, along with the
possible methods used for their sterilization, the principle behind each sterilization technique, and the
reason why each method is suitable for the respective object.

Figure A: Petri Dishes
  •   Sterilization Method: Autoclaving
  •   Principle: Autoclaving uses high-pressure saturated steam at 121°C for 15–20 minutes. The high
      temperature and pressure denature microbial proteins, nucleic acids, and enzymes, leading to the
      death of all forms of life, including bacteria, fungi, and spores.
  •   Why Suitable: Petri dishes, especially those made of glass, are commonly reused in microbiology
      labs for culturing bacteria and fungi. Because microbial cultures must grow in a sterile
      environment, autoclaving is essential to ensure complete sterilization. Autoclaving is safe for glass
      and can sterilize large batches at once, making it efficient and reliable.

Figure B: Test Tubes
  •   Sterilization Method: Autoclaving
  •   Principle: Steam sterilization under pressure causes irreversible coagulation and denaturation of
      proteins, thereby killing all microorganisms including spores. Steam effectively penetrates glass
      materials and distributes heat evenly.
  •   Why Suitable: Test tubes are often used to hold media, reagents, or microbial cultures. Glass test
      tubes are heat-resistant and can tolerate autoclaving without damage. This method guarantees the
      sterility of the test tubes before they are used for culturing or chemical reactions, minimizing any
      risk of contamination.

Figure C: Inoculating Loops & Spatulas
  •   Sterilization Method: Flame Sterilization
  •   Principle: Flame sterilization involves directly exposing metal instruments to an open flame, such
      as a Bunsen burner. The intense heat incinerates any adhering microorganisms through oxidative
      destruction.
  •   Why Suitable: Inoculating loops and spatulas are frequently used for transferring microbial
      cultures or small quantities of chemicals. Since they are often reused within a single experiment or
      between samples, flaming offers a fast, effective, and accessible way to sterilize them on the spot.
      The method ensures aseptic technique without requiring complex equipment.

Figure D: Serological Pipettes
  •   Sterilization Method: Autoclaving (for reusable pipettes) or pre-sterilized single-use pipettes
  •   Principle: Autoclaving uses moist heat under pressure to coagulate microbial proteins, resulting in
      microbial death. The heat and steam penetrate the glass pipette's surface and interior effectively.
  •   Why Suitable: Pipettes are used to measure and transfer exact liquid volumes in sterile
      procedures. Glass pipettes can be safely autoclaved and reused, while plastic ones are usually
      disposed of after use and come pre-sterilized. Autoclaving reusable pipettes eliminates all
      microbial contaminants and prevents cross-contamination in sensitive procedures like tissue
      culture or media preparation.

Figure E: Micropipette Tips in Tip Boxes
  •   Sterilization Method: Autoclaving
  •   Principle: High-pressure steam at 121°C destroys microbial cells and spores through protein
      denaturation and disruption of cellular structures.
  •   Why Suitable: Micropipette tips are used in highly sensitive molecular biology techniques, such
      as PCR, DNA extraction, and enzyme assays. Even minimal contamination can lead to inaccurate
      results or complete experimental failure. Sterile tips prevent contamination of samples and
      reagents. Tip boxes containing autoclavable tips are sterilized in advance and kept sealed until use
      to maintain aseptic conditions.

Figure F: Laminar Air Flow Cabinet
  •   Sterilization Method: UV Sterilization and Surface Disinfection
  •   Principle: Ultraviolet (UV) radiation (typically UV-C at 254 nm) causes the formation of thymine
      dimers in microbial DNA, leading to mutations and ultimately cell death. UV rays are effective in
      inactivating a wide range of microorganisms, including bacteria, fungi, and viruses.
  •   Why Suitable: Laminar air flow cabinets are designed to provide a sterile working area by
      filtering air through HEPA filters. Before and after use, UV light is used to sterilize the work
      surface. Surface disinfectants are also used to clean the interior physically. This combination
      ensures the prevention of any microbial contamination during critical laboratory work such as cell
      culture, media preparation, and aseptic transfer of materials.

Figure G: Buffer Solution Bottle
