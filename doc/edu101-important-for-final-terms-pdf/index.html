                    EDU101
Conflict Theories (130–131)

   1. Conflict Theories: Emphasize power struggles between dominant and subordinate groups,
      explaining social order through economic, political, and cultural power.
   2. Achievement Ideology: The belief that success is based on merit, often disguising
      systemic inequalities.
   3. Cultural Capital: The knowledge, habits, and social advantages passed through families
      and schools.

Interactional Theories (132)

   4. Interactional Theories: Critique functional and conflict theories by analyzing micro-level
      interactions, like labeling students as "gifted" or "disabled."

Schooling Effects on Individuals (133–134)

   5. Knowledge Impact: The information students acquire through education.
   6. Attitude Development: Changes in student behavior and mindset due to schooling.
   7. Employment Mobility: How education influences career opportunities.

Labeling Theory (137–138)

   8. Labeling: The process of assigning evaluative tags to students, impacting their academic
      and social experiences.
   9. Self-Fulfilling Prophecy: When expectations influence behaviors, reinforcing initial
      assumptions.

Sociological Foundations of Education (123–140)

   10. Functionalism: Focuses on education’s role in promoting social cohesion.
   11. Determinism vs. Voluntarism: Behaviors shaped by external forces versus individual free
       will.
   12. Diversity in Schools: Addressing cultural, gender, and racial differences in education.

Educational Change and Curriculum (140)

   13. Dynamic Education Goals: Adapting aims of education to technological and societal
       changes.
   14. Ideological Curriculum: Balancing worldly knowledge with preparation for the afterlife.

Psychological Foundations of Education (69–121)

   15. Behaviorist Theories: Focus on observable behaviors influenced by reinforcement (e.g.,
       Skinner’s Operant Conditioning).
   16. Cognitive Theories: Emphasize mental processes, like memory and problem-solving (e.g.,
       Piaget’s developmental stages).
   17. Phenomenology: Studies consciousness and subjective experiences, focusing on
       individual perspectives.

Thorndike’s Laws of Learning (74–76)

   18. Law of Readiness: Learning is most effective when individuals are prepared for it.
   19. Law of Exercise: Repetition strengthens learning connections.
   20. Law of Effect: Positive reinforcement strengthens behaviors, while negative outcomes
       weaken them.

Operant Conditioning (83–86)

   21. Positive Reinforcement: Adding stimuli to encourage desired behaviors.
   22. Negative Reinforcement: Removing adverse stimuli to reinforce behavior.
   23. Punishment: Discouraging undesired behaviors through adverse consequences.

Lev Vygotsky’s Theories (109–115)

   24. Zone of Proximal Development (ZPD): The range between what a learner can do
       independently and with assistance.
   25. Scaffolding: Temporary support provided to learners to achieve tasks they couldn’t
       complete alone.

Historical Foundations of Education (141–266)

   26. Pre-Literate Societies: Education focused on survival skills and oral transmission of
       knowledge.
   27. Confucian Principles: Education aimed at preparing elites for governance.
   28. Greek Education: Focused on civic responsibility and personal development.
   29. Roman Education: Prioritized administrative and military skills.
   30. Medieval Education: Centered on religious instruction and social order.

Renaissance and Reformation (149–150)

   31. Humanism: Emphasized classical knowledge, Greek and Latin, focusing on literature, art,
       and philosophy.
   32. Reformation Education: Focused on literacy and religious instruction to build
       commitment to specific religious denominations.
   33. Dual Track System: Differentiated schools based on socioeconomic class and career
       goals.

Important Educational Theorists (151–152)

   34. Confucius: Developed an ethical system emphasizing hierarchy, duties, and societal
       stability.
   35. Socrates: Advocated for self-examination and philosophical dialogue.
   36. Plato: Believed in idealism and classifying humans by intellectual capabilities.
   37. Aristotle: Supported realism and rational observation to guide conduct.

Eastern Philosophers and Islamic Education (153–157)

   38. Farabi: Distinguished teaching from education and emphasized innate human wisdom.
   39. Ibn Sina (Avicenna): Advocated for teaching moral values alongside knowledge.
   40. Biruni: Emphasized the importance of understanding and respecting diverse cultures.
   41. Madrasas: Islamic educational institutions teaching both religious and vocational subjects.

Ottoman Education (157–158)

   42. Madrasa System: Included primary, middle, and high schools, focusing on religious,
       philosophical, and scientific education.
   43. Enderun Schools: Educated talented children for administrative roles, especially from
       non-Muslim families.

Industrialization and Education (159)

   44. Technological Influence: Education evolved to meet the demands of industrialization,
       such as operating machinery.
   45. New Philosophies: Systems like socialism, communism, and capitalism shaped
       educational goals.

Philosophical Foundations of Education (160–163)

   46. Materialism: Focuses on physical and economic realities shaping education.
   47. Existentialism: Emphasizes individual choice and self-directed learning.
   48. Postmodernism: Critiques grand narratives, focusing on pluralism and localized truths.
   49. Critical Theory: Examines how power structures marginalize individuals through
       educational institutions.

Educational Psychology (168–171)

   50. Jean Piaget: Identified four stages of cognitive development (e.g., sensorimotor,
       preoperational, concrete operational, formal operational).
   51. Lev Vygotsky: Emphasized social learning and the role of language in cognitive
       development.
