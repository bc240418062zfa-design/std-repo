                                  SHORT QUESTIONS(3)

   1. Define Population.
Population is group of individuals belonging to same species occupying same space in particular time.
   2. Define biogeography.
Biogeography is the scientific study of the distribution patterns of organisms across space and time,
examining how ecological and evolutionary factors shape the geographic range and diversity of
species.
   3. What are heterotrophs?
Heterotrophs are organisms that obtain their energy and nutrients by consuming other organisms or
organic matter, as they are unable to produce their own food through photosynthesis. E.g.: Animals.
   4. Define Omnivores.
An omnivore is an organism that eats plants and animals. The term stems from the Latin words
omnis, meaning “all or everything,” and vorare, meaning “to devour or eat.” Example: humans.
Primary omnivores are animals that mainly eat plants, but also consume small amounts of animal
matter. Examples of primary omnivores include humans, bears, and pigs.
Secondary omnivores are animals that mainly eat other animals, but also consume some plants.
Examples of secondary omnivores include raccoons, foxes, and some bird species.
   5. Mutualism
• Long-term relationship between individuals of different species • Both individuals get benefit
Example: An example of mutualism is the relationship between bees and flowers. Bees rely on
flowers for nectar and pollen as a food source, while flowers rely on bees for pollination. As bees
collect nectar from flowers, they transfer pollen from one flower to another, allowing the flowers to
reproduce and produce seeds.
   6. Define Population growth rate.
Population growth rate is the rate at which the number of individuals in a population increases within
a specific time period, and it is specific to each species.
   7. How many types of energy exist in ecosystem?
Energy exists in several different forms
   1) Light
   2) Chemical-bond energy
   3) Motion
   4) Heat
All organisms need energy to function
   8. Layers of atmosphere:
   1)     Troposphere: 0 to 12 km
   2)     Stratosphere: 12 to 50 km
   3)     Mesosphere: 50 to 80 km
   4)     Thermosphere: 80 to 700 km
   5)     Exosphere: 700 to 10,000 km

   9. Height of stratospheres at equator and pole.
   •    20 km (66,000 ft; 12 mi) at equator
   •    10 km (33,000 ft; 6.2 mi) at midalttide
   •    7 km (23,000 ft; 4.3 mi) at the poles

   10.        Role of Wisconsin Madison University in restoration evolution?
The University of Wisconsin–Madison Arboretum has pioneered restoration ecology.
   a) The restoration of the prairie was at an early stage in November 1935.
   b) The prairie as it looks today. This picture was taken at approximately the same location as the
      1935 photograph
The University of Wisconsin-Madison has contributed significantly to the study of restoration ecology
and evolutionary biology. Its strong research program, including the Department of Integrative
Biology and the Nelson Institute for Environmental Studies, has led to important discoveries in
population genetics, social behavior, and ecosystem restoration. Faculty and researchers at UW-
Madison continue to advance our understanding of these fields.
   11.        Habitat fragmentation?
Habitat fragmentation refers to the process in which a large, continuous habitat is divided into
smaller, isolated fragments. This fragmentation occurs due to human activities such as urbanization,
agriculture, and infrastructure development.
   12.        What is aestivation?
Survival in extended period of drying. Avoid temperature damage and dehydration. Aestivation or
“summer sleep”, on the other hand, is the low metabolic process by the animals during summers.
Examples: Insects, frog, turtle
   13.        Analogy and homology
Homology involves the study of organs that have evolved from same origin or from common
ancestor but have different functions. For example, wings of bat and hands of humans.
Analogy involves the study of organs that are evolved from different ancestors but perform a similar
function. For example, wings of bat and wings of insects, which have similar function but different
structure.
   14.        Ecological Pyramids
An ecological pyramid is a graphical representation of the relationship between different
organisms in an ecosystem. It shows the number, biomass, and energy of different trophic levels in an
ecosystem.
There are three different types of ecological pyramids to illustrate ecosystems:
   1) Pyramid of energy: represents how much energy is available in each trophic level
   2) Pyramid of numbers: represents the actual number of organisms present in each trophic
      level
   3) Pyramid of biomass: represents the total mass of living things in each trophic level

   15.        What is ecological imbalance? Write two examples.
Ecological imbalance refers to a disruption or disturbance in the natural equilibrium of an ecosystem,
often caused by human activities or natural events, resulting in negative impacts on the environment
and the organisms that depend on it.
Examples:
   1) Overfishing leading to the collapse of marine food chains and loss of biodiversity.
   2) Deforestation causing habitat destruction and endangering numerous species

   16.        Ecological niche
In a community, each organism faces the challenge of survival in its own unique way. It occupies a
specific functional role and place called its niche. An organism's niche is defined by how it uses the
resources available in its environment. This includes how it utilizes space, consumes food, tolerates
temperature ranges, finds suitable conditions for mating, and has specific moisture requirements.
The niche of an organism can be described by looking at these factors:
   •   how it occupies space,
   •   what it eats,
   •   its temperature preferences,
   •   its mating requirements,
   •   and its need for moisture.
