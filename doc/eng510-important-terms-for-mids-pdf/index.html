➡️What is Sociolinguistics?

Sociolinguistics studies the relationship between language and society, examining how language
use varies across social contexts.

➡️Some Definitions of Sociolinguistics

   -   The study of language in social contexts
   -   The examination of language variation and change
   -   The analysis of language as a social phenomenon

➡️Sociolinguistics and Linguistics

Sociolinguistics complements linguistics by focusing on language use beyond structural analysis.

➡️Sociolinguistics and the Sociology of Language

Sociolinguistics overlaps with sociology of language, but focuses on language-specific issues.

➡️Sociolinguistics and Other Disciplines

   -   Anthropology
   -   Sociology
   -   Psychology
   -   Education

➡️Sociolinguistic Phenomena include:

   -   Language variation
   -   Language change
   -   Language contact
   -   Language attitudes

➡️Varieties of Language include:

   -   Dialects
   -   Registers
   -   Jargons

   -   Idiolects

➡️Linguistic Items

   -   Phonological
   -   Morphological
   -   Syntactic
   -   Lexical

➡️Variety and Lect

   -   Variety: a language subset
   -   Lect: a specific language variety

➡️Varieties of language:

   -   Regional dialects
   -   Social dialects
   -   Occupational dialects
   -   Ethnic dialects

➡️ Language attitudes:

   -   Prestige
   -   Stigma
   -   Neutral

➡️Speech Communities

Speech communities are:

   -   Groups sharing language norms
   -   Social networks
   -   Language repertoires

➡️Intersecting Communities

Communities intersect through:

   -   Overlapping membership
   -   Shared language practices

➡️Rejecting the Idea of Speech Communities

Critics argue speech communities are:

   -   Too broad
   -   Too narrow
   -   Too static

➡️Networks and Repertoires

   -   Describe language use
   -   Explain language variation

➡️Access to the Codes

   -   Influences language use
   -   Affects language change

➡️Rigidity of the Social Matrix

   -   Restricts language change
   -   Maintains language norms

➡️Variables and Variants

   -   Describe language variation
   -   Explain language change

➡️Synchronic Variation

Studies language variation at a single point

➡️ Diachronic Variation

Studies language change over time

➡️ Some Examples of Dialects

Dialects:

   -   Regional (e.g., Southern American)
   -   Social (e.g., African American Vernacular)
   -   Occupational (e.g., Medical jargon)

➡️ Standard Languages

   -   Official recognition
   -   Widespread use

➡️ The Delimitation of Languages

Language delimitation:

    -   Geographic
    -   Cultural
    -   Linguistic criteria

➡️ The Family Tree Model

The family tree model illustrates language relationships, showing how languages diverge and
evolve.

➡️ Regional Dialects

Regional dialects vary geographically, reflecting local culture and history.

➡️ Social Dialects

Social dialects emerge from social factors like class, ethnicity, or occupation.

➡️ Idiolects

Idiolects are unique individual language varieties.

➡️ Dialects and Identity

Dialects often reflect speakers' identity, including regional, social, or cultural affiliations.

➡️ Study of Dialects in Sociolinguistics

Sociolinguistics examines dialects to understand language variation and social context.

➡️ Language and Dialect

Language and dialect distinctions are often blurry, with dialects sometimes considered
languages.

➡️ Regional Dialectology

Regional dialectology studies geographic language variation.

➡️ The Work of Labov

William Labov's research on language variation and change pioneered sociolinguistics.

➡️ Martha's Vineyard

Labov's Martha's Vineyard study demonstrated how social factors influence language.

➡️ Register

A register is a language variety associated with specific contexts or purposes.

➡️ Examples of Register

Registers include:

   -   Formal
   -   Informal
   -   Technical
   -   Literary

➡️ Register vs. Dialect

Registers differ from dialects in their contextual dependence.

➡️ Jargon

Jargon is specialized vocabulary used within professions or groups.

➡️ Accent

Accent refers to pronunciation characteristics.

➡️ Accents vs. Dialect

Accents differ from dialects in focusing on pronunciation rather than vocabulary/grammar.

➡️ Style

Style concerns language choices in specific contexts.

➡️ Register vs. Style

Registers are more formal, while style is more flexible.

➡️ Speech Communities and Communicative Competence

Speech communities share language norms, and communicative competence enables effective
communication.

➡️ Code

Code refers to language varieties or systems.

➡️ Bilingualism

Bilingualism involves proficiency in two languages.

➡️ Bilinguality vs. Bilingualism

Bilinguality emphasizes individual language ability, while bilingualism focuses on societal
language use.

➡️ Bi/Multilingualism and Sociolinguistics

Sociolinguistics examines bilingualism/multilingualism in social contexts.

➡️ Measuring Bilingualism

Bilingualism is measured through:

   -   Proficiency tests
   -   Language use surveys

➡️ Code Switching and Sociolinguistics

Code switching involves alternating languages or varieties.

➡️ Code Mixing and Sociolinguistics

Code mixing combines languages or varieties within utterances.

➡️ Tag Switching

Tag switching involves inserting words/phrases from another language.

➡️ Why Code Switching and Mixing?

Code switching/mixing facilitates communication, expresses identity, or navigates social
contexts.

➡️ Hybridization

Hybridization creates new language forms through blending.

➡️ Social Correlates

Social correlates include:

   -   Social class
   -   Gender
   -   Age
   -   Ethnicity

➡️ Politeness and Sociolinguistics

Politeness concerns language use to maintain social relationships.
