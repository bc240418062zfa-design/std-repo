                                              Lesson-08

     Quantitative analyses of the existing data often show that differences between a bilingual
      speaker’s brain and a monolingual’s brain are the exceptions rather than the rule.

     Bilinguals do not seem to vary from monolinguals in neurological processes.

     The lateralization of language in the brains of the two groups of speakers is also similar.

     A related issue concerns the mental representation of a bilingual’s two languages and the
      processing emanating from such representation.

     Evidence exists for both separate storage and shared storage of the two languages in the
      bilingual’s brain.

     Bilinguals have a language store for each of their two languages and a more general conceptual
      store.

     There are strong, direct interconnecting channels between each of these three separate stores.

     The interconnections between the two languages comprise association and translation systems,
      and common images in the conceptual store act as mediators.

     Speakers of different proficiency levels or at different acquisitional stages vary in strength and
      directness of the interconnections between the separate stores in language processing;

     Highly proficient speakers in two languages may go directly from a concept to the target
      language.

     Those speakers whose second language is weaker than their first tend to use the first language to
      mediate.

     Bilingualism refers to someone who can function in both languages in conversational interaction.

     Bilingual speakers choose to use their different languages depending on a variety of factors,
      including the type of person addressed and different situations and contexts.

     There is a widespread impression that bilingual speakers code-switch because they cannot
      express themselves adequately in one language.

     Code-switching is an extremely common practice among bilinguals and takes many forms.

     Code-switching involves skilled manipulation of overlapping sections of two (or more) grammars.

     Code-switching is a discrete mode of speaking, emanating from a single code-switching grammar.

     Code-switching grammar is concerned that the two languages involved do not play the same role
      in sentence making.

     Code-switching; is not a simple combination of two sets of grammatical rules but grammatical
      integration of one language in another.

     One consequence of the ‘two-in-one’ perspective is that bilingual speakers are often compared
      to monolinguals in terms of their language proficiency.

     Psycholinguistic studies, tests are given without taking into account that bilingual speakers may
      have learnt their two languages under different conditions for different purposes, and they only
      use them in different situations.

     Bilingual speakers have a unique linguistic and psychological profile; their two languages are
      constantly in different states of activation; they are able to call upon their linguistic knowledge
      and resources according to the context and adapt their behaviour.

     Interpreters and translators, switch between languages as a routine part of their job.

     Professional interpreters and translators as special people with highly developed language skills
      in each of their languages. They are trained to translate from the ‘passive’ to the ‘active’
      language. They are also trained to think rapidly of appropriate wording of ideas and produce
      words from a restricted area of meaning.

     Another group of bilinguals engage themselves in cross-modality language production. This is the
      case with speech-sign bilinguals who, in addition to the oral modality, use the manual-visual
      modality in everyday communication.

     The early nineteenth century was a time of a widespread belief that bilingualism has a
      detrimental effect on a human being’s intellectual and spiritual growth.

     A bilingual who could not acquire full competence in any of the languages were termed as
      ‘semilinguals’.
