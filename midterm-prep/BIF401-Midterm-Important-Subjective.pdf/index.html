  7. What is the scope of bioinformatics?
    Topic: Introduction to Bioinformatics

    Bioinformatics is the use of computational algorithms and techniques for: 1. Storage, 2. Organization, 3. Analysis, and 4. Representation of biological information.

  8. What are the few challenges / frontiers in the field of bioinformatics?
    Topic: Introduction to Bioinformatics

    Bioinformatics is full of challenges and opportunities. Amongst other frontiers in bioinformatics, there are protein structures, systems biology and personalized medicine. Accurate solution of protein structure is one of the toughest problems, and identification of unknown types and roles of RNA and of post-translational modifications are other important frontiers.

  9. Define system biology and write its applications.
    Topic: Introduction to Bioinformatics

    Systems biology is the computational and mathematical modeling of complex biological systems. Applications: • Model protein and gene interactions • Dynamical analysis of such models • Understand system properties • Predict system level behaviours

  10. Which databases are used for proteins?
    Topic: Biological Databases

    UniProt and Swiss-Prot are the famous databases used for protein sequences. Protein sequences are reported from sequencing experiments and this data is stored in protein sequence databases, the famous ones being UniProt & Swiss-Prot.

  11. Generally used databases and which type of information do they store?
    Topic: Biological Databases

    Generally used databases are GenBank, PDB, UniProt and Swiss-Prot. The type of information they store includes: • Locus • Accession number • Sequence • Molecular mass • Authors • Journal etc.

  12. What is the purpose of UniProt and what are its features?
    Topic: Biological Databases

    UniProt is a freely accessible database of protein sequence and functional information, many entries being derived from genome sequencing projects. It contains a large amount of information about the biological function of proteins derived from the research literature. UniProt is used to search the sequence of proteins, and its queries allow batch search with UniProt IDs or conversion of IDs to another type of database ID (or vice versa), and finding sequences that exactly match a query peptide sequence.

  13. How many types of databases are there in bioinformatics and what type of information do they contain?
    Topic: Biological Databases

    Bioinformatics databases contain different formats of data including text, sequence data, protein structure and links. Each of these can be found from certain sources, for example: text formats are provided by PubMed and OMIM; sequence data is provided by GenBank, in terms of DNA, and UniProt, in terms of protein. The nucleotide database contains sequence data from GenBank, EMBL, DDBJ as well as from the Genome Sequence Database and the US Patent and Trademark Office, and it includes STSs and ESTs.

  14. Enlist the fields of bioinformatics that can be explored under ExPASy.
    Topic: Biological Databases

    ExPASy is developed by the Swiss Institute of Bioinformatics (SIB). The website provides access to databases and tools for proteomics, genomics, phylogeny, systems biology, population genetics, transcriptomics etc. ExPASy provides access to a variety of online databases and tools; depending upon your requirement, you find sequence information from ExPASy. ExPASy stands for Expert Protein Analysis System.

  15. What is a dot plot matrix? What are the benefits of dot plots?
    Topic: Sequence Alignment and Dot Plots

    Dot plots employ a dot matrix with two sequences plotted on top & left of the matrix. Matches are represented by dots, and dots on diagonals are connected and represent alignments. Benefits: Dot plots are one of the simplest plots for comparing two sequences. They are useful for highlighting matching regions, clusters and gaps, repeats and inverted regions, and they conserve the numerical information of the comparison. Aligned portions of the sequences appear as diagonal lines in the plot.

  16. How can we modify a dot plot to consider matches and mismatches in an alignment?
    Topic: Sequence Alignment and Dot Plots

    In a simple dot plot only matches are marked with dots. To consider mismatches, a scoring scheme is used in which matches are labelled by +1 instead of a dot, while mismatches (substitutions) and indels can be included in the dot plot as -1. In this way the dot matrix becomes a scoring matrix in which diagonal moves represent matches/mismatches and the highest scoring path gives the alignment.

  17. Define identity and similarity. Also write the formula of identity.
    Topic: Sequence Alignment and Dot Plots

    Identity is the count (number) of exact matches between two sequences; gaps are excluded. Similarity is the comparison between sequences calculated by using the alignment approach. Formula of identity: Identity = No. of Matches / smaller length × 100

  18. What is the difference between local and global alignment?
    Topic: Sequence Alignment and Dot Plots

    Global alignment maximizes the number of matches between the query and source sequences along the entire length of both the sequences. Local alignment gives the highest scoring local match between both the query and source sequences, and is used for finding similar domains and motifs and for detecting distant homology.

  19. What is the difference between an indel and a substitution?
    Topic: Sequence Alignment and Dot Plots

    Insertions or deletions (“indels”) result in gaps in alignments, and they are treated using gaps and gap penalties. Substitutions result in mismatches in the alignment, and they are treated with substitution penalties.

  20. Why do we need dynamic programming?
    Topic: Dynamic Programming, Scoring Matrices and MSA

    Dynamic programming is both a mathematical optimization method and a computer programming method. In computer science, if a problem can be solved optimally by breaking it into sub-problems and then recursively finding the optimal solutions to the sub-problems, then it is said to have optimal substructure. Sequence analysis is a costly process, and dynamic programming helps to reduce the costs of sequence analysis by breaking the alignment problem into smaller sub-problems and reusing their solutions.

  21. Why do we need to use dynamic programming in predicting RNA structure?
    Topic: Dynamic Programming, Scoring Matrices and MSA

    RNA sequences comprise 4 types of nucleotides; G/C, G/U & A/U are complementary and can form H-bonds. RNA sequences may contain hundreds of nucleotides, hence many combinations are possible. So, given an RNA sequence, there is a large number of possible 2’ (secondary) structures. This presents us with an extremely complex and large problem, which is solved efficiently by dynamic programming.

  22. What is a scoring matrix? Write in detail, along with the scoring matrix formula.
    Topic: Dynamic Programming, Scoring Matrices and MSA

    Scoring matrices are used to determine the relative score made by matching two characters in a sequence alignment. To quantify the similarity achieved by an alignment, scoring matrices are used: they contain a value for each possible substitution, and the alignment score is the sum of the matrix’s entries for each aligned amino acid pair. There are many flavours of scoring matrices for amino acid sequences, nucleotide sequences and codon sequences, and each is derived from the alignment of “known” homologous sequences. There are 2 types of scoring matrices i.e. PAM and BLOSUM. Formula for scoring matrix: s(a,b) = (1/λ) log ( p_ab / (f_a × f_b) )

  23. What is a PAM matrix? Write the steps to compute PAM matrices.
    Topic: Dynamic Programming, Scoring Matrices and MSA

    PAM means “Point Accepted Mutations”. A point accepted mutation is a substitution of one amino acid by another such that the protein function stays conserved. A PAM unit is the time in which about 1% of amino acids in a sequence undergo accepted mutations. Steps to compute PAM matrices: Step 1: Align protein sequences which are 1-PAM unit diverged. Step 2: Let A(i,j) be the number of times Ai is substituted by Aj. Step 3: Compute the frequency fi of amino acid Ai. Then, PAM1 = p(i,j), and PAM’n’ = (PAM1)^n.

  24. Write down the steps of BLOSUM matrices.
    Topic: Dynamic Programming, Scoring Matrices and MSA

    Step 1: Collecting sample blocks — aligned, ungapped blocks of protein sequences from related proteins are collected. Step 2: Computing probabilities — the frequencies of amino acid pairs within the blocks are counted and the probabilities of each pair are computed. Step 3: Computing the BLOSUM matrix — log-odds scores are calculated for every pair of amino acids from the observed and expected frequencies, giving the BLOSUM matrix (e.g. BLOSUM62).

  25. What is the basic principle of progressive alignment for MSA? Also write the uses of ClustalW.
    Topic: Dynamic Programming, Scoring Matrices and MSA

    Progressive alignment methods build a multiple sequence alignment step by step: first the most similar pair of sequences is aligned, and then sequences/profiles are progressively added according to a guide tree. These methods are efficient enough to implement on a large scale for many (100s to 1000s of) sequences. Uses of ClustalW: ClustalW is a widely used system for aligning any number of homologous nucleotide or protein sequences. For multi-sequence alignments, ClustalW uses progressive alignment methods. It can create multiple alignments, optimize existing alignments, do profile analysis and create phylogenetic trees.

  26. What is the function of BLAST? How do blastx and tblastx work?
    Topic: BLAST and FASTA

    BLAST (Basic Local Alignment Search Tool) is an algorithm for comparing primary biological sequence information, such as the amino-acid sequences of proteins or the nucleotides of DNA and/or RNA sequences. It can search sequence databases and identify unknown sequences by comparing them to the known sequences, and helps in identifying the parent organism, function and evolutionary history. Blastx: searches a protein database using a translated nucleotide query. Tblastx: searches a translated nucleotide database using a translated nucleotide query.

  27. Write the steps to use FASTA and the steps of the FASTA algorithm.
    Topic: BLAST and FASTA

    Steps to use FASTA: Step 1: Specify the tool input (sequence and database). Step 2: Entering of input sequence. Step 3: Set up the parameters. Step 4: Submit the query for processing. FASTA can search sequence databases and identify unknown sequences by comparing them to the known sequence databases. This can help obtain information on the parent organism, function and evolutionary history.

  28. What is the difference between BLAST and FASTA?
    Topic: BLAST and FASTA

    BLAST is the most widely used tool for the local alignment of nucleotide and amino acid sequences. FASTA is a fine similarity searching tool which uses sequence patterns or words. BLAST performs searches by quick alignments and is faster, while FASTA uses the sequence patterns/words approach for similarity searching.

  29. Write the types of BLAST and their uses.
    Topic: BLAST and FASTA

    1. BLASTP: compares a protein query against a protein database. 2. BLASTN: compares a nucleotide query against a nucleotide database. 3. BLASTX: compares a translated nucleotide query against a protein database. 4. TBLASTN: compares a protein query against a translated nucleotide database. 5. TBLASTX: compares a translated nucleotide query against a translated nucleotide database. Multiple types of BLAST exist which assist in aligning nucleotide and amino acid (protein) sequences.

  30. Describe the types of phylogenetic trees. What is the difference between rooted and unrooted trees?
    Topic: Phylogenetics

    Rooted and unrooted trees can be used to show phylogenetic relationships between sequences. A rooted tree reflects the most basal ancestor of the tree in question; one of the nodes is stipulated to be the root, and thus the direction of ancestral relationships is determined. The root node is the ancestor of all other nodes, and the direction of evolution is from ancestor to the terminal nodes. An unrooted tree does not imply a known ancestral root. Unrooted trees represent the branching order / relatedness of organisms but do not indicate the root or location of the last common ancestor. Trees can also be scaled (branch lengths are equal to the magnitude of change) or unscaled (only representing the relationship between sequences).

  31. What are the ways of constructing a phylogenetic tree? Enumerate the clustering and objective based methods.
    Topic: Phylogenetics

    Several methods exist for constructing phylogenetic trees. Broadly, they belong to objective methods or clustering methods. Clustering methods (e.g. UPGMA — Unweighted Pair Group Method using Arithmetic averages) group sequences step by step on the basis of a distance matrix obtained by pairwise sequence alignment. Objective methods (e.g. least squares distance method, maximum parsimony and maximum likelihood) select the tree that best satisfies a defined criterion.

  32. Define mutation.
    Topic: Phylogenetics

    Mutations are: • Replacement of amino acids with certain other amino acids in proteins. • Replacement of nucleotides with certain other nucleotides in DNA/RNA. A mutation is the changing of the structure of a gene, resulting in a variant form which may be transmitted to subsequent generations, caused by the alteration of single base units in DNA, or the deletion, insertion, or rearrangement of larger sections of genes or chromosomes. Mutations are changes in the genetic sequence, and they are a main cause of diversity among organisms.

  33. Give an example of Newick notation.
    Topic: Phylogenetics

    Newick notation is a way of writing the branching pattern of a phylogenetic tree using nested parentheses, in which sequences grouped together share a common node. For example: ((A, B), (C, D)); represents a tree in which A and B form one group (clade) and C and D form another group, and the two groups join at the root. Branch lengths can also be written after the names, e.g. ((A:0.1, B:0.2):0.3, C:0.4);

  34. Write the types and functions of RNA.
    Topic: Genes, RNA and Protein Structure

    RNA can be divided into two categories: • Coding RNAs, which code for proteins. • Non-coding RNAs, which regulate/assist in the process of translation. Three major types of RNA are: 1. Messenger RNA (mRNA): carries the genetic information copied from DNA in the form of a series of three-base code “words”, each of which specifies a particular amino acid; these serve as temporary copies of the information found in DNA. 2. Transfer RNA (tRNA): is the key to converting the code words in mRNA, and ferries amino acids to the ribosome to be assembled. 3. Ribosomal RNA (rRNA): associates with a set of proteins to form ribosomes, the structural components of protein-making structures; these complex structures physically move along an mRNA molecule and catalyze the assembly of amino acids into protein chains. Other types include micro RNAs (miRNA), which function in RNA silencing and post-transcriptional regulation of gene expression, and small interfering RNA (siRNA), used for inducing short-term silencing of protein coding genes.

  35. What is the difference between RNA and DNA with reference to nucleotides?
    Topic: Genes, RNA and Protein Structure

    A nucleotide is composed of a nucleobase (nitrogenous base), a five-carbon sugar (either ribose or 2’-deoxyribose), and one to three phosphate groups. In DNA the sugar is 2’-deoxyribose, while in RNA the sugar is ribose. In DNA, the purine bases are adenine and guanine, while the pyrimidines are thymine and cytosine. RNA uses uracil in place of thymine.

  36. Why is RNA less stable than DNA? How does the hydroxyl group of RNA make it unstable?
    Topic: Genes, RNA and Protein Structure

    Unlike DNA, RNA in biological cells is predominantly a single-stranded molecule. While DNA contains deoxyribose, RNA contains ribose, characterised by the presence of the 2’-hydroxyl group on the pentose ring. This hydroxyl group makes RNA less stable than DNA because it is more susceptible to hydrolysis. RNA has a short life span and is more prone to degradation.

  37. What is the role of the 5’ cap and the 3’ poly-A tail?
    Topic: Genes, RNA and Protein Structure

    1. The 5’ cap helps identify mRNAs at the ribosomes. 2. The 5’ cap also acts as a shield against 5’ exonucleases, thus leading to an increase in mRNA stability. The 3’-end of mRNA has a poly-A tail (around 30-200 adenylate residues) which helps shield against 3’ exonucleases.

  38. What are purine and pyrimidine bases?
    Topic: Genes, RNA and Protein Structure

    Purines and pyrimidines are nitrogenous bases that make up the two different kinds of nucleotide bases in DNA and RNA. The two-carbon nitrogen ring bases (adenine and guanine) are purines, while the one-carbon nitrogen ring bases (thymine and cytosine, and uracil in RNA) are pyrimidines.

  39. What is the difference between acidic and basic amino acids?
    Topic: Genes, RNA and Protein Structure

    Acidic amino acids have acidic side chains at neutral pH, while basic amino acids have basic side chains at neutral pH. A carboxylic acid is the side chain for acidic amino acids, and basic amino acids contain nitrogen containing groups. Lysine, arginine and histidine are basic amino acids.

  40. Name any three hydrophobic amino acids.
    Topic: Genes, RNA and Protein Structure

    Hydrophobic amino acids include glycine (Gly), alanine (Ala), valine (Val), leucine (Leu), isoleucine (Ile), proline (Pro), phenylalanine (Phe), methionine (Met) and tryptophan (Trp). Any three of these, e.g. alanine, valine and leucine.

  41. If pH is less than pKa, what happens?
    Topic: Genes, RNA and Protein Structure

    If the pH is less than the pKa, then the acid (protonated) form of the compound predominates. If the pH is greater than the pKa, then the conjugate base (deprotonated) form predominates.

  42. What is a conserved sequence?
    Topic: Genes, RNA and Protein Structure

    A conserved sequence is a base sequence in a DNA molecule (or an amino acid sequence in a protein) that has remained essentially unchanged, and so has been conserved, throughout evolution.

  43. What is protein folding? Also write about the Chou-Fasman algorithm.
    Topic: Genes, RNA and Protein Structure

    Protein folding is the physical process by which a protein chain acquires its native 3-dimensional structure, a conformation that is usually biologically functional, in an expeditious and reproducible manner. The Chou–Fasman method is an empirical technique for the prediction of secondary structures in proteins, originally developed in the 1970s by Peter Y. Chou and Gerald D. Fasman.

  44. What do you know about the Ramachandran plot?
    Topic: Genes, RNA and Protein Structure

    A Ramachandran plot is a way to visualize dihedral angles ψ (psi) against φ (phi) of amino acid residues in protein structure. Ramachandran recognized that many combinations of angles in a polypeptide chain are forbidden because of steric collisions between atoms, so only certain regions of the plot are allowed for a folded protein.

  45. What is a protein domain? Give examples of protein domains.
    Topic: Genes, RNA and Protein Structure

    Domains are semi-independent functional structures in a protein. A protein domain is a conserved part of a given protein sequence and (tertiary) structure that can evolve, function, and exist independently of the rest of the protein chain. Each domain forms a compact three-dimensional structure and often can be independently stable and folded. A protein may contain multiple domains, hence we can try to classify proteins by their domains. Examples of protein domains: 1. Alpha Domains 2. Beta Domains 3. Alpha/Beta Domains 4. Alpha + Beta Domains 5. Alpha & Beta Multi-Domains 6. Membrane & cell-surface proteins

  46. What is the purpose of the Bowie algorithm?
    Topic: Genes, RNA and Protein Structure

    The Bowie algorithm (the 3D-1D profile method of Bowie, Lüthy and Eisenberg) is used for fold recognition / threading in protein structure prediction. It converts the 3D environment of each position in a protein structure into a 1D profile (string of environment classes), and a query sequence is then aligned/scored against these 3D-1D profiles to detect compatibility of the sequence with known folds, even when sequence similarity is very low.

  47. Complete the protein sequence given by the PROSITE pattern F-x(3)-X-R-F-K-X(4-5)-D-E-R.
    Topic: Genes, RNA and Protein Structure

    In a PROSITE pattern, x represents any amino acid and the bracketed number is the repeat length of that residue, while elements of the pattern are separated by hyphens. So x(3) gives three amino acids and X(4-5) gives four to five amino acids. F-x(3)-X-R-F-K-X(4-5)-D-E-R completes to: FXXXXRFKXXXXDER (with 4 residues) or FXXXXRFKXXXXXDER (with 5 residues).

  48. How are bulges formed?
    Topic: RNA Structure Prediction

    Bulges are formed when a double-stranded region cannot form base pairs perfectly. Bulges can be asymmetric, with varying number of base pairs on one side of the loop.

  49. What is Gibbs free energy? What role does Gibbs energy play in RNA folding?
    Topic: RNA Structure Prediction

    Gibbs free energy is a thermodynamic quantity equal to the enthalpy (of a system or process) minus the product of the entropy and the absolute temperature. It is the free energy of an RNA molecule available for reaction — the smaller, the better. RNA structure formation lowers the free energy, so the most stable RNA secondary structure is the one with the minimum (lowest) Gibbs free energy.

  50. How do pseudoknots affect/alter RNA structure?
    Topic: RNA Structure Prediction

    A 1’ (primary) RNA structure can fold into a 2’ (secondary) structure; 2’ structures can then form 3’ (tertiary) structures but avoid pseudoknots in simple prediction algorithms. A pseudoknot is a nucleic acid secondary structure containing at least two stem-loop structures in which half of one stem is intercalated between the two halves of another stem. Pseudoknots fold into knot-shaped three-dimensional conformations (but are not true topological knots) and are potentially important tertiary structural motifs of RNA. A simple graph-based approach, the “Circular Plot”, can help us detect the pseudoknots, which appear as intersecting arcs.

  51. How many scoring positions are considered in the Nussinov and Jacobson algorithm?
    Topic: RNA Structure Prediction

    4 positions are considered to calculate the Nussinov-Jacobson scoring matrix. At every step, we can see score contributions from the 4 possible locations (the cell below, the cell to the left, the diagonal cell with a pairing bonus, and the maximum over intermediate positions).

  52. What is the Wobble hypothesis?
    Topic: RNA Structure Prediction

    The Wobble Hypothesis explains why multiple codons can code for a single amino acid. One tRNA molecule (with one amino acid attached) can recognise and bind to more than one codon, due to the less precise base pairs that can arise between the 3rd base of the codon and the base at the 1st position on the anticodon.

  53. Enlist the salient features of the block diagram of a mass spectrometer.
    Topic: Proteomics and Mass Spectrometry

    A mass spectrum is a plot of the ion signal as a function of the mass-to-charge ratio. These spectra are used to determine the elemental or isotopic signature of a sample, the masses of particles and of molecules, and to elucidate the chemical structures of molecules and other chemical compounds. The block diagram of a mass spectrometer consists of: a sample inlet, an ionization source (e.g. MALDI or ESI) which ionizes the protein/peptide molecules, a mass analyzer which separates the ions according to their mass-to-charge ratio, a detector which measures the ions, and a data system which records the mass spectrum.

  54. What is protein sequencing? Name the database used for protein structures.
    Topic: Proteomics and Mass Spectrometry

    Protein sequencing is the practical process of determining the amino acid sequence of all or part of a protein or peptide. The Protein Data Bank (PDB) is a database for the three-dimensional structural data of large biological molecules, such as proteins and nucleic acids. The PDB is overseen by an organization called the Worldwide Protein Data Bank, wwPDB.

  55. What does a UniProt sequence give information about a protein query?
    Topic: Proteomics and Mass Spectrometry

    A UniProt entry gives information about the protein query such as its sequence, molecular mass, function, authors and journal references (locus, accession number, sequence, molecular mass, authors, journal etc.), because UniProt is a freely accessible database of protein sequence and functional information containing a large amount of information about the biological function of proteins derived from the research literature.
