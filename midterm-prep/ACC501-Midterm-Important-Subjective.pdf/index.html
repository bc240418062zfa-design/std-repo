  7. Define finance and explain the three major decisions taken by a financial manager.
    Topic: Introduction to Finance & Business Organizations • 3 marks marks

    Finance is the study of how individuals and businesses acquire, spend and manage money and other financial assets - i.e. how funds are raised and used. The three major decisions of financial management are: 1. Investment (capital budgeting) decision - deciding which long-term assets/projects the firm should invest in. 2. Financing (capital structure) decision - deciding how to raise the required funds, i.e. the mix of debt and equity. 3. Dividend decision - deciding how much of the profit to distribute to shareholders and how much to retain in the business.

  8. Compare the three forms of business organization: sole proprietorship, partnership and corporation.
    Topic: Introduction to Finance & Business Organizations • 3 marks marks

    Sole proprietorship: owned by one person; easiest and cheapest to form; the owner has unlimited liability and keeps all profits, but the life of the business is limited to the owner's life and raising capital is difficult. Partnership: owned by two or more persons; partners share gains and losses and (in a general partnership) all partners have unlimited liability for business debts; easier to raise funds than a sole proprietorship. Corporation: a legal entity separate from its owners; owners (shareholders) have limited liability, ownership is easily transferable through shares, and it can raise large amounts of capital, but it is the most expensive and complicated to form and is subject to double taxation of dividends.

  9. Why is maximization of shareholders' wealth considered a better goal of financial management than profit maximization?
    Topic: Introduction to Finance & Business Organizations • 2 marks marks

    Profit maximization is a vague and short-term goal: it ignores the timing of returns (time value of money), ignores risk, and 'profit' itself is ambiguous (which year's profit, before or after tax?). Shareholder wealth maximization means maximizing the market price of the firm's shares, which reflects the magnitude, timing and risk of expected future cash flows, so it is a precise, long-run goal consistent with the owners' interest.

  10. What is the agency problem? Give two ways in which it can be reduced.
    Topic: Introduction to Finance & Business Organizations • 2 marks marks

    The agency problem arises from the separation of ownership and management in a corporation: managers (agents) may pursue their own interests (e.g. job security, perks, empire building) instead of maximizing the wealth of the shareholders (principals) who own the firm. It can be reduced by: (1) linking managerial compensation to performance, e.g. bonuses and stock options based on share price; and (2) monitoring and control mechanisms, e.g. audits, boards of directors, and the threat of takeover if the share price falls too low.

  11. Differentiate between the balance sheet and the income statement.
    Topic: Financial Statements & Cash Flow • 3 marks marks

    The balance sheet is a snapshot of the firm's financial position at a particular date: it shows what the firm owns (assets) and what it owes (liabilities and shareholders' equity), following the identity Assets = Liabilities + Shareholders' Equity. The income statement measures the firm's performance over a period of time: it shows revenues earned and expenses incurred during the period, and the resulting net income (or loss). In short, the balance sheet is a picture at a point in time, while the income statement is a record over a period of time.

  13. Explain 'sources of cash' and 'uses of cash' with two examples of each.
    Topic: Financial Statements & Cash Flow • 3 marks marks

    A source of cash is any activity that brings cash into the firm (increases cash), while a use of cash is any activity on which cash is spent (decreases cash). Sources of cash: an increase in a liability or equity account (e.g. issuing bonds or shares, taking a bank loan), and a decrease in an asset account (e.g. selling inventory or collecting receivables). Uses of cash: a decrease in a liability or equity account (e.g. repaying a loan, paying dividends), and an increase in an asset account (e.g. purchasing fixed assets or inventory).

  16. Name the five categories of financial ratios and state what each category measures.
    Topic: Ratio Analysis & Financial Planning • 3 marks marks

    1. Liquidity ratios - measure the firm's ability to meet its short-term obligations (e.g. current ratio, quick ratio). 2. Leverage (debt management) ratios - measure the extent to which the firm is financed by debt (e.g. debt ratio, debt-equity ratio). 3. Activity (asset management) ratios - measure how efficiently the firm uses its assets (e.g. inventory turnover, receivables turnover, total asset turnover). 4. Profitability ratios - measure the firm's overall earning performance (e.g. profit margin, ROA, ROE). 5. Market value ratios - relate the firm's stock price to its earnings and book value (e.g. price-earnings ratio).

  19. A firm has an ROE of 15% and pays out 60% of its earnings as dividends. Calculate its sustainable growth rate.
    Topic: Ratio Analysis & Financial Planning • 3 marks marks

  20. What is the percentage-of-sales approach to financial planning? What does EFN mean in this approach?
    Topic: Ratio Analysis & Financial Planning • 3 marks marks

    In the percentage-of-sales approach, the items of the pro forma income statement and balance sheet that vary directly with sales are expressed as fixed percentages of sales. Sales are first projected, the pro forma statements are prepared on that basis, and the balancing figure shows how much external financing the firm will need. EFN (External Financing Needed) is the amount of funds the firm must raise from outside sources (debt or equity) because the projected increase in assets exceeds the financing provided by spontaneous liabilities and retained earnings.

  25. Differentiate between simple interest and compound interest. Support your answer by calculating the amount on Rs. 10,000 invested for 3 years at 10% under each method.
    Topic: Time Value of Money • 2 marks marks

    Under simple interest, interest is earned only on the original principal. Under compound interest, interest is earned on the principal as well as on all previously earned interest ('interest on interest'). Simple interest amount = 10,000 x (1 + 0.10 x 3) = Rs. 13,000. Compound amount = 10,000 x (1.10)^3 = Rs. 13,310. The difference of Rs. 310 is the interest earned on interest under compounding.

  26. A bank quotes a nominal interest rate of 12% compounded quarterly. Calculate the effective annual rate (EAR).
    Topic: Time Value of Money • 3 marks marks

    EAR = (1 + Quoted rate / m)^m - 1, where m = number of compounding periods per year = 4 EAR = (1 + 0.12 / 4)^4 - 1 = (1.03)^4 - 1 = 1.1255 - 1 = 0.1255, i.e. 12.55%. The EAR is higher than the nominal rate because of compounding within the year.

  27. A bond has a face value of Rs. 1,000, a coupon rate of 10% (paid annually) and 5 years to maturity. If the market required rate of return (yield to maturity) is 12%, calculate the price of the bond. Is it trading at a premium or a discount?

  28. State the relationship between a bond's coupon rate and its yield to maturity (YTM) when the bond sells at (a) par, (b) a premium and (c) a discount.
    Topic: Bond Valuation & Interest Rates • 3 marks marks

    (a) At par (price = face value): coupon rate = YTM. (b) At a premium (price > face value): coupon rate > YTM - the bond pays more interest than the market requires, so investors pay extra for it. (c) At a discount (price < face value): coupon rate < YTM - the bond pays less interest than the market requires, so it sells for less than face value.

  29. Define current yield and yield to maturity (YTM). Calculate the current yield of a bond with an annual coupon of Rs. 100 selling for Rs. 927.90.
    Topic: Bond Valuation & Interest Rates • 2 marks marks

    Current yield is the annual coupon payment divided by the current market price of the bond; it measures only the interest return. YTM is the total rate of return earned if the bond is held until maturity - it considers the coupon payments as well as the difference between the purchase price and the face value received at maturity. Current Yield = 100 / 927.90 = 0.1078, i.e. approximately 10.78%.

  30. What is interest-rate (price) risk in bonds? Which bonds carry more of it, and why?
    Topic: Bond Valuation & Interest Rates • 2 marks marks

    Interest-rate risk is the risk that a bond's price will fall when market interest rates rise (bond prices and interest rates move in opposite directions). It is greater for bonds with (1) a longer time to maturity, because their cash flows are exposed to the new rate for longer, and (2) a lower coupon rate, because a larger share of the bond's value comes from the distant face-value payment.

  31. What is the term structure of interest rates? Name the three shapes of the yield curve.
    Topic: Bond Valuation & Interest Rates • 2 marks marks

    The term structure of interest rates is the relationship between interest rates (yields) and the time to maturity for bonds of similar quality at a given point in time. It is shown graphically by the yield curve. The three shapes are: (1) upward sloping (long-term rates higher than short-term rates - the normal shape), (2) downward sloping / inverted (short-term rates higher than long-term rates), and (3) flat (rates almost the same for all maturities).

  32. A corporation paid a dividend of Rs. 14 per share last year. Its dividends are expected to grow at 7% per year indefinitely, and investors require a 17% return on similar stocks. Calculate the price per share using the dividend growth model.
    Topic: Stock Valuation & Dividends • 5 marks marks

    Expected dividend next year: D1 = D0 x (1 + g) = 14 x 1.07 = Rs. 14.98 Price = D1 / (r - g) = 14.98 / (0.17 - 0.07) = 14.98 / 0.10 = Rs. 149.80 per share.

  33. A corporation's stock is selling for Rs. 150 per share. The next dividend is expected to be Rs. 35 per share and is expected to grow at 14% indefinitely. What required rate of return does this stock offer?
    Topic: Stock Valuation & Dividends • 3 marks marks

    From the dividend growth model: r = (D1 / P0) + g = (35 / 150) + 0.14 = 0.2333 + 0.14 = 0.3733, i.e. approximately 37%.

  34. Compare the zero-growth and constant-growth dividend models, and state the condition necessary for the constant-growth model to give a meaningful answer.
    Topic: Stock Valuation & Dividends • 3 marks marks

    Zero-growth model: the dividend is constant forever (D1 = D2 = ... = D), so the share is valued as a perpetuity: P0 = D / r. It suits preferred stock and mature firms with fixed dividends. Constant-growth model (Gordon model): dividends grow at a constant rate g forever: P0 = D1 / (r - g). Necessary condition: the required return must be greater than the growth rate (r > g). If g >= r, the denominator becomes zero or negative and the formula gives a meaningless (infinite or negative) price.

  35. A corporation has 3 shareholders: Mr. Salman with 25 shares, Mr. Kareem with 35 shares and Mr. Amjad with 40 shares. Six directors are to be elected. Under the cumulative voting rule, how many votes can Mr. Kareem cast for a single director? How does this differ from straight voting?
    Topic: Stock Valuation & Dividends • 2 marks marks

    Under cumulative voting, each shareholder gets votes equal to shares owned x number of directors to be elected, and may cast all of them for one candidate. Mr. Kareem's votes = 35 x 6 = 210 votes, which he can give entirely to one director. Under straight voting, he would have only 35 votes for each of the six seats separately, so a majority shareholder can elect the entire board; cumulative voting helps minority shareholders win at least one seat.

  36. An investment has the following possible returns: in a boom (probability 0.30) it returns 20%; in a normal economy (probability 0.50) it returns 10%; in a recession (probability 0.20) it returns -5%. Calculate the expected return.

  37. For the investment in the previous question (expected return 10%; returns 20%, 10% and -5% with probabilities 0.30, 0.50 and 0.20), calculate the variance and standard deviation of the returns.
    Topic: Risk, Return & Portfolio Theory • 5 marks marks

    Variance = Sum of [Probability x (Return - Expected Return)^2] = 0.30 x (20 - 10)^2 + 0.50 x (10 - 10)^2 + 0.20 x (-5 - 10)^2 = 0.30 x 100 + 0 + 0.20 x 225 = 30 + 45 = 75 (% squared) Standard Deviation = sqrt(75) = 8.66% (approximately).   38. The risk-free rate is 8% and the expected return on the market is 14%. Using the Capital Asset Pricing Model (CAPM), calculate the required return of a stock with a beta of 1.5, and interpret the beta value.
    Topic: Risk, Return & Portfolio Theory

    CAPM: Required Return = Rf + Beta x (Market Return - Rf) = 8% + 1.5 x (14% - 8%) = 8% + 1.5 x 6% = 8% + 9% = 17%. A beta of 1.5 means the stock is 1.5 times as volatile as the market: if the market moves by 1%, the stock is expected to move by about 1.5% in the same direction, so it is riskier than the average stock and must offer a higher return.

  39. Differentiate between systematic and unsystematic risk. Which of the two can be eliminated by diversification?
    Topic: Risk, Return & Portfolio Theory • 2 marks marks

    Systematic (market) risk affects all firms and securities at the same time - e.g. changes in interest rates, inflation and economic recession. It cannot be avoided by diversification and is measured by beta. Unsystematic (firm-specific) risk affects a single firm or industry - e.g. a strike, loss of a major customer, or a product failure. It CAN be eliminated by holding a diversified portfolio, because the bad performance of one firm is offset by the good performance of others.

  40. A corporation's target capital structure is 40% debt and 60% equity. Its pre-tax cost of debt is 9%, the tax rate is 40%, and its cost of equity is 14%. Calculate the weighted average cost of capital (WACC).

  41. A firm's stock is selling for Rs. 50 per share. It just paid a dividend of Rs. 5 per share, and dividends are expected to grow at 5% per year indefinitely. Calculate the firm's cost of equity using the dividend growth model.

  42. Pearson Corporation has issued Rs. 2,000,000 worth of bonds at 6.5% per annum. The tax rate is 40%. Calculate the annual interest tax shield and the present value of the (permanent) tax shield.

  43. Define financial leverage and homemade leverage.
    Topic: Cost of Capital & Capital Structure • 2 marks marks

    Financial leverage is the use of fixed-cost sources of financing - debt and preferred stock - in a firm's capital structure to magnify the returns (and the risk) to common shareholders: a given change in EBIT produces a larger change in earnings per share. Homemade leverage is the use of personal borrowing by an individual investor to change the amount of financial leverage in his own portfolio - by borrowing personally, an investor can replicate the leverage of a firm, which is why (in perfect markets) a firm's leverage policy alone cannot create value for investors.

  44. Define the operating cycle and the cash cycle of a firm, and write the relationship between them.
    Topic: Working Capital Management • 3 marks marks

    The operating cycle is the time between acquiring inventory and receiving cash from its sale: Operating Cycle = Inventory Period + Accounts Receivable Period. The cash cycle is the time between paying cash for inventory and receiving cash from customers: Cash Cycle = Operating Cycle - Accounts Payable Period. The cash cycle measures how long the firm's own cash is tied up in its operations and must be financed.

  45. A firm has a cash cycle of 100 days. Its inventory turnover is 5 and its receivables turnover is 2. Calculate the firm's accounts payable turnover.

  46. Mr. Munir purchased goods of Rs. 100,000 on 1 June from Zeeshan & Brothers on credit terms of 3/10, net 30. (a) How much will he pay if he pays on 9 June? (b) What is the approximate annual cost of forgoing (not taking) the cash discount?

(b) Cost of forgoing the discount = [Discount% / (100 - Discount%)] x [365 / (Credit period - Discount period)] = (3 / 97) x (365 / 20) = 0.0309 x 18.25 = 0.5644, i.e. approximately 56.4% per year - so taking the discount is very worthwhile.

  47. What is the hedging (matching) approach to financing working capital?
    Topic: Working Capital Management • 2 marks marks

    Under the hedging (matching) approach, the maturity of the financing is matched with the maturity of the assets being financed: temporary (seasonal) current assets are financed with short-term borrowing, while permanent current assets and fixed assets are financed with long-term debt and equity. This balances risk and cost - the firm avoids both the high risk of financing long-lived assets with short-term debt and the unnecessary cost of long-term financing for temporary needs.

  48. Mehran Corporation expects a project to generate cash flows of Rs. 5,000,000 in year 1, Rs. 7,000,000 in year 2 and Rs. 8,000,000 in year 3. The project costs Rs. 10,000,000. Calculate the net present value (NPV) at a discount rate of 25% and state whether the project should be accepted.
    Topic: Capital Budgeting & Project Cash Flows • 5 marks marks

    PV of cash flows = 5,000,000/1.25 + 7,000,000/(1.25)^2 + 8,000,000/(1.25)^3 = 4,000,000 + 4,480,000 + 4,096,000 = Rs. 12,576,000 NPV = 12,576,000 - 10,000,000 = Rs. 2,576,000. Since NPV is positive, the project earns more than the required 25% return and should be ACCEPTED.

  49. An investment costs Rs. 1,500,000 and is expected to generate cash flows of Rs. 500,000 in year 1, Rs. 800,000 in year 2 and Rs. 600,000 in year 3. Calculate its payback period.
    Topic: Capital Budgeting & Project Cash Flows • 3 marks marks

    Cumulative cash flows: Year 1 = 500,000; Year 2 = 500,000 + 800,000 = 1,300,000; Year 3 = 1,900,000. The cost of 1,500,000 is recovered during year 3. Amount still needed after year 2 = 1,500,000 - 1,300,000 = 200,000. Payback Period = 2 + (200,000 / 600,000) = 2 + 0.33 = 2.33 years (approximately).

  50. The present value of a project's future cash flows is Rs. 450,000 and the project costs Rs. 300,000. Calculate the profitability index (PI) and state the PI decision rule.
    Topic: Capital Budgeting & Project Cash Flows • 3 marks marks

    Profitability Index = PV of future cash flows / Initial cost = 450,000 / 300,000 = 1.50. Decision rule: accept the project if PI is greater than 1 (each rupee invested returns more than one rupee in present-value terms), and reject it if PI is less than 1. Here PI = 1.50 > 1, so the project should be accepted.

  51. Compare the NPV and IRR methods of capital budgeting. Which one is preferred, and why?
    Topic: Capital Budgeting & Project Cash Flows • 3 marks marks

    NPV measures the absolute increase in firm value from a project in rupees; the rule is to accept if NPV > 0. IRR is the discount rate at which NPV = 0; the rule is to accept if IRR exceeds the required return. Both give the same decision for independent projects with conventional cash flows. NPV is preferred because: (1) it directly measures the addition to shareholders' wealth, which is the goal of the firm; (2) IRR can give multiple answers when cash flows change sign more than once; (3) for mutually exclusive projects, IRR can rank projects wrongly because it ignores the scale of investment and assumes reinvestment at the IRR itself, whereas NPV assumes reinvestment at the required return.

  52. Differentiate between a sunk cost and an opportunity cost, giving one example of each. Which one is relevant in capital budgeting?
    Topic: Capital Budgeting & Project Cash Flows • 2 marks marks

    A sunk cost has already been incurred and cannot be recovered - e.g. a fee already paid to a consultant for preparing a project's feasibility report. An opportunity cost is the benefit forgone by choosing one alternative over another - e.g. using a building the firm already owns for a new project instead of renting it out. In capital budgeting, sunk costs are NOT relevant and are excluded from project cash flows, while opportunity costs ARE relevant and must be included.

  53. Why is finance important for a business firm?
    Topic: Introduction to Finance & Business Organizations • 5 marks marks

  55. What is the difference between a dealer and a broker in financial markets?

  60. Saratoga Farms has sales of $2,000,000, net income of $200,000 and total assets of $1,000,000. Debt is 60% of total assets. Using the DuPont identity, find the return on equity. What would the ROE be if debt were only 20% of total assets?
    Topic: Ratio Analysis & Financial Planning • 10 marks marks

    Profit margin = Net income / Sales = 200,000 / 2,000,000 = 10%. Asset turnover = Sales / Total assets = 2,000,000 / 1,000,000 = 2 times. With debt at 60% of assets, equity is 40% of assets, so the equity multiplier = Assets / Equity = 1 / 0.40 = 2.5. ROE = Profit margin × Asset turnover × Equity multiplier = 10% × 2 × 2.5 = 50%. If debt were 20% of assets, equity would be 80% of assets and the equity multiplier = 1 / 0.80 = 1.25. ROE = 10% × 2 × 1.25 = 25%. Greater financial leverage raises the ROE (when the return earned on assets exceeds the cost of debt).

  63. What is benchmarking? Briefly describe the two ways in which benchmarks can be chosen.
    Topic: Ratio Analysis & Financial Planning • 5 marks marks

    Benchmarking is a comparison of a firm's performance, ratios or practices against a standard (benchmark) in order to judge how well the firm is doing and where it can improve.   64. Why is the present value of an ordinary annuity less than the present value of an annuity due, when the payments, interest rate and term are the same?
    Topic: Time Value of Money • 3 marks marks

    In an ordinary annuity the payments are made at the END of each period, while in an annuity due the payments are made at the BEGINNING of each period. Because every payment of an annuity due is received one period earlier, each payment is discounted for one period less, so its present value is higher. In fact, PV of annuity due = PV of ordinary annuity × (1 + r). Hence the present value of an ordinary annuity is less than that of an annuity due.

  67. What does a bond indenture contain? List its main contents.
    Topic: Bond Valuation & Interest Rates • 3 marks marks

    The indenture is the written agreement between the corporation (borrower) and its creditors (bondholders). It normally includes: • The basic terms of the bonds (face value, coupon rate, maturity date). • A description of the property used as security, if any. • The seniority of the bonds and the repayment arrangements (e.g., a sinking fund). • Call provisions, if any. • Protective covenants — things the firm promises to do or not to do.

  68. How is the yield to maturity (YTM) of a bond calculated?
    Topic: Bond Valuation & Interest Rates • 3 marks marks

    The YTM is the discount rate that makes the present value of the bond's future cash flows (coupon payments plus face value) equal to its current market price. Because the bond pricing equation cannot be solved directly for this rate, the YTM is found by trial and error: different discount rates are tried until the calculated price matches the market price (financial calculators and spreadsheets use the same idea). If the bond sells at par, the YTM simply equals the coupon rate; at a discount the YTM is higher than the coupon rate, and at a premium it is lower.

  69. What are protective covenants? How are they classified?
    Topic: Bond Valuation & Interest Rates • 5 marks marks

    Protective covenants are provisions in the bond indenture that protect the interests of the bondholders by restricting the actions of the borrowing firm. They are classified into two types: 1. Negative covenants — things the firm promises NOT to do, e.g., not to pledge assets to other lenders, not to sell major assets, not to issue additional long-term debt, and not to pay dividends beyond a stated limit. 2. Positive covenants — things the firm promises TO do, e.g., to maintain working capital at or above a minimum level, to furnish audited financial statements to lenders, and to maintain the firm's assets in good condition.

  70. What is the Fisher effect? Write down its equation.
    Topic: Bond Valuation & Interest Rates • 3 marks marks

    The Fisher effect describes the relationship between nominal interest rates, real interest rates and inflation. It states that the nominal rate of return adjusts for expected inflation so that investors earn the required real return. Equation: 1 + R = (1 + r) × (1 + h), where R = nominal rate, r = real rate and h = inflation rate. Approximately, R approximately r + h.

  71. Calculate the approximate and the exact real rate of return in each case: (a) Nominal rate = 15%, inflation rate = 6%. (b) Nominal rate = 12%, inflation rate = 5%.
    Topic: Bond Valuation & Interest Rates • 5 marks marks

    Approximate real rate = Nominal rate - Inflation rate. (a) Approximate = 15% - 6% = 9%. Exact (Fisher effect): 1 + r = (1 + R) / (1 + h) = 1.15 / 1.06 = 1.0849, so r = 8.49%. (b) Approximate = 12% - 5% = 7%. Exact: 1 + r = 1.12 / 1.05 = 1.0667, so r = 6.67%.

  72. Name and briefly explain the three determinants (effects) that form the term structure of interest rates.
    Topic: Bond Valuation & Interest Rates • 3 marks marks

    1. Real rate of interest — the compensation investors demand for forgoing the use of their money, before any risk or inflation adjustments. 2. Inflation premium — the compensation for the expected loss of purchasing power due to future inflation; it reflects expected inflation over the life of the security. 3. Interest rate risk premium — the compensation for bearing the risk that interest rate changes will reduce the value of longer-term securities; it increases with the term to maturity.

  76. What are the two components of the total return on a stock investment in the dividend growth model?
    Topic: Stock Valuation & Dividends • 3 marks marks

    The total return (R) on a stock has two components: 1. Dividend yield = D1 / P0 — the income component, the expected cash dividend divided by the current price. 2.   78. How is the overall cost of capital of a firm calculated?
    Topic: Cost of Capital & Capital Structure • 3 marks marks

    The overall cost of capital is the weighted average cost of capital (WACC). The cost of each source of funds — debt, preferred stock and common equity — is calculated separately, and each is then weighted by its proportion in the firm's target capital structure: WACC = (E/V) × RE + (D/V) × RD × (1 - TC), where E and D are the market values of equity and debt, V = E + D, RE is the cost of equity, RD is the cost of debt and TC is the corporate tax rate. The cost of debt is taken after tax because interest is tax-deductible.
