  1. Define international marketing. How does international marketing differ from domestic marketing?
    Topic: Overview of International Marketing

    International marketing refers to exchanges across national boundaries for the satisfaction of human needs and wants. The basic nature of marketing does not change from domestic to international marketing, but marketing outside national boundaries poses special problems. An international marketer has to operate simultaneously in more than one kind of environment, manage operations in distant markets, optimize business in more than one country, deal with foreign nationals, coordinate these operations, and use the experience gained in one country for making decisions in another country. The demands are tough and the stakes are high, so the marketer must be sensitive to different marketing environments internationally and must balance marketing moves worldwide to seek optimum results for the company.

  2. Global marketing passed through different evolutionary changes. Describe all the steps (phases) in detail.
    Topic: Overview of International Marketing

    Depending on their level of involvement in foreign markets, firms pass through five evolutionary phases: 1. Domestic marketing: Domestic marketers tend to be ethnocentric, focus solely on the domestic market and pay little attention to changes in the global marketplace. They produce and sell only in their home country, and may be vulnerable to sudden changes forced on them by foreign competition. 2. Export marketing: Exporting firms fulfill unsolicited or solicited orders from foreign countries. They face difficulties in import/export restrictions, cost and availability of shipping, exchange rate fluctuations, collection of money and development of distribution channels. Export marketers still tend to take an ethnocentric approach, mostly making products in their home countries with no direct involvement in foreign markets.

  3. International marketing: An international marketing firm has a polycentric orientation with emphasis on product and promotional adaptation in foreign markets whenever necessary. Strategic decisions are tailored to suit the cultures of the foreign countries, and the company may establish an independent foreign subsidiary in each foreign market it services (also called multi-domestic marketing).

  6. What is globalization? Discuss the market and cost drivers of globalization.
    Topic: Overview of International Marketing

    Globalization is a process which embodies a transformation in the spatial organization of social relations and transactions, generating transcontinental or interregional flows and networks of activity, interaction and the exercise of power. Globalization represents a convergence of tastes and increasing homogeneity, integration of purchasing and manufacturing processes on a global scale to achieve cost efficiencies, industries dominated by a few major players worldwide, and large organizations with global cultures and mindsets. Market globalization drivers depend on customer behavior and the structure of distribution channels. They include: (i) common customer needs, affected by economic development, climate, physical environment and culture; (ii) global customers and channels, who buy on a centralized or coordinated basis for decentralized use; (iii) transferable marketing, as elements of the marketing mix such as brand name and pricing strategy may be transferable across markets; and (iv) lead countries, where innovations in particular industries are prone to take place, e.g. Japan for consumer electronics, Germany for industrial control equipment and the United States for computer software. Cost globalization drivers depend on the economics of the business and include: global economies of scale and scope, a steep experience curve, global sourcing efficiencies, favorable logistics, differences in country costs (factor costs and exchange rates), high product development costs, and fast-changing technology.

  7. Describe the five steps of the international marketing process.
    Topic: International Marketing Process and Company Orientations

    The international marketing process comprises five steps: 1. Analyzing international marketing opportunities to identify unfulfilled or under-fulfilled needs that a marketer may satisfy through its products or services. This analysis can be done through information seeking and analysis or through market research (secondary or primary data collection and analysis). 2. Selecting the target markets: choosing the groups of potential international customers to whom to sell. This step involves identifying potential buyers, demand measurement and forecasting, market segmentation, market targeting and market positioning, and deciding the key benefits to offer and the aspects on which to differentiate from the competition. 3. Adopting appropriate business and marketing strategies so that the firm offers the best value to customers and aligns, coordinates and optimizes all its activities and resources (e.g. Porter's cost leadership, differentiation or focus strategies). 4. Developing the international marketing mix: product, place, price and promotion. The firm develops appropriate programs in these four key areas and ensures they are well coordinated and in conformity with each other to give a clear image to the target market.

  9. Distinguish between needs, wants and demands.
    Topic: International Marketing Process and Company Orientations

    Need: A need arises with a state of felt deprivation, when the situation of an individual, a group or a business is less than the desired situation and there is an urge to achieve the desired situation. Needs are a basic part of human make-up and can be physical (food, clothing, warmth and safety), social (belonging, affection) or individual (knowledge, self-expression). Wants: Once needs are felt, humans and businesses look for solutions. Wants are the manifested solutions of needs; they are the forms taken by human needs, shaped by culture, individual personality, etc. Demands: Human wants backed by buying power and choices translate into demands, i.e. what is chosen as the desired solution from among the various available and viable options.

  10. What is 'value' in marketing? In which ways can the value of a product be enhanced?
    Topic: International Marketing Process and Company Orientations

    Value refers to the perceived net benefits one gets from acquiring or owning a certain product. It is the difference between the values the customer gains from owning and using a product and the cost/effort of obtaining the product. Value is usually perceived rather than objective; buyers consider functional as well as emotional benefits, while costs include monetary, time, energy and psychic costs. Value can be enhanced by: - Raising benefits for the same costs - Reducing costs for the same benefits - Raising benefits by more than the raise in costs - Lowering benefits by less than the reduction in costs

  11. Explain the production concept, the product concept and the selling concept of company orientation.
    Topic: International Marketing Process and Company Orientations

    The production concept is the oldest of the concepts in business. It holds that consumers will prefer products that are widely available and inexpensive. Managers focusing on this concept concentrate on achieving high production efficiency, low costs and mass distribution, assuming consumers are primarily interested in product availability and low prices. This orientation makes sense in developing countries. The product concept holds that consumers will favor those products that offer the most quality, performance or innovative features. Managers concentrate on making superior products and improving them over time. However, such managers are sometimes caught up in a love affair with their product and may commit the 'better-mousetrap' fallacy, believing a better mousetrap will lead people to beat a path to their door, without realizing what the market needs. The selling concept holds that consumers and businesses, if left alone, will ordinarily not buy enough of the selling company's products, so the organization must undertake an aggressive selling and promotion effort. It assumes consumers typically show buying inertia or resistance and must be coaxed into buying. Most firms practice it when they have overcapacity; their aim is to sell what they make rather than make what the market wants.

  12. Explain the marketing concept and its four pillars. How does it differ from the selling (sales) concept?
    Topic: International Marketing Process and Company Orientations

    The marketing concept holds that the key to achieving organizational goals consists of the company being more effective than competitors in creating, delivering and communicating customer value to its selected target customers. It rests on four pillars: (1) target market (the potential customers), (2) customer needs, (3) integrated marketing (approaching customers through product offers, marketing communication, distribution and pricing), and (4) profitability. Difference from the selling concept: the sales concept focuses on the needs of the seller, while the marketing concept focuses on the needs of the buyer. The sales concept is preoccupied with the seller's need to convert his product into cash, whereas the marketing concept is preoccupied with satisfying the needs of the customer by means of the product as a solution to the customer's problem.

  13. What is the societal marketing concept? Why did this orientation emerge?
    Topic: International Marketing Process and Company Orientations

    The societal marketing concept holds that the organization's task is to determine the needs, wants and interests of target markets and to deliver the desired satisfactions more effectively and efficiently than competitors, and additionally to do so in a way that preserves or enhances the consumer's and the society's well-being. This orientation arose as some questioned whether the marketing concept is an appropriate philosophy in an age of environmental deterioration, resource shortages, explosive population growth, world hunger and poverty, and neglected social services. The marketing concept possibly sidesteps the potential conflicts among consumer wants, consumer interests and long-run societal welfare. For example, the fast-food hamburger industry offers tasty but unhealthy food with high fat content, wrapped in convenient packaging which leads to much waste; in satisfying consumer wants, such restaurants may be hurting consumer health and causing environmental problems.

  14. What is the value chain? Name the primary and support activities in a firm's value chain.
    Topic: International Marketing Process and Company Orientations

    The value chain is a tool for identifying ways to create more customer value. Every firm is a collection of activities performed to design, produce, market, deliver and support its products. Just like a metal chain, the strength of a business value chain is the strength of its weakest link, so to improve delivery of final customer value the firm must first improve its weakest links. The value chain identifies nine strategically relevant activities: Primary activities (through which a product/service passes during various stages of manufacturing and delivery): inbound logistics, operations, outbound logistics, marketing and sales, and service. Support activities (not directly involved in manufacturing and delivery, but supporting such activities): firm infrastructure, human resource management, technology development, and procurement.

  15. Why do companies engage in international business? List the major reasons.
    Topic: Engaging in International Markets and Trade Theories

    Companies engage in international business for a variety of reasons. Identifying these reasons for any firm is important to understand the nature and direction of its motivation to engage in international marketing. The major reasons are: - To expand sales of the firm - To acquire resources, technology and skills from foreign countries - To diversify their sources of sales and supplies - To capitalize on incentives from governments, local and foreign - To follow existing customers who have moved overseas

  16. Write down the core concepts of 'Mercantilism' (classical country-based theory).
    Topic: Engaging in International Markets and Trade Theories

    Mercantilism is an old 16th century economic philosophy that attempted to explain how countries may become prosperous and strong. Its core concepts are: - A country's wealth is measured by its holdings of gold and silver (the reserves of the modern era). - The country's goal should be to enlarge those holdings. - To do this, a country should maximize the difference between its exports and imports; it should promote exports and discourage imports, so that if exports are more than imports, foreigners have to pay the difference in gold and silver. - With larger holdings of gold and silver, kings could have more wealth and could afford larger armies to expand their kingdoms. - This approach makes exporters and domestic manufacturers of export products happy as their businesses grow. Today's 'unfavorable balance of trade', when exports of a country are less than its imports, is an extension of the same idea. Critics argue that mercantilism actually weakens a country, because a country's true wealth is measured by the wealth of all its citizens and depends on the production of goods and services rather than the accumulation of gold reserves.

  17. Explain the theory of absolute advantage. What are natural and acquired advantages?
    Topic: Engaging in International Markets and Trade Theories

    The theory of absolute advantage was forwarded in 1776 by Adam Smith. It advocates free trade among world countries to maximize citizens' wealth. Free trade enables a country to expand the amount of goods and services available to it by specializing in the production of some goods and services and trading for others. A country should export those goods and services for which it is more productive than others, and import those goods and services for which other countries are more productive. A country can have advantages over other countries of two types: - Natural advantage: climatic conditions, natural resources, abundant cheap labor force, etc. - Acquired advantage: development of a product or process, skills development, etc.

  18. Explain the theory of comparative advantage and the factor-proportions concept.
    Topic: Engaging in International Markets and Trade Theories

    The theory of comparative advantage was forwarded in the early 19th century. It resolves the issue of a country having absolute advantage in many products: a country should produce and export such products where it has comparatively more advantage and hence can earn better margins, even if it has absolute advantage in several products. Its salient features are: - A country should produce and export those goods and services for which it is relatively more productive than other countries. - The concept of opportunity cost (what a country gives up to get or produce a certain good) should be applied in determining which goods a country should produce. The factor-proportions concept identifies which products may offer comparative advantage to a country. Factors (resources) vary among countries, e.g. oil is cheaper in Saudi Arabia, China has cheaper labor, Singapore has capital and funds. A country will have comparative advantage in producing products that intensively use the resources it has in abundance.

  19. Describe the international product life-cycle theory and its stages.
    Topic: Engaging in International Markets and Trade Theories

    The international product life-cycle theory explains the impact of a product's life-cycle stage on the flow of its trade, i.e. where a product would be manufactured and where it would be in demand. Shifts in manufacturing and trade flow go through four phases: 1. New product stage: The product is initially produced and sold mostly in the country in which it is developed (nearby observed need and market). Advanced technology products are initially conceptualized in developed countries and sold in these markets. 2. Growth stage: The market for the successful product grows rapidly. The product is produced in the innovating and other industrial countries and sold in many industrial countries. 3. Mature stage: The market becomes competitive and buyers become experienced. Margins decline and competitive pressure requires manufacturers to seek lower production costs, so production shifts from industrialized countries to countries where costs are lower; the innovating country may stop producing and start importing. 4. Decline stage: Demand declines, especially in advanced countries, as more effective technologies and products are introduced. Production and market of the product are mainly in less developed countries. Exceptions: products with very short life cycles, luxury products where cost is less important, products requiring specialized skills, strategic products, and differentiated products (e.g. handmade Italian leather fashion products) experience less, if any, impact of the life-cycle stage.

  20. Explain Porter's theory of national competitive advantage.
    Topic: Engaging in International Markets and Trade Theories

    Professor Michael Porter forwarded this theory in 1990, combining country-specific and firm-specific factors to explain how firms and industries of certain countries achieve success in international markets. According to this theory, success in international trade comes from the interaction of four country- and firm-specific elements: 1. Factor conditions: abundance and quality of land, raw materials, labor, capital, educational level of the workforce, the country's infrastructure, etc., i.e. the factors essentially needed for producing certain products and services. 2. Demand conditions: a large, sophisticated domestic market stimulates development and distribution of innovative products which may also be exported. Most new innovative products are first developed for domestic markets and then sold in other countries. 3. Related and supporting industries: most products require many complex technologies, and no single firm can master all of them. Firms need to collaborate with other firms as buyers and suppliers; industries successful internationally are those where related and supporting firms are co-located in proximity. 4. Firm strategy, structure and rivalry: the presence of a competitive domestic market forces local firms to focus efforts on skills training, strategizing and research and development, which eventually shapes companies to reduce costs and become competitive internationally.

  21. What categories of information do international marketing managers require for successfully operating in international markets?
    Topic: Information Requirements and National Environments

    Managers need a vast variety of information for successfully operating in international markets. The seven categories are: 1. Marketing mix information: to add, delete or change products, stage of product life cycle, marketing/sales campaign, distribution channel selection, price/demand and profitability analysis. 2. Competitor information: corporate, business and functional strategies, and market share. 3. Foreign exchange information: interest rates, exchange rates, balance of payment, attractiveness of a country's currency, and expectations of analysts. 4. Market potential information: demand estimates, consumer behavior, review of products, channels, communication media, and market performance. 5. Prescriptive information: laws, regulations and rulings concerning taxes, earnings and dividends in both home and host countries. 6. Resource information: availability of human, financial, information and physical resources. 7. General conditions: overall review of socio-cultural, political and technological environments.

  22. Discuss the major political risks faced by firms in international markets.
    Topic: Information Requirements and National Environments

    A marketer's firm faces numerous political risks in international markets. The major political risks are: - Direct interference in a foreign company's business, such as expropriation, confiscation and nationalization. - Campaigns against foreign goods (from governments or people/publics). - Kidnappings, terrorist threats and other forms of violence targeted against foreign firms or foreigners. - Civil wars, which have the potential to disrupt businesses in many foreign countries. - Changes in policies and regulations, too often and non-consistently, which affect businesses in any country. - Changes in governments, which may also result in changing national priorities. - Monetary and fiscal policies such as inflation, currency devaluation, increased taxation and currency controls. - Government as a business competitor: when a government becomes involved in business in its own country, it has access to large resources and may implement regulations favoring its own businesses, becoming a formidable competitor. International marketers may use multilateral agency guarantees, such as those of the Multilateral Investment Guarantee Agency (MIGA), a subsidiary of the World Bank, to protect themselves against some political risks.

  23. Describe the main types of legal systems found in different countries.
    Topic: Information Requirements and National Environments

    Business environments in foreign countries vary with the types of legal systems: - Common law: based on the cumulative wisdom of judges' decisions on individual cases over time. Relevant judgments of upper courts have bearings on lower court decisions. - Statutory law: laws enacted by legislative actions, i.e. through parliamentary proceedings. - Civil law: based on codification and detailed listings. Laws, their applications and penalties are all detailed, and judges have little flexibility compared with common-law systems. - Tort law: covers wrongful acts, damages and injuries caused by an action other than breach of contract. In some countries, like the USA, judges make big awards for tort liabilities, while in many other countries courts are reluctant to entertain big claims under tort. - Religious law (theocracy): cases are adjudicated based on religious knowledge and beliefs. - Bureaucratic law: officials adjudicate cases, such as in communist countries, which results in lack of consistency, predictability and appeal procedures.

  24. Differentiate between theocracy (religious law) and bureaucratic law.
    Topic: Information Requirements and National Environments

    Theocracy (religious law): In this type of legal system, cases are adjudicated based on religious knowledge and beliefs. The source of law is religion, and judgments are derived from religious principles and interpretations by religious authorities. Bureaucratic law: In this type of legal system, officials adjudicate cases, such as in communist countries. Because decisions rest with government officials rather than an independent judiciary applying codified law or precedent, this results in lack of consistency, predictability and proper appeal procedures. Thus, the fundamental difference is the basis of adjudication: religious knowledge and beliefs in theocracy, versus the discretion of state officials in bureaucratic law.

  25. What are the methods of international business dispute resolution? Distinguish between adjudicative and consensual processes.
    Topic: Information Requirements and National Environments

    Methods of international business dispute resolution include lawsuits (litigation), negotiations, conciliation, mediation and arbitration. Dispute resolution processes fall into two major types: - Adjudicative processes, such as litigation or arbitration, in which a judge, jury or arbitrator determines the outcome for the parties. - Consensual processes, such as mediation, conciliation or negotiation, in which the parties themselves attempt to reach agreement. In conciliation, the parties agree to use a conciliator who meets with them separately to resolve their differences; the process has no legal standing by itself and the conciliator usually makes no award. In mediation, a neutral third party (the mediator) assists the parties, using appropriate techniques, to negotiate an agreement; decision-making remains with the parties rather than being imposed by the third party.

  26. Why do governments intervene in international trade? Give the industry-level and national-level rationales.
    Topic: Role of Governments in International Markets

    Governments intervene in trade in their countries and abroad for a variety of reasons. Industry-level needs: - National defense argument: to promote the local defense industry. - Strategic industry argument: to support development of an essential industry in the country (such as textiles in Pakistan). - Infant industry argument: to support an emerging industry in the country, to protect it in the infancy stage from foreign competition. - Maintenance of existing jobs: governments intervene to support certain industries to maintain existing jobs in the economy. - To help make local firms compete internationally, so that exports from the country increase. National-level needs: - As part of economic development programs, including import substitution and export promotion. - As a result of public choice, to pacify pressures from various interest groups, and because of the unemployment level. - To ensure required revenue earnings to manage the government and its programs. - For regulating the demand of certain products (cigarettes, alcohol, etc.). - To influence economic relationships with other countries, and due to trade deficits or political/reactionary measures. Other needs include achieving balance-of-payments adjustments, price-control objectives, maintaining spheres of influence, preserving national identities in certain industries, and mere bureaucratic attitude.

  27. Make a list of those subsidies that a government offers to exporters to promote exports.
    Topic: Role of Governments in International Markets

    Governments promote exports in a variety of ways. Export subsidies and related support include: - Tax breaks - Direct payments to producers - Product price support - Cheaper resources (i.e. land, utilities) - Public services provided at lower cost In addition, governments support exporters through: - Establishment of export trade / processing zones - Export financing programs - Training and assistance programs - Other governmental assistance

  28. What are non-tariff barriers? Describe their main forms.
    Topic: Role of Governments in International Markets

    Non-tariff barriers are government controls on international trade other than tariffs (taxes placed on goods involved in international trade). Their main forms are: Direct price influences: - Export subsidies - Customs valuation - Other direct price influences Quantity controls: - Import/export quotas - Buy-local legislation - Voluntary export restraint (VER) - Embargo Other controls: - Licensing, foreign exchange controls, administrative delays, reciprocal requirements, restrictions on services, and technical and government regulations. Cultural and language differences among countries also create non-tariff barriers for international marketers.

  29. Define culture and list the elements of culture.
    Topic: International Cultural and Social Environments

    Culture is the collection of values, beliefs, rules, behaviors, customs, attitudes, institutions and artifacts that characterize a human population. Its characteristics are: it consists of learned patterns of behavior common to members of a given society; its elements are interrelated; it is adaptive; and it defines membership of the society. The elements of a culture are: - Social structure: individuals and their attitudes, families, groups, social stratification, and social mobility across the social/economic strata. - Language: a cultural mirror (words and discourses and their use and meaning in a particular culture). - Communication: low-context vs high-context cultures, nonverbal communication (which constitutes over 80% of social communication), gift giving and hospitality. - Attitudes and beliefs: attitudes towards time, work and leisure, achievement, change, jobs, religion, aesthetics, moral/ethical issues, and other cultures and races.

  30. Why do we study Hofstede's dimensions of national culture? Explain all the dimensions.
    Topic: International Cultural and Social Environments

    Hofstede's dimensions of national culture are studied because culture strongly influences how business is conducted in different countries: management styles, decision making, organizational structures and employee behavior all vary with national culture. Understanding these dimensions helps an international marketer and manager anticipate workplace values in a foreign country, adapt management and marketing practices accordingly, and avoid costly misunderstandings when operating across cultures. The dimensions are: 1. Power distance: the extent to which power in organizations is unequally distributed. In high power distance cultures there are centralized, tall, pyramid organizations and people at lower strata have lower qualifications (e.g. Philippines, Mexico, South Korea, India). In low power distance cultures organizations are less centralized and flatter, and people at lower strata have higher qualifications (e.g. Austria). 2. Uncertainty avoidance: the extent to which a society feels threatened by uncertain or ambiguous situations. High uncertainty avoidance societies have structured organizational activities, more written rules, less risk taking by managers, lower labor turnover and less ambitious employees (e.g. Germany, Japan and Spain); Denmark and Great Britain are low in uncertainty avoidance. 3. Individualism - collectivism: individualism is the tendency of people to look after themselves and their immediate family (normally high in wealthy countries); collectivism means people belong to groups or collectives and look after each other in exchange for loyalty (normally high in poor countries). 4. Masculinity - femininity: in masculine societies the dominant values are success, money and advancement, with large-scale enterprises and economic growth considered important, higher job stress and employees kept under a higher degree of control (e.g. Japan). In feminine societies the dominant values are caring for others and quality of life, with greater importance on cooperation, a friendlier atmosphere, employment security, low job stress and considerable freedom for employees, and more women holding higher-level jobs (e.g. Norway).

  31. Describe the three-stage analysis used to determine export potential in international markets.
    Topic: Export Potential and Marketing Research

    International marketers use a three-stage analysis to determine which foreign countries offer the best potential for exports from their companies: Stage One - Preliminary Screening: This involves defining the physical, political, economic and cultural environment of the countries. The demographic/physical environment includes population size, growth and density, urban and rural distribution, climate, shipping distance, product-significant demographics, physical distribution and communications networks, and natural resources. The political environment includes the system of government, political stability, ideological orientation, government involvement in business and attitudes towards foreign business. The economic environment includes the overall level of development, economic growth, currency and inflation, balance of payments, per capita income and disposable income. The social/cultural environment includes literacy rate, existence of a middle class, similarities with the home market, and language. Some foreign markets are eliminated from further consideration at this stage. Stage Two - Analysis of Industry Market Potential: The short-listed markets are analyzed for industry potential through market access (tariff levels, quotas, documentation and import regulations, local standards and other non-tariff barriers, patents and trademarks, preferential treaties, legal considerations) and product potential (customer needs and desires, local production, imports and consumption, exposure to and acceptance of products, availability of linking products, competitive offerings, availability of intermediaries, transportation facilities and manpower). Stage Three - Analysis of Company Sales Potential: The promising countries are assessed for the firm's own sales and profit potential through sales volume forecasting, landed cost (international freight and insurance, cost of product modification), cost of internal distribution (tariffs and duties, value-added tax, local packaging and assembly, trade margins, distribution and inventory costs, promotional expenditures) and other determinants of profitability (going price levels, competitive strengths and weaknesses, credit practices, current and projected exchange rates).

  32. Describe the four steps of the international marketing research process.
    Topic: Export Potential and Marketing Research

    A business (marketing) research process consists of four steps: Step 1 - Defining the problem and research objectives: The problem (the decision for which information is needed) and research objectives are defined, along with the market structure and the product concept (the meaning of the product in a particular environment). A research project may have one of three types of objectives: exploratory research (to gather preliminary information that will help define the problem and suggest hypotheses), descriptive research (to describe things such as the market potential for a product, demographics or attitudes of customers), or causal research (to test hypotheses about cause-and-effect relationships). Step 2 - Developing the research plan: The plan includes determining specific information needs, planning for gathering secondary information (information that already exists somewhere, collected for another purpose), the primary data collection plan (research approaches: observation, survey, experiment), deciding the contact method (mail, telephone, personal), and detailing the sampling plan (sampling unit, sample size, sampling procedure). Step 3 - Implementing the research plan: In this step the data is collected and processed. The data collection phase is generally the most expensive and most subject to error, due to wrong implementation, problems in contacting respondents, biased or dishonest answers, and problems with interviewers. Step 4 - Interpreting and reporting the findings: Researchers and managers must work together at this stage, because researchers know the methodology better while managers understand the problems and possible management solutions better.

  33. Distinguish between primary and secondary data. What issues arise in primary data collection in international research?
    Topic: Export Potential and Marketing Research

    Secondary data is information that already exists somewhere, having been collected for another purpose. It may be readily available and cheaper than primary research, but its quality and reliability may be compromised by the mechanisms used to collect it, and comparability may be hindered by lack of functional or conceptual equivalence. In many developing nations, secondary data are very scarce. Primary data is information collected for the specific purpose at hand. Issues with primary global research include: - Sampling problems arising out of biased respondents, difficulties in reaching respondents, and non-response; there may also be an absence of proper sampling frames. - Different languages and cultural variations cause translation and meaning problems; questionnaires need to fulfill the criteria of conceptual, functional, translation and scalar equivalence. Back-translation and parallel translation are used to avoid sloppy translations. - Instrument error, which arises from the questionnaire and the interviewer, and frame error, which occurs when sampling frames are available from different sources in different countries.

  34. What are the modes of entry into international markets? Explain exporting and its forms.
    Topic: Modes of Entry and Export Management

    The modes of entry into international markets are: exporting, international licensing, international franchising, specialized modes, foreign direct investment, and countertrade. Exporting is a simple mode of internationalizing a domestic business. Its advantages are that it allows a firm to quickly enter the foreign market, often involves less financial exposure, and permits a firm to enter a foreign market gradually, allowing it to assess local conditions and fine-tune its products to better suit the needs of customers in the host country. Its disadvantages are little control over marketing and distribution in the host country, the risk of quickly losing the market to other firms, and high transportation costs for many goods, which may render the exported products too expensive. Forms of exporting: - Indirect exporting: occurs when a firm sells its products to a domestic customer, who in turn exports the product, in either its original form or a modified form. - Direct exporting: involves sales to customers, either distributors or end-users, located outside the firm's home country. - Intracorporate transfers: selling of goods/services by a firm in one country to an affiliated firm in another country.

  35. Explain the main terms of payment used in an export transaction.
    Topic: Modes of Entry and Export Management

    The terms of payment of an export transaction include: - Cash with order: cash payment when the order is placed. - Confirmed irrevocable letter of credit: a letter of credit issued by the importer's bank and confirmed by a bank, usually in the exporter's country; the obligation of the second bank is added to the obligation of the issuing bank to honor drafts presented in accordance with the terms of credit. - Unconfirmed irrevocable letter of credit: a letter of credit issued by the importer's bank; the issuing bank still has an obligation to pay. - Revocable letter of credit: a letter of credit that may be withdrawn from the beneficiary at any time without prior notice to the exporter; it does not carry a bank's obligation to pay. - Sight draft: a draft so drawn as to be payable on presentation to the drawee (usually the buyer). - Time draft: a draft maturing at a certain fixed time after presentation or acceptance. - Open account: no draft is drawn; the transaction is payable when specified on the invoice. - Consignment: a shipment that is held by the importer until the merchandise has been sold, at which time payment is made to the exporter.
