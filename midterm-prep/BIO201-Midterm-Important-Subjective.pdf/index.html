  1. Why is carbon called a tetravalent element, and why is it so important in biomolecules?
    Topic: Biomolecules & Basic Chemistry

    Carbon has four electrons in its outer shell, so it can form four covalent bonds with other atoms; this is why it is called tetravalent. Because of this property, carbon atoms can join with each other and with hydrogen, oxygen, nitrogen and other elements to build the large and varied molecules of life, such as carbohydrates, proteins, lipids and nucleic acids.

  2. What is the general formula of carbohydrates? Give two examples and one function of carbohydrates in living organisms.
    Topic: Biomolecules & Basic Chemistry

    The general formula of carbohydrates is (CH2O)n, where n is three or more; the name itself means 'hydrates of carbon'. Examples are glucose (C6H12O6) and sucrose. Their main function is to provide and store energy: glucose is the chief fuel broken down in respiration, while starch (in plants) and glycogen (in animals) store energy for later use.

  3. What is a peptide bond? Between which two atoms is it formed, and how?
    Topic: Biomolecules & Basic Chemistry

    A peptide bond is the linkage that joins amino acids together to form proteins. It is formed between the carbon atom of the carboxyl group (–COOH) of one amino acid and the nitrogen atom of the amino group (–NH2) of the next amino acid. It is made by a condensation (dehydration) reaction in which a molecule of water is removed.

  4. At what temperature is ammonia (NH3) formed industrially from hydrogen and nitrogen? Name the process.
    Topic: Biomolecules & Basic Chemistry

    Ammonia is formed from nitrogen and hydrogen at a high temperature of about 450–500 °C (under high pressure and in the presence of an iron catalyst). This industrial method of making ammonia is called the Haber process.

  5. What is ATP? Describe its hydrolysis and the amount of energy released. Why is ATP called the energy currency of the cell?
    Topic: Biomolecules & Basic Chemistry

    ATP (adenosine triphosphate) is the main energy-carrying molecule of the cell. It consists of adenine, a ribose sugar and three phosphate groups. In hydrolysis, the terminal phosphate group is removed by water, producing ADP and inorganic phosphate: ATP + H2O → ADP + Pi + energy. This reaction releases about −12 kcal/mol of energy under cellular conditions. ATP is called the energy currency of the cell because, like money, it is earned (made during respiration and photosynthesis), stored briefly, and spent wherever the cell needs energy — for example in muscle contraction, active transport and the synthesis of molecules.

  6. What does ΔG (change in free energy) tell us about a reaction? Distinguish between exergonic and endergonic reactions.
    Topic: Biomolecules & Basic Chemistry

    ΔG, the change in free energy, tells us the direction of a reaction and the equilibrium point it moves towards; it does not tell us the speed of the reaction. If ΔG is negative, the reaction releases free energy and can proceed spontaneously — it is called exergonic (for example, ATP hydrolysis). If ΔG is positive, the reaction needs an input of energy to proceed — it is called endergonic (for example, the synthesis of glucose in photosynthesis).

  7. What is the cytoskeleton? Name and describe its three types of filaments.
    Topic: Cell Structure

    The cytoskeleton is a network of protein fibres inside the cytoplasm that gives the cell its shape, holds its organelles in place and helps in movement and cell division. It is made of three types of filaments: (1) Microfilaments — the thinnest fibres, made of actin; they help in cell movement and muscle contraction. (2) Microtubules — hollow tubes made of tubulin; they form the spindle during cell division and the core of cilia and flagella. (3) Intermediate filaments — rope-like fibres of medium thickness; they give mechanical strength and hold the nucleus and other organelles in position.

  8. Where is the nucleolus located, and what is its function?
    Topic: Cell Structure

    The nucleolus is a dense, round body present inside the nucleus of the cell. Its function is to make ribosomal RNA (rRNA) and to assemble the subunits of ribosomes, which are then sent out to the cytoplasm where protein synthesis takes place.

  9. Why does the DNA molecule carry a negative charge?
    Topic: DNA, Chromatin & Central Dogma

    DNA carries a negative charge because of its phosphate groups. Each nucleotide in the sugar–phosphate backbone contains a phosphate group that bears a negative charge at cellular pH. Since a DNA molecule contains a very large number of phosphate groups along both strands, the whole molecule is strongly negatively charged — which is why DNA moves towards the positive pole in gel electrophoresis.

  10. Describe the Hershey and Chase experiment. What conclusion did it give?
    Topic: DNA, Chromatin & Central Dogma

    In 1952, Hershey and Chase experimented with the T2 bacteriophage, a virus that infects bacteria. They labelled the protein coat of one batch of viruses with radioactive sulphur (35S) and the DNA of another batch with radioactive phosphorus (32P), then allowed the viruses to infect bacteria. After blending and centrifugation, they found that the phosphorus (DNA) had entered the bacterial cells, while the sulphur (protein) remained outside. The infected bacteria then produced new viruses. Conclusion: DNA — not protein — is the hereditary (genetic) material that carries information from one generation to the next.

  11. What is a nucleosome? Describe its structure.
    Topic: DNA, Chromatin & Central Dogma

    A nucleosome is the basic unit of chromatin packaging. It is formed when a segment of DNA wraps around a core of histone proteins (an octamer of histones). This 'beads on a string' arrangement compacts the long DNA molecule so that it can fit inside the nucleus, and further coiling of nucleosomes produces chromatin and, ultimately, the chromosome.

  12. What is the central dogma of molecular biology? Write its flow of information.
    Topic: DNA, Chromatin & Central Dogma

    The central dogma describes the one-way flow of genetic information in the cell: DNA → RNA → Protein. The DNA is first copied into messenger RNA by transcription, and the mRNA is then used to build a protein by translation. In this way the information stored in genes is expressed as the proteins that run the cell.

  13. Differentiate between DNA and RNA. (Any five points)
    Topic: DNA, Chromatin & Central Dogma

    1) Sugar: DNA contains deoxyribose sugar; RNA contains ribose sugar. 2) Strands: DNA is double-stranded and forms a double helix; RNA is usually single-stranded. 3) Bases: DNA uses adenine, thymine, guanine and cytosine; RNA uses uracil instead of thymine. 4) Location: DNA is found mainly in the nucleus; RNA is found in the nucleus and the cytoplasm. 5) Function: DNA stores the hereditary information; RNA (mRNA, tRNA, rRNA) carries and uses that information to make proteins. 6) Stability: DNA is chemically more stable and long-lived; RNA is short-lived.

  14. Which metal cation is present in the catalytic subunit of RNA polymerase, and what is its role?
    Topic: Transcription & Translation (Gene Expression)

    The catalytic subunit of RNA polymerase contains the magnesium cation, Mg2+. These Mg2+ ions sit at the active site of the enzyme and are essential for catalysis: they help the enzyme join incoming ribonucleotides to the growing RNA chain by forming phosphodiester bonds during transcription.

  15. What is meant by the +1 site in transcription?
    Topic: Transcription & Translation (Gene Expression)

    The +1 site is the exact position on the DNA where transcription begins — the first nucleotide that is copied into RNA. At initiation, the catalytic unit of RNA polymerase is placed just over the +1 site so that the first ribonucleotide is added at the correct starting point.

  16. What is an operon? Describe the components of a bacterial operon in their order.
    Topic: Transcription & Translation (Gene Expression)

    An operon is a cluster of genes in bacteria that are transcribed together as a single unit and are controlled by the same regulatory elements. Its components, in order, are: (1) Regulatory gene — produces a repressor protein that can switch the operon off. (2) Promoter — the site where RNA polymerase binds to start transcription. (3) Operator — the site where the repressor binds; when occupied, it blocks transcription. (4) Structural genes — the genes that code for the enzymes/proteins. (5) Terminator — located downstream of the structural genes, it marks the end of transcription.

  17. Describe the structure of the eukaryotic (80S) ribosome.
    Topic: Transcription & Translation (Gene Expression)

    The ribosome is the site of protein synthesis and is made of ribosomal RNA (rRNA) and proteins. The eukaryotic ribosome (80S) consists of two subunits. The small subunit (40S) contains one rRNA (18S) and about 33 polypeptides (proteins); it binds the mRNA and reads its codons. The large subunit (60S) contains three rRNAs (28S, 5.8S and 5S) and about 49 proteins; it joins amino acids together with peptide bonds. During translation the two subunits come together around the mRNA.

  18. Name the three binding sites for tRNA on the ribosome. Where does a charged tRNA land first, and with whose help?
    Topic: Transcription & Translation (Gene Expression)

    The ribosome has three tRNA binding sites: the A site (aminoacyl site), the P site (peptidyl site) and the E site (exit site). A charged tRNA (aminoacyl-tRNA, carrying its amino acid) lands first at the A site, where it is delivered with the help of a transfer (elongation) factor. The growing chain is held on the tRNA at the P site, and the empty, discharged tRNA leaves through the E site.

  19. Differentiate between transcription and translation. (Any five points)
    Topic: Transcription & Translation (Gene Expression)

    1) Definition: transcription is the copying of a DNA segment into mRNA; translation is the synthesis of a protein from the mRNA template. 2) Template: transcription uses DNA as template; translation uses mRNA as template. 3) Product: transcription produces RNA; translation produces a polypeptide (protein). 4) Enzyme/machine: transcription is carried out by RNA polymerase; translation is carried out by the ribosome. 5) Location: in eukaryotes transcription occurs in the nucleus, while translation occurs in the cytoplasm on ribosomes. 6) Building blocks: transcription uses ribonucleotides; translation uses amino acids carried by tRNA.

  20. What is Down syndrome? Which factor plays a major role in its occurrence?
    Topic: Genetics & Genetic Disorders

    Down syndrome is a chromosomal disorder caused by an extra copy of chromosome 21 (trisomy 21), so the affected person has 47 chromosomes instead of 46. It produces characteristic facial features, short stature and intellectual disability. The major risk factor is maternal age: the chance of having a child with Down syndrome increases greatly as the mother's age increases, especially after 35 years.

  21. List the characteristic features of Turner syndrome. What is its chromosomal cause?
    Topic: Genetics & Genetic Disorders

    Turner syndrome occurs in females who have only one X chromosome (45, XO) instead of two. Its characteristic features include short stature, a broad (webbed) chest and neck, and lymphoedema — swelling of the hands and feet, especially at birth. Affected females usually have underdeveloped ovaries and are infertile.

  22. What is alkaptonuria? Why is it more prevalent in cousin marriages?
    Topic: Genetics & Genetic Disorders

    Alkaptonuria is an inherited metabolic disorder in which the body cannot break down homogentisic acid because the enzyme that degrades it is missing; the acid accumulates and the urine turns black on standing. It is an autosomal recessive disorder, so it appears when a child receives the faulty gene from both parents. Cousin marriages increase this chance because close relatives are more likely to carry the same recessive gene; according to the course material its prevalence in cousin marriages is about 12.5%.

  23. What are chromosomal abnormalities? Explain with the examples of Down syndrome and Turner syndrome.
    Topic: Genetics & Genetic Disorders

    Chromosomal abnormalities are disorders caused by a change in the number or structure of chromosomes, usually arising from non-disjunction — the failure of chromosomes to separate properly during meiosis. Down syndrome is an example of an extra chromosome: there are three copies of chromosome 21 (trisomy 21, total 47), causing intellectual disability and typical facial features, and its risk rises with maternal age. Turner syndrome is an example of a missing chromosome: a female has only one X chromosome (45, XO, total 45), causing short stature, a broad chest, lymphoedema and infertility. Thus the normal chromosome number of 46 must be maintained for normal development.

  24. What is RFLP? What is it used to identify?
    Topic: Genetic Engineering & Genomics

    RFLP (Restriction Fragment Length Polymorphism) is a technique in which DNA is cut with restriction enzymes and the lengths of the resulting fragments are compared. Because the fragment pattern differs from person to person, RFLP is used to identify a specific DNA — for example in DNA fingerprinting, paternity testing, forensic investigations and the diagnosis of genetic diseases.

  25. What is genetic engineering? Describe the basic steps of inserting a foreign gene into a plasmid.
    Topic: Genetic Engineering & Genomics

    Genetic engineering is the direct manipulation of an organism's genes, in which a desired gene is cut out and transferred into another organism. The basic steps are: (1) Isolate the desired (foreign) gene and cut it out with a restriction enzyme. (2) Cut open a plasmid — a small circular DNA from bacteria that acts as a vector — with the same restriction enzyme. (3) Insert the foreign gene into the opened plasmid and join the ends with the enzyme DNA ligase; the result is recombinant DNA. (4) Introduce the recombinant plasmid into a host bacterium. (5) Select and grow the bacteria carrying the gene, so that many copies of the gene (clones) or its protein product are produced.

  26. How much money was spent on the Human Genome Project, and what was its main goal?
    Topic: Genetic Engineering & Genomics

    About 2.7 billion US dollars were spent on the Human Genome Project. Its main goal was to determine the complete sequence of the roughly three billion base pairs of human DNA and to identify all the genes present in the human genome.

  27. What is passive transport? Why does it not require energy? Give two examples.
    Topic: Membrane & Transport

    Passive transport is the movement of substances across the cell membrane from a region of higher concentration to a region of lower concentration, that is, along the concentration gradient. It does not require any input of energy (ATP) because the molecules move down the gradient by their own kinetic energy. Examples are simple diffusion of gases (O2 and CO2) and osmosis, the diffusion of water across a semi-permeable membrane.

  28. Differentiate between passive transport and active transport. (Any five points)
    Topic: Membrane & Transport

    1) Energy: passive transport needs no energy (ATP); active transport requires energy from ATP. 2) Direction: passive transport moves substances from higher to lower concentration (down the gradient); active transport moves them from lower to higher concentration (against the gradient). 3) Speed: passive transport is relatively slow; active transport is faster. 4) Carrier proteins: active transport always needs specific carrier/pump proteins; passive transport may or may not use channel proteins. 5) Example: diffusion and osmosis are passive; the sodium–potassium pump is active transport.

  29. Which component of the membrane absorbs light energy, and where is it found?
    Topic: Membrane & Transport

    Membrane pigments absorb light energy. In plant cells these pigments, such as chlorophyll, are present in the membranes of the chloroplast (the thylakoid membranes), where they capture sunlight for photosynthesis.

  30. Define electronegativity.
    Topic: Biomolecules & Basic Chemistry

    Electronegativity is the tendency (ability) of an atom to attract the shared pair of electrons towards itself in a covalent bond. Atoms with high electronegativity, such as oxygen, pull the electrons more strongly and make the bond polar — for example, the O–H bonds in a water molecule.

  31. What is an ionic bond?
    Topic: Biomolecules & Basic Chemistry

    An ionic bond is a chemical bond formed by the complete transfer of one or more electrons from one atom to another, producing oppositely charged ions that attract each other. For example, in sodium chloride (NaCl), sodium donates one electron to chlorine, and the resulting Na+ and Cl- ions are held together by electrostatic attraction.

  32. Define carbohydrates. Also write their classification.
    Topic: Biomolecules & Basic Chemistry

    Carbohydrates are organic compounds made of carbon, hydrogen and oxygen with the general formula (CH2O)n; they are a major source of energy for living organisms. They are classified into three groups: (1) Monosaccharides — single sugar units, e.g. glucose and fructose; (2) Disaccharides — two monosaccharide units joined together, e.g. sucrose and lactose; (3) Polysaccharides — long chains of many monosaccharide units, e.g. starch, glycogen and cellulose.

  33. What is meant by denaturation of a protein? Why do proteins denature?
    Topic: Biomolecules & Basic Chemistry

    Denaturation is the loss of the natural three-dimensional structure (secondary, tertiary and quaternary structure) of a protein — and with it the loss of its biological activity — without breaking its peptide bonds. Proteins denature due to heat, extremes of pH, organic solvents and heavy metal salts, which break the weak bonds (hydrogen bonds, ionic bonds and hydrophobic interactions) that maintain the folded structure.

  34. Differentiate between cofactor, coenzyme and prosthetic group.
    Topic: Biomolecules & Basic Chemistry

    A cofactor is a non-protein substance whose presence is essential for the activity of an enzyme. A coenzyme is a small organic, non-protein molecule that binds loosely with the protein part (apoenzyme) to form the active enzyme (holoenzyme); it cannot catalyse a reaction by itself, e.g. NAD+. A prosthetic group is a cofactor that binds very tightly to the enzyme (often by a covalent bond) and is not easily removed, e.g. the haem group.

  35. What is the active site of an enzyme?
    Topic: Biomolecules & Basic Chemistry

    The active site is the specific region of an enzyme where substrate molecules bind and undergo a chemical reaction. It consists of residues that form temporary bonds with the substrate (the binding site) and residues that catalyse the reaction of that substrate (the catalytic site).

  36. Enlist the macromolecules present in a cell.
    Topic: Biomolecules & Basic Chemistry

    A cell contains four major types of macromolecules: (1) Carbohydrates, (2) Proteins, (3) Lipids, and (4) Nucleic acids (DNA and RNA). These large molecules perform the structural, energetic, catalytic and informational functions of the cell.

  37. Write the role (functions) of lipids in living organisms.
    Topic: Biomolecules & Basic Chemistry

    Lipids are hydrophobic, water-insoluble molecules that aggregate together through hydrophobic and van der Waals forces. Their functions are: they store energy (fats and oils); phospholipids form the membranes of cells and organelles; carotenoids help in capturing light; some lipids act as hormones and vitamins; animal fat provides insulation under the skin and lipid sheaths insulate the nerves; and waxes make skin, fur and feathers water-repellent.

  38. What are the roles of proteins in living organisms?
    Topic: Biomolecules & Basic Chemistry

    Proteins perform a wide variety of roles: enzymes catalyse biochemical reactions; structural proteins such as collagen and keratin give strength and shape; transport proteins such as haemoglobin carry substances; antibodies defend the body against pathogens; hormones such as insulin regulate body processes; contractile proteins (actin and myosin) bring about movement; and storage proteins store amino acids and ions.

  39. Enlist the organelles of a typical animal cell.
    Topic: Cell Structure

    The components of a typical animal cell are: nucleus, nucleolus, ribosomes, vesicles, rough endoplasmic reticulum, smooth endoplasmic reticulum, Golgi apparatus, cytoskeleton, mitochondria, lysosomes and vacuoles, all present in the cytoplasm and bounded by the cell (plasma) membrane.

  40. Write the features of mitochondria.
    Topic: Cell Structure

    Mitochondria are double-membrane organelles: the outer membrane is smooth, while the inner membrane is folded into cristae enclosing the matrix. They contain their own small circular DNA and 70S ribosomes and can make some of their own proteins. They are the site of cellular respiration and produce ATP, so they are called the powerhouse of the cell.

  41. What are cristae? Write the types of cristae.
    Topic: Cell Structure

    Cristae are the folds of the inner mitochondrial membrane; they increase the surface area available for ATP production. On the basis of their shape, cristae are of different types: lamellar (flattened, plate-like), tubular (tube-like) and vesicular (sac-like).

  42. Write a short note on lysosomes and heat shock proteins.
    Topic: Cell Structure

    Lysosomes are membrane-bound vesicles containing digestive (hydrolytic) enzymes; they digest worn-out organelles, food particles and invading bacteria, and are called the suicidal bags of the cell. Heat shock proteins are special proteins produced by cells in response to stress such as high temperature; they act as chaperones that help other proteins fold correctly and protect the cell from damage.

  43. Define cell adhesion and cell recognition.
    Topic: Cell Structure

    Cell adhesion is the process by which cells attach to one another or to the extracellular matrix through special junction proteins, holding the tissues together. Cell recognition is the ability of a cell to identify other cells or substances as self or foreign through specific marker molecules (glycoproteins and glycolipids) present on its surface; it is the basis of immune responses.

  44. Write down the functions of the cellular membrane.
    Topic: Membrane & Transport

    The cellular (plasma) membrane: (1) separates the cell contents from the external environment and maintains the internal composition of the cell; (2) selectively controls the entry and exit of substances, being selectively permeable; (3) receives signals through receptors and helps in cell recognition and communication; (4) provides attachment sites for enzymes and the cytoskeleton; and (5) takes part in endocytosis and exocytosis.

  45. Define aquaporins.
    Topic: Membrane & Transport

    Aquaporins are special channel proteins present in the cell membrane that allow water molecules to pass rapidly across the membrane by facilitated diffusion, without allowing ions and other solutes to pass.

  46. Write a note on endocytosis, pinocytosis and phagocytosis.
    Topic: Membrane & Transport

    Endocytosis is the process by which a cell takes in materials by folding its membrane around them to form a vesicle. Its main types are: phagocytosis ('cell eating'), in which large solid particles such as bacteria are engulfed, e.g. by white blood cells; and pinocytosis ('cell drinking'), in which droplets of extracellular fluid are taken in. In both processes the material ends up inside the cell within a membrane-bound vesicle.

  47. Differentiate between purines and pyrimidines.
    Topic: DNA, Chromatin & Central Dogma

    Purines and pyrimidines are the two types of nitrogenous bases found in DNA and RNA. Purines have a double carbon–nitrogen ring structure; they are adenine and guanine. Pyrimidines have a single carbon–nitrogen ring; they are thymine and cytosine (and uracil in RNA). In DNA, a purine always pairs with a pyrimidine (A with T, and G with C).

  48. Differentiate between a nucleoside and a nucleotide.
    Topic: DNA, Chromatin & Central Dogma

    A nucleoside is a molecule made of a pentose sugar and a nitrogenous base only, e.g. adenosine. A nucleotide is a nucleoside plus a phosphate group (nucleotide = nucleoside + phosphoric acid); nucleotides are the building blocks of DNA and RNA, and ATP is also a nucleotide.

  49. What is Chargaff's rule?
    Topic: DNA, Chromatin & Central Dogma

    Chargaff's rule states that in DNA the amount of adenine is equal to the amount of thymine (A = T) and the amount of guanine is equal to the amount of cytosine (G = C); consequently, the total amount of purines equals the total amount of pyrimidines. This rule reflects the complementary base pairing of the DNA double helix.

  50. Write the functions of DNA.
    Topic: DNA, Chromatin & Central Dogma

    DNA is the hereditary material of the cell. Its functions are: it stores the genetic information needed for the development and functioning of the organism; it passes this information from one generation to the next; it controls the synthesis of RNA and proteins, and through them all the activities of the cell; and it can replicate itself so that each daughter cell receives a complete copy.

  51. Which type of bacterial colonies did F. Griffith use in his experiment?
    Topic: DNA, Chromatin & Central Dogma

    In his 1928 experiment, Griffith used two strains (colony types) of the bacterium Streptococcus pneumoniae: the smooth (S) strain, which has a capsule, forms smooth colonies and is virulent (disease-causing); and the rough (R) strain, which has no capsule, forms rough colonies and is non-virulent. His experiment led to the discovery of transformation — a 'transforming principle' (later identified as DNA) passed from dead S bacteria to living R bacteria.

  52. Write the three dilemmas of DNA replication.
    Topic: DNA, Chromatin & Central Dogma

    Three problems (dilemmas) faced during DNA replication are: (1) The two strands of DNA are antiparallel, but DNA polymerase can synthesize a new strand only in the 5' to 3' direction — solved by continuous synthesis of the leading strand and discontinuous synthesis of the lagging strand in short Okazaki fragments. (2) DNA polymerase cannot start a new strand from scratch — solved by the enzyme primase, which lays down a short RNA primer. (3) The double helix must be unwound and the separated strands kept apart without tangling — solved by helicase, single-strand binding proteins and topoisomerase.

  53. Differentiate between the dispersive, conservative and semi-conservative models of DNA replication.
    Topic: DNA, Chromatin & Central Dogma

    In the conservative model, the original DNA molecule is preserved intact and an entirely new molecule is generated. In the semi-conservative model, each daughter DNA molecule contains one complete old (parental) strand and one newly synthesized strand — this is the model that actually occurs, as proved by Meselson and Stahl. In the dispersive model, each strand of the daughter molecules would contain a mixture of old and new DNA segments.

  54. Differentiate between helicase and primase.
    Topic: DNA, Chromatin & Central Dogma

    Helicase is the enzyme that unwinds and separates the two strands of DNA by breaking the hydrogen bonds between them, forming the replication fork. Primase is the enzyme that synthesizes a short RNA primer on the DNA template; this primer provides the starting point (a free 3'-OH group) from which DNA polymerase begins adding nucleotides.

  55. Discuss the types of RNA and their functions.
    Topic: Transcription & Translation (Gene Expression)

    There are three main types of RNA. (1) mRNA (messenger RNA) carries the genetic message from DNA in the nucleus to the ribosome for protein synthesis. (2) tRNA (transfer RNA) carries specific amino acids to the ribosome and matches them to the codons of mRNA through its anticodon. (3) rRNA (ribosomal RNA), together with proteins, forms the ribosomes — the sites where proteins are assembled.

  56. Write a brief note on transcription.
    Topic: Transcription & Translation (Gene Expression)

    Transcription is the process in which an RNA copy is made from a DNA template by the enzyme RNA polymerase. It begins when RNA polymerase binds tightly to the promoter, which tells it where to start, which strand to read and the direction to take; the initiation site is the transcription start (+1) site. RNA polymerase moves along the template strand in the 3' to 5' direction and synthesizes RNA in the 5' to 3' direction, unwinding about 20 base pairs at a time; the transcript is antiparallel to the DNA template strand. RNA polymerase has no proofreading activity, so its error rate is about 1 in 10,000–100,000. In eukaryotes, the first product of transcription is pre-mRNA, which is processed before translation.

  57. What is a codon? Write the three stop codons.
    Topic: Transcription & Translation (Gene Expression)

    A codon is a sequence of three nucleotides (a triplet) on mRNA that specifies one amino acid, or a stop signal, during protein synthesis. The three stop (termination) codons, which do not code for any amino acid and end translation, are UAA, UAG and UGA.

  58. Write a brief note on the cell cycle.
    Topic: Cell Cycle & Cell Division

    The cell cycle is the ordered series of events by which a cell grows, prepares for division and divides into two daughter cells. It consists of interphase and the mitotic (M) phase. Interphase has three stages: G1 (cell growth), S (synthesis — DNA replication takes place) and G2 (preparation for division). In the M phase, mitosis (division of the nucleus) is followed by cytokinesis (division of the cytoplasm), producing two genetically identical daughter cells.

  59. Describe the process of mitosis.
    Topic: Cell Cycle & Cell Division

    Mitosis is the division of a single cell into two daughter cells which have the same number of chromosomes as the parent cell. It proceeds through four stages: (1) Prophase — chromatin condenses into chromosomes, the nuclear membrane breaks down and spindle fibres form. (2) Metaphase — chromosomes line up at the equator (middle) of the cell. (3) Anaphase — sister chromatids separate and move to opposite poles. (4) Telophase — two new nuclei form at the poles and the chromosomes decondense; cytokinesis then divides the cytoplasm.

  60. Differentiate between mitosis and meiosis.
    Topic: Cell Cycle & Cell Division

    Mitosis occurs in somatic (body) cells, involves a single division and produces two diploid daughter cells genetically identical to the parent cell; it functions in growth, repair and asexual reproduction. Meiosis occurs in reproductive cells, involves two successive divisions (meiosis I and meiosis II) and produces four haploid daughter cells that are genetically different from one another; it produces gametes and maintains the chromosome number across generations.

  61. Write the stages of meiosis I.
    Topic: Cell Cycle & Cell Division

    Meiosis I (the reduction division) has four stages: (1) Prophase I — chromosomes condense, homologous chromosomes pair up (synapsis) and exchange segments by crossing over. (2) Metaphase I — pairs of homologous chromosomes (bivalents) line up at the equator. (3) Anaphase I — homologous chromosomes separate and move to opposite poles, while the centromeres do not split. (4) Telophase I — two haploid nuclei form, followed by cytokinesis, giving two daughter cells with half the chromosome number.

  62. Define incomplete dominance.
    Topic: Genetics & Genetic Disorders

    Incomplete dominance is a form of intermediate inheritance in which one allele for a specific trait is not completely expressed over its paired allele. This results in a third phenotype in which the expressed physical trait is a combination (blend) of the phenotypes of both alleles, e.g. pink flowers produced by crossing red and white flowers in snapdragon.

  63. Define codominance.
    Topic: Genetics & Genetic Disorders

    Codominance is a form of dominance in which both alleles of a gene pair in a heterozygote are fully expressed. This results in offspring with a phenotype that is neither dominant nor recessive; a typical example is the ABO blood group system, in which both A and B antigens appear in individuals with AB blood group.

  64. Define epistasis.
    Topic: Genetics & Genetic Disorders

    Epistasis is the interaction between genes in which one gene prevents (masks) the expression of another gene located at a different locus. The gene that masks the other is called epistatic, and the gene whose expression is masked is called hypostatic.

  65. Define mutation.
    Topic: Genetics & Genetic Disorders

    A mutation is a sudden, permanent and heritable change in the DNA sequence (genetic material) of an organism. Mutations may occur in a single gene (gene or point mutation) or in the number or structure of chromosomes, and they are an important source of genetic variation.

  66. Write the laws of inheritance given by Mendel.
    Topic: Genetics & Genetic Disorders

    Mendel's laws of inheritance are: (1) Law of dominance — in a heterozygote, one allele (the dominant) expresses itself while the other (the recessive) remains hidden. (2) Law of segregation — the two alleles of a gene separate from each other during gamete formation, so each gamete receives only one allele. (3) Law of independent assortment — genes of different characters assort independently of one another during gamete formation.

  67. Describe the techniques used by Mendel in his experiments.
    Topic: Genetics & Genetic Disorders

    Mendel selected the garden pea (Pisum sativum) because it has clear contrasting characters, a short life cycle and can easily be self-pollinated or cross-pollinated. He studied seven pairs of contrasting characters, one or two at a time; performed reciprocal crosses; controlled pollination by removing the anthers (emasculation) and covering the flowers; counted the offspring of each generation (F1 and F2) numerically; and analysed the results statistically to derive his laws of inheritance.

  68. Define a genetic map.
    Topic: Genetics & Genetic Disorders

    A genetic map (linkage map) is a diagram that shows the relative positions and distances of genes on a chromosome. The distances are estimated from the frequency of crossing over (recombination) between genes: the higher the recombination frequency, the farther apart the genes are. The unit of map distance is the centimorgan (cM).

  69. Define the threshold effect.
    Topic: Genetics & Genetic Disorders

    The threshold effect applies to multifactorial (polygenic) disorders: the disorder appears only when the combined genetic and environmental liability crosses a certain threshold value; below the threshold the person remains normal. Because close relatives share more of the liability genes, the rate of occurrence is higher in first-degree relatives, and it is highest in identical (monozygotic) twins, who share all of their genes.

  70. Differentiate between Klinefelter syndrome and Turner syndrome.
    Topic: Genetics & Genetic Disorders

    Klinefelter syndrome occurs in males who have an extra X chromosome (47, XXY); they are tall, have underdeveloped testes and are usually sterile. Turner syndrome occurs in females who are missing one X chromosome (45, XO); they have short stature, a broad chest, lymphoedema and underdeveloped ovaries, and are infertile. Thus, Klinefelter syndrome is an extra-chromosome condition of males, while Turner syndrome is a missing-chromosome condition of females.

  71. Define polyploidy and aneuploidy with examples.
    Topic: Genetics & Genetic Disorders

    Polyploidy is the condition in which a cell or organism has more than two complete sets of chromosomes, e.g. triploid (3n) and tetraploid (4n) plants such as seedless watermelon and many wheat varieties. Aneuploidy is the gain or loss of one or a few individual chromosomes, e.g. trisomy 21 (Down syndrome, 47 chromosomes) and monosomy X (Turner syndrome, 45 chromosomes).

  72. What is the difference between PKU and PAH?
    Topic: Genetics & Genetic Disorders

    PAH is the enzyme phenylalanine hydroxylase, which converts the amino acid phenylalanine into tyrosine. PKU (phenylketonuria) is the genetic disease that results when this enzyme is missing or defective: phenylalanine then accumulates in the body and damages the developing brain. In short, PAH is the enzyme, and PKU is the disorder caused by its deficiency.

  73. What is PCR? Enlist the requirements of PCR and write its applications.
    Topic: Genetic Engineering & Genomics

    PCR (polymerase chain reaction) is a technique used to make millions of copies of a specific DNA segment in vitro. Its requirements are: the DNA template to be copied, two primers, the heat-stable enzyme Taq polymerase (whose optimum temperature is about 72 °C), free nucleotides (dNTPs), and a thermal cycler that repeatedly heats and cools the mixture through the denaturation, annealing and extension steps. Applications: PCR can detect and measure very small amounts of DNA, so it is used in diagnosing genetic diseases and infections, in DNA fingerprinting and forensic investigations, and in research and gene cloning.

  74. What are restriction endonucleases? Write their function.
    Topic: Genetic Engineering & Genomics

    Restriction endonucleases (restriction enzymes) are special bacterial enzymes that can cut double-stranded DNA at specific recognition sites. In bacteria they act as a kind of immune system, protecting the cell from the invasion of foreign DNA, such as that of viruses. Because they cut DNA at specific sites, they are essential tools of recombinant DNA technology and genetic engineering.

  75. What is gel electrophoresis?
    Topic: Genetic Engineering & Genomics

    Gel electrophoresis is a technique used to separate DNA (or protein) fragments according to their size. The sample is loaded into a porous gel and an electric current is applied; because DNA is negatively charged (due to its phosphate groups), it moves towards the positive pole, and smaller fragments move faster and farther through the gel than larger ones, forming separate bands.

  76. What is the full form of VNTRs?
    Topic: Genetic Engineering & Genomics

    VNTR stands for Variable Number Tandem Repeats. These are short DNA sequences repeated one after another (in tandem) at a particular locus, in which the number of repeats varies from person to person; this variation is used in DNA fingerprinting.
