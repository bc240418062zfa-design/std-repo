  1. Give Sapir's definition of language and discuss it.
    Topic: Language: Nature, Origin and Characteristics

    According to Sapir (1921), "Language is a purely human and non-instinctive method of communicating ideas, emotions and desires by means of voluntarily produced symbols." The definition highlights four points: language is purely human, as it distinguishes man from other species; it is non-instinctive, i.e. it is learnt and not inborn like a reflex; it is a method of communicating ideas, emotions and desires; and it works through voluntarily produced symbols. The definition is not free from defects: much that is communicated by language is not covered by 'ideas, emotions and desires', and there are other systems of voluntarily produced symbols, such as body language, which we count as language only in an extended or metaphorical sense.

  2. Language is a symbol system. Explain.
    Topic: Language: Nature, Origin and Characteristics

    A language is a system of arbitrary vocal symbols by means of which a social group co-operates. Language is a system of symbols and rules that enable us to communicate. The symbols used in language can include speech sounds as well as writing, while the rules include grammar, parsing and pragmatics. There is an arbitrary relationship between a linguistic symbol and its referent: a symbol simply stands for something else, and the link between the two is a matter of convention. Every language has its own way of encoding and expressing human experience through its own set of symbols.

  3. Describe language as a sign system.
    Topic: Language: Nature, Origin and Characteristics

    A sign is something that stands for something else, and language is frequently used as a synonym for a sign system. Signs are of three types. An iconic sign directly resembles its referent, e.g. a drawing or a photograph. An indexical sign points to its referent or is naturally connected with it, e.g. smoke is an index of fire. A symbolic sign has an arbitrary relationship with its referent and depends on social convention, e.g. the words of a language. Linguistic signs are symbolic signs: the word 'tree' stands for the object only because the speech community agrees on the convention.

  4. What is cultural transmission?
    Topic: Language: Nature, Origin and Characteristics

    Cultural transmission is a design feature of human language. It means that language is not passed on genetically from parents to children; we inherit physical features from our parents, not their language. A specific language is acquired by learning it in a culture from other speakers. The traditions and conventions of a language community are passed from one generation to the next through teaching and learning. Human infants growing up in isolation produce no language, which proves that exposure to a language in a culture is crucial in the acquisition process.

  5. What is interchangeability? Write examples.
    Topic: Language: Nature, Origin and Characteristics

    Interchangeability is a design feature of human language which means that any speaker of a language can both send and receive messages: the roles of speaker and hearer are interchangeable. Any organism with a message can repeat a message it hears from another member of the community. For example, the sentence 'I am hungry' can be spoken by any speaker and understood by any hearer, and a message like 'The shop is closed' can be passed on from one person to another without change. This is not possible in most animal communication systems, where signals are tied to a particular sex or role.

  6. What is reflectiveness as a design feature of language?
    Topic: Language: Nature, Origin and Characteristics

    Reflectiveness means that human beings can use language to talk about language itself. We can reflect on language and communicate about communication: we define words, discuss grammar, translate sentences and comment on how something was said. For example, asking 'What does the word arbitrary mean?' is a use of language about language. This feature, also called reflexiveness, is uniquely human and was one of the features Hockett added later to his list of design features.

  7. Explain the quality of arbitrariness in human language.
    Topic: Language: Nature, Origin and Characteristics

    Arbitrariness means that there is no natural or inherent connection between the form of a linguistic sign and its meaning. The relationship between a word and the thing it refers to is purely conventional: the creature we call 'dog' in English is 'kutta' in Urdu and 'chien' in French, and none of these forms resembles the animal. The connection between the word 'dog' and the creature it refers to is not natural; it seems quite arbitrary. A small exception is onomatopoeic words like 'cuckoo' or 'buzz', which imitate natural sounds, but they too vary from language to language.

  8. Discuss animal communication and how it differs from human language.
    Topic: Language: Nature, Origin and Characteristics

    Animal communication is instinctive and limited. Animals are born with a set of specific signals that are produced instinctively, and each signal has a fixed reference: it can only be used for one particular situation, such as warning of danger, claiming territory or attracting a mate. Animals can only talk about their immediate environment; their systems lack displacement, productivity, duality and cultural transmission. Human language, by contrast, is learnt, culturally transmitted, arbitrary and creative: humans continually create new expressions for new objects and situations, can talk about the past and the future, and combine a small set of sounds into unlimited meaningful utterances.

  9. Differentiate between a communicative signal and an unintentional informative signal.
    Topic: Language: Nature, Origin and Characteristics

    A communicative signal is produced intentionally in order to convey a message to a receiver, e.g. a spoken sentence, a wave of the hand or a traffic light turning red. An unintentional informative signal is not produced in order to communicate, yet it still gives information to an observer, e.g. a blush reveals embarrassment, a sneeze reveals that a person has a cold, and a yawn reveals tiredness. The difference lies in intention: the first is deliberately sent, the second is merely noticed.

  10. What are bioacoustic exchanges of information?
    Topic: Language: Nature, Origin and Characteristics

    Bioacoustic exchanges of information refer to communication between animals through sound signals. Animals produce vocal and auditory signals — calls, songs and cries — in order to exchange information about danger, food, territory, identity and mating. Such exchanges are instinctive and limited to a fixed set of signals; for example, vervet monkeys have about thirty-six vocal calls, each tied to a specific situation. They differ from human language, which is learnt and culturally transmitted rather than inherited.

  11. What is meant by high and low explicitness?
    Topic: Language: Nature, Origin and Characteristics

    Explicitness refers to the degree to which meaning is fully and clearly spelled out in the verbal message itself. In high explicitness, everything is stated in detail and little is left to the context, as in legal documents, manuals and formal instructions. In low explicitness, much of the meaning is left to the shared context, background knowledge and nonverbal cues, as in conversation between close friends or family members. Languages and cultures differ in how much explicitness they prefer in communication.

  12. Define open and closed climates in verbal communication.
    Topic: Language: Nature, Origin and Characteristics

    Rather than verbal communication being directed at one person as a means of control, the way we talk creates overall climates of communication that may control many. Verbal communication characterized by empathy, understanding, respect and honesty creates open climates that lead to more collaboration and more information exchange. Verbal communication that is controlling, deceitful and vague creates a closed climate where people are less willing to communicate and less trusting.

  13. Define 'habit' in the behaviorist view of language.
    Topic: Language: Nature, Origin and Characteristics

    The term 'habit' was used with reference to the bits of behaviour that could be identified as predictable responses to particular stimuli. The behaviorists define language as a learned behaviour involving a stimulus and a response: a child learns verbal habits through imitation, practice and reinforcement. Hall presumably means by language 'symbols', the vocal signals that are actually transmitted from the sender to the receiver in the process of communication and interaction. Critics point out that there is no sense of the term 'habit' in which the utterances of a language are themselves habits, since speakers constantly produce sentences they have never heard before.

  14. What is universal grammar?
    Topic: Language: Nature, Origin and Characteristics

    Universal Grammar is the system of principles, conditions and rules that is common to all human languages and is genetically inborn in every child. According to Chomsky, children are born with an innate language faculty (the Language Acquisition Device) containing these universal principles, which is why every child can acquire any language easily and rapidly despite limited exposure. Chomsky is thus a rationalist rather than an empiricist: language learning is innate, not merely a product of experience.

  15. Write the principles of nonverbal communication.
    Topic: Functions of Language and Nonverbal Communication

    Nonverbal communication has a distinct history and serves separate evolutionary functions from verbal communication. It is primarily biologically based, while verbal communication is primarily culturally based; some nonverbal messages have the same meaning across cultures, which no verbal system shares. It evolved earlier than verbal communication and served an early survival function. It is processed by an older part of the brain, which makes it more instinctual and involuntary than verbal communication. In short, nonverbal communication is interpersonal, conveys emotional messages, is more involuntary, more ambiguous and more credible than verbal communication.

  16. Why is nonverbal communication ambiguous?
    Topic: Functions of Language and Nonverbal Communication

    Nonverbal communication is ambiguous because its signals have no fixed, dictionary-like meanings. The same gesture or expression can carry different meanings in different situations and cultures: a smile may express joy, politeness or nervousness, and silence may show agreement, anger or respect. Nonverbal messages are also continuous and multi-channeled, so a receiver may attend to the wrong channel. Because nonverbal communication does not have explicit rules of grammar that bring structure, order and agreed-on patterns of usage, it is easily misinterpreted.

  17. Define the three types of kinesics.
    Topic: Functions of Language and Nonverbal Communication

    Kinesics is the study of body movements in communication. Its three main types are: (1) Head movement and postures — the way we hold and move the head and body, e.g. nodding for agreement or leaning forward to show interest; (2) Eye contact — gaze and eye behaviour (studied as oculesics), which shows attention, interest or dominance; (3) Facial expressions — movements of the face that display emotions such as happiness, fear, surprise and anger. Together these channels communicate feelings and attitudes, often more honestly than words.

  18. What is vocalics? State its functions.
    Topic: Functions of Language and Nonverbal Communication

    Vocalics, also called paralanguage, is the vocal element of nonverbal communication: the vocalized but not verbal part of a spoken message. It includes pitch, volume, speaking rate, tone, pauses and vocal quality. Its functions are: it expresses emotions and attitudes of the speaker; it emphasizes or modifies the verbal message; it helps regulate conversation, e.g. through pauses and turn-taking cues; and it can repeat, complement or even contradict the words spoken, which is why listeners often trust vocal cues when words and voice conflict.

  19. Define haptics.
    Topic: Functions of Language and Nonverbal Communication

    Haptics refers to touching behaviours that convey meaning during interactions. Touch — a handshake, a pat on the back, a hug, a slap — communicates affection, comfort, power, status and control. A lack of nonverbal communication competence related to touch can have negative interpersonal consequences, because the same touch may be welcome in one relationship or culture and offensive in another.

  20. Define proxemics and chronemics.
    Topic: Functions of Language and Nonverbal Communication

    Proxemics is the study of the human use of space and the effects that population density has on behaviour, communication and social interaction. It deals with the four zones of personal space: intimate, personal, social and public distance. Chronemics is the study of the use of time in communication, e.g. punctuality and waiting. In a monochronic time system time is divided into fixed units and one thing is done at a time, while in a polychronic time system a wider view of time is exhibited and time is perceived in large fluid sections.

  21. Language is a means of control. Explain.
    Topic: Functions of Language and Nonverbal Communication

    One function of verbal communication is control: we use language to direct, regulate and influence the behaviour of others through orders, instructions, rules, requests and warnings. In legal contexts, performative language can be a means of control, because utterances such as 'I sentence you...' or 'The court orders...' themselves perform the act. Controlled and restricted forms of language are also used in fields like air-traffic control and law so that communication remains precise and binding. The word 'control' usually has negative connotations, but control through language is essential for coordination in society.

  22. Language as a source of social interaction. Explain.
    Topic: Functions of Language and Nonverbal Communication

    Language is the main instrument of social interaction. Through language people greet each other, maintain relationships, negotiate, cooperate and express solidarity; much everyday talk — greetings, small talk, compliments — has no informational purpose but keeps social bonds alive. Quality time spent talking is an important part of interpersonal relationships. By interacting verbally, individuals form groups, share norms and construct their social identity, which is why language is described as a social phenomenon and not merely a system of sounds.

  23. What is general linguistics?
    Topic: Branches of Linguistics

    General linguistics is the branch of linguistics devoted to the study of the theoretical bases for describing language and the methods of investigating linguistic phenomena. The study of language in general — its nature, structure and the general patterns that shape each and every language, in both their current structure and their historical development — is termed general linguistics. It deals with language as a whole rather than with one particular language, and it also studies the connection between linguistics and other fields of learning.

  24. What are the intra-disciplinary branches of linguistics?
    Topic: Branches of Linguistics

    The intra-disciplinary branches are the divisions within linguistics itself, dealing with the three aspects of language: sound, structure and meaning. They are: General Linguistics, the study of language in general; Descriptive Linguistics, which deals with the description and elaboration of the theories presented by language scholars; Comparative Linguistics, which compares languages; and Historical Linguistics, which studies language change over time. Under these fall the core levels of analysis: phonetics (the study of speech sounds — their physiological production, acoustic properties, auditory perception and neurophysiological status), phonology (the organization of sound patterns), morphology (the study of words and how they are formed), syntax (the rules governing sentence structure and word order) and semantics (the study of meaning).

  25. Differentiate between prescriptive and descriptive linguistics.
    Topic: Branches of Linguistics

    Prescriptive linguistics tells us how a language ought to be used: it lays down rules of correctness, as traditional grammar does when it insists that a pronoun must be in the nominative case after a comparative adjective, or that a sentence must never begin with 'and'. Descriptive linguistics, in contrast, is a nonjudgmental approach that describes how a language is actually spoken and written by its users, without labelling forms right or wrong. Modern linguists follow the descriptive approach: except in certain educational contexts, modern linguists utterly reject prescriptivism.

  26. Linguistics is non-speculative in nature. Explain.
    Topic: Branches of Linguistics

    Linguistics is non-speculative. The main difference between the scientific and the non-scientific study of language is that linguistics is empirical rather than speculative or intuitive. It operates with publicly verifiable data obtained by means of observation and experiment: a claim about language must be supported by evidence that other scholars can check. Closely related to empiricism is objectivity, which considers all languages to be equal; objectivity demands that prescriptive beliefs should be challenged and terms like 'pure' and 'primitive' should either be clearly defined or rejected.

  27. Define sociolinguistics.
    Topic: Branches of Linguistics

    Sociolinguistics is the inter-disciplinary branch of linguistics that studies language in relation to society. It investigates how social factors — region, class, age, gender, occupation and situation — affect the way language is used, and how language in turn reflects social structure. Its topics include dialects and social dialects, registers, styles, bilingualism, language variation and language change in society.

  28. Define semantics.
    Topic: Branches of Linguistics

    Semantics is the branch of linguistics that studies meaning. It examines how words, phrases and sentences convey meaning, the relations between words such as synonymy, antonymy, hyponymy and polysemy, and the difference between denotative (descriptive) meaning and connotative (associative) meaning. In semantics, the concept is a minimal unit of meaning, which could be called a sememe in the same way as the unit of sound is called a phoneme in phonology.

  29. Define psycholinguistics.
    Topic: Branches of Linguistics

    Psycholinguistics is a branch of linguistics and psychology, part of the field of cognitive science, which discusses the way in which language is represented and processed in the brain. It covers the cognitive processes that make it possible to generate a grammatical and meaningful sentence out of vocabulary and grammatical structures, and it studies how language is acquired, comprehended, produced and remembered. The term was introduced by the American psychologist Jacob Robert Kantor in his book An Objective Psychology of Grammar (1936).

  30. Define computational linguistics.
    Topic: Branches of Linguistics

    Computational linguistics is the branch of linguistics in which the techniques of computer science are applied to the analysis and synthesis of language and speech. It is an interdisciplinary field concerned with the rule-based and statistical modelling of natural language from a computational perspective. Its applications include machine translation, speech recognition and synthesis, text processing, information retrieval and language teaching software.

  31. What is geographical linguistics?
    Topic: Branches of Linguistics

    Geographical linguistics, also called geolinguistics or linguistic geography, is the branch of linguistics mainly concerned with the analysis of the distribution patterns and spatial structures of languages in contact. It studies where languages and dialects are spoken, maps their boundaries (isoglosses), and explains how languages spread, mix and change across geographical areas.

  32. Define social dialect.
    Topic: Branches of Linguistics

    A social dialect (sociolect) is a variety of a language used by a particular social group or class. It differs from other varieties in pronunciation, vocabulary and grammar, and it marks the speaker's social background, education and status. For example, the speech of an educated urban group may differ systematically from that of a rural working-class group, even in the same city and the same regional dialect.

  33. Discuss the typological and genealogical classification of languages.
    Topic: Branches of Linguistics

    Languages are classified on two main grounds. Genealogical classification groups languages into families according to their historical descent from a common ancestor: languages belong to one family when they show systematic correspondences in basic vocabulary and grammar because of genealogical sharing, e.g. the Indo-European family. Typological classification groups languages according to their structural features, regardless of ancestry, e.g. isolating, agglutinative and fusional languages, or languages with SVO versus SOV word order. Linguistic similarity between languages arises for three reasons: genealogical sharing, areal diffusion and chance typological commonality.

  34. Differentiate between logographic and syllabic scripts.
    Topic: Branches of Linguistics

    The most basic model of written language acknowledges three general classes of script: logographic, syllabic and alphabetic. In a logographic script, each written symbol represents a whole word or morpheme, e.g. Chinese characters. In a syllabic script, each symbol represents a syllable of the spoken language, e.g. the Japanese kana scripts. In an alphabetic script, each symbol roughly represents a single sound (phoneme) of the language, e.g. the Roman alphabet used for English.

  35. Define an allograph.
    Topic: Branches of Linguistics

    An allograph is a variant form of the same letter or grapheme. Just as allophones are variants of one phoneme and allomorphs are variants of one morpheme, the different shapes of a letter — for example the capital 'A' and the small 'a', or different handwritten shapes of the same letter — are allographs of a single grapheme: they look different but count as the same unit in the writing system.

  36. What is articulatory phonetics?
    Topic: Phonetics and Phonology

    Articulatory phonetics describes in detail how the speech organs, also called vocal organs or articulators, in the vocal tract are used in order to produce, or articulate, speech sounds. It studies the movements and positions of the lips, teeth, alveolar ridge, palate, velum, tongue and vocal cords. In the oral cavity, after the teeth and the alveolar ridge comes the palate. Articulatory activity is investigated with instruments such as palatography, glottography and radiography.

  37. Discuss the scope of phonetics and phonology as branches of linguistics.
    Topic: Phonetics and Phonology

    Phonetics has been derived from the Greek word 'phone' meaning sound or voice. It is one of the important branches of linguistics, which deals with the study of speech sounds: it covers the domain of speech production, its transmission, and the reception aspect of speech. The sounds made by us when we talk are studied through the three branches of phonetics: articulatory phonetics (production), acoustic phonetics (the physical properties of sound waves) and auditory phonetics (perception). Phonology, on the other hand, focuses on the organization of sounds by studying speech patterns; its key words are distribution and patterning, and it aims to determine the sound patterns of all languages. In short, phonetics is considered the physics of sound and phonology the psychology of sound.

  38. What is phonology? Give its branches.
    Topic: Phonetics and Phonology

    Phonology is the study of the sound system of languages: it studies how abstract sound categories are manipulated in the processing of language, i.e. how sounds are organized, patterned and combined in a particular language. It has two branches. Segmental phonology deals with individual sounds — phonemes, the smallest units which cause a change in meaning — and their possible combinations. Supra-segmental phonology deals with features that extend over stretches larger than one segment, such as stress, pitch, tone and juncture.

  39. What is supra-segmental phonology?
    Topic: Phonetics and Phonology

    Supra-segmental phonology is the branch of phonology that studies the prosodic features of speech which are superimposed on the syllables and extend over more than one segment. These supra-segmental features include stress (relative emphasis on a syllable or word), pitch and intonation, tone, rhythm and juncture — the manner of transition between two consecutive sounds. Such features can change meaning: compare the noun 'REcord' with the verb 'reCORD', where only the stress pattern differs.

  40. What is amplitude?
    Topic: Phonetics and Phonology

    Amplitude is one of the physical properties in terms of which sound waves can be described, along with cycle, period and frequency. It is the amount of variation in air pressure in a sound wave — the height of the wave. Greater amplitude produces a louder sound, and smaller amplitude a softer sound, so amplitude is perceived as loudness and is measured in decibels. A cycle is a sequence of one increase and one decrease in air pressure, and frequency is the number of cycles in one second, expressed in hertz (Hz).

  41. Explain the process by which aerodynamic energy is transformed into acoustic energy.
    Topic: Phonetics and Phonology

    Speech production begins with aerodynamic energy: air is set in motion by the lungs and pushed up through the trachea into the vocal tract. When this moving air passes through the vibrating vocal cords and is modified by the articulators — tongue, lips, palate and velum — the energy of the moving air is converted into sound waves, i.e. acoustic energy. These pressure variations travel through the air from the speaker to the listener's ear, where they are received and decoded. Thus the aerodynamic energy of the airstream becomes the acoustic energy of speech sounds.

  42. Define the syllable phonetically and phonologically.
    Topic: Syllable and Stress

    Phonetically, a syllable is a unit of pronunciation having one vowel sound (a peak of sonority), with or without surrounding consonants, forming the whole or a part of a word. Phonologically, a syllable is a structural unit that organizes sounds in a language: it consists of an optional onset (consonants before the vowel), a rhyme, which itself consists of the nucleus (the vowel, the obligatory part) and an optional coda (consonants after the vowel). A minimum syllable has no onset and no coda, only a nucleus, e.g. 'eye'. Syllables influence the rhythm of a language.

  43. Define monosyllable, disyllable, trisyllable and polysyllable.
    Topic: Syllable and Stress

    Words are classified according to the number of syllables they contain. A monosyllable is a word of one syllable, e.g. 'cat', 'go'. A disyllable is a word of two syllables, e.g. 'table', 'painted'. A trisyllable is a word of three syllables, e.g. 'banana', 'remember'. A polysyllable is a word of more than three syllables, e.g. 'dictionary', 'information'. The sounds /l/, /r/ and /n/ can themselves stand as weak syllables in words like 'bottle', 'butter' and 'button'.

  44. What is zero onset?
    Topic: Syllable and Stress

    Zero onset means that a syllable has no consonant at its beginning: its onset position is empty and the syllable starts directly with the vowel nucleus. For example, the words 'eye', 'at' and 'owe' consist of a single syllable with zero onset and zero coda. Zero onset is possible in English, although many languages require every syllable to begin with a consonant.

  45. Define open syllable.
    Topic: Syllable and Stress

    An open syllable is a syllable that ends in a vowel and has no coda, e.g. the syllables of 'go', 'see' and the first syllables of 'female' and 'frozen'. A syllable that ends in a consonant (has a coda) is a closed syllable; the words 'in', 'of' and 'at' are examples of closed syllables. Open syllables typically contain a long vowel or a diphthong.

  46. What is a syllable-timed language?
    Topic: Syllable and Stress

    A syllable-timed language is a language whose syllables take approximately equal amounts of time to pronounce: each syllable receives roughly equal prominence, so there is little variation in stress. It can be compared with a stress-timed language, where the stressed syllables occur at regular intervals and the unstressed ones are squeezed between them. Turkish, French and Spanish are examples of syllable-timed languages.

  47. What are the features of a stress-timed language?
    Topic: Syllable and Stress

    In a stress-timed language, the stressed syllables of an utterance occur at roughly regular intervals of time, no matter how many unstressed syllables come between them. The unstressed syllables are compressed and their vowels are often reduced to the weak vowel /ə/. Rhythm is therefore based on stress rather than on the syllable. English is a stress-timed language: in 'The DOCtor is IN' the beats fall on the stressed syllables at equal intervals.

  48. What is stress?
    Topic: Syllable and Stress

    Stress is the relative emphasis or prominence given to a certain syllable in a word, or to a certain word in a sentence. A stressed syllable is pronounced with greater force, higher pitch and longer duration than the neighbouring syllables. Word stress is also called word accent: it is the manner in which stresses are distributed on the syllables of a word, and every language has its own stress rules. The two types of stress are primary stress (the strongest) and secondary stress.

  49. What are the types of stress?
    Topic: Syllable and Stress

    Stress is of two main types. Primary stress is the strongest emphasis placed on one syllable of a word, marked with a high vertical line, e.g. 'PHOtograph'. Secondary stress is a weaker emphasis on another syllable of a longer word, e.g. 'photo'GRAPHic'. Besides word stress, there is sentence stress: the accent given to certain (content) words within a sentence, while structure words normally remain unstressed. In an English sentence, adjectives, nouns and main verbs are normally stressed, while articles, conjunctions and auxiliary verbs are usually unstressed.

  50. State the general rules of stress.
    Topic: Syllable and Stress

    1. When nouns and adjectives possess two syllables, the stress is on the first syllable, e.g. PRO-gress. 2. When verbs possess two syllables, the stress is on the last syllable, e.g. pre-SENT; if the second syllable is strong, it is stressed in verbs such as 'arrive' or 'apply'. 3. Words ending in the 'shen' sound (-TION, -SION, -CIAN) are usually stressed on the second last syllable, e.g. e-du-CA-tion. 4. Words ending in -IC are usually stressed on the second last syllable, e.g. e-co-NO-mic. 5. Words ending in -EE and -OO are usually stressed on the last syllable, e.g. em-ploy-EE.

  51. What is morphology?
    Topic: Morphology and Word Formation

    Morphology is the branch of linguistics that studies the internal structure of words: how words are formed from smaller meaningful units (morphemes) and how they are related to other words in the same language. English speakers recognize that the words 'dog', 'dogs' and 'dogcatcher' are closely related, and morphology explains such relations. It studies the forms of words, for example the relation between 'take' and 'took', and describes word formation processes such as affixation, compounding, blending and clipping.

  52. Define morpheme. What are free morphemes?
    Topic: Morphology and Word Formation

    A morpheme is a minimal unit of meaning or grammatical function: the smallest unit of language that carries meaning and cannot be divided into smaller meaningful units. Free morphemes are morphemes that can stand alone as single words by themselves, e.g. 'book', 'go', 'happy'. They are of two kinds: lexical morphemes, which carry the main content of a message (nouns, verbs, adjectives and adverbs — an open class of words), and functional morphemes, which serve grammatical functions (conjunctions, prepositions, articles and pronouns — a closed class). The meaning of a free morpheme remains in it even after the segregation of prefixes and suffixes.

  53. Define bound morpheme.
    Topic: Morphology and Word Formation

    A bound morpheme is a morpheme that cannot stand alone as a word; it must be attached to another form. Segments or forms that cannot normally stand alone and are typically attached to another form are bound morphemes — affixes are another name for them. Examples are the prefixes un-, re-, pre- and the suffixes -s, -ed, -ness, -er. Bound morphemes are divided into two types: derivational morphemes and inflectional morphemes.

  54. What is a functional morpheme?
    Topic: Morphology and Word Formation

    Functional morphemes are the free morphemes that function to specify the relationship between one lexical morpheme and another, rather than carrying content of their own. They include conjunctions (and, but), prepositions (in, of, at), articles (the, a) and pronouns (she, it). In 'It is HER book', 'her' represents a functional morpheme. Functional morphemes belong to the closed class of words: new functional morphemes cannot normally be coined, whereas new lexical morphemes are added to the language all the time.

  55. What are derivational morphemes?
    Topic: Morphology and Word Formation

    Derivational morphemes are bound morphemes used to make new words and to change the grammatical category (part of speech) of words. For example: bake (verb) + -er = baker (noun); quick (adjective) + -ly = quickly (adverb); happy (adjective) + -ness = happiness (noun). Derivational morphemes can be added to free morphemes or to other derivational morphemes: in 'transformation', trans- is a derivational prefix added to the root 'form', and -ation makes the noun, to which -al can be added to give the adjective 'transformational'. Derivational morphemes often change the part of speech; the set of derivational affixes is open-ended, so there is potentially an unlimited number of them.

  56. Define suffix.
    Topic: Morphology and Word Formation

    A suffix is a bound morpheme (affix) added to the end of a word. Affixes added to the end of a word are called suffixes, while those added to the beginning are prefixes. English suffixes include the inflectional -s (plural), -ed (past tense) and -ing, and the derivational -er, -ness, -ly and -ation, as in 'teacher', 'happiness' and 'transformation' (which is a noun).

  57. Explain prefixes and suffixes.
    Topic: Morphology and Word Formation

    Prefixes and suffixes are the two positions in which affixes (bound morphemes) attach to a word. A prefix is added before the base or root, e.g. un- in 'unhappy', re- in 'rewrite', pre- in 'preview'; prefixes usually change the meaning of the word but not its class. A suffix is added after the base, e.g. -ness in 'happiness', -er in 'teacher', -s in 'books'; suffixes frequently change the word class as well as the meaning. A third type, the infix, is inserted inside a word; it is not normally used in English but is found in some other languages of the world.

  58. What is morphological analysis? Discuss the problems in morphological description.
    Topic: Morphology and Word Formation

    Morphological analysis is the analysis of the internal structure of words: decomposing a word into its constituent morphemes, e.g. 'cats' = cat + -s. The apparent description seems simple, but problems arise. What is the plural of 'sheep' (which has a zero plural) or of 'man' (which changes its vowel to 'men')? If institution + -al = institutional and -al is an adjective suffix, then in 'legal' the -al is not used to make an adjective, as 'leg' alone has no meaning of the word 'legal' in it. Such irregular, fused and empty forms make morphological description difficult.

  59. Describe briefly the inflectional affixes of English.
    Topic: Morphology and Word Formation

    English has only eight inflectional affixes, all of them suffixes: plural -s (books), possessive -'s (Ali's), third person singular -s (he runs), past tense -ed (walked), past participle -en/-ed (taken), present participle -ing (walking), comparative -er (taller) and superlative -est (tallest). Inflectional morphemes never change the part of speech of a word; they only add grammatical information such as number, tense or degree. When a word contains both types, the inflectional morpheme follows the derivational morpheme, e.g. 'teach-er-s'.

  60. Explain coinage and borrowing with examples.
    Topic: Morphology and Word Formation

    Coinage is the invention of totally new terms; the process of coining new words into a language is called neologism. Coinage typically happens with brand names that become general words, e.g. Kleenex, Xerox and Vaseline. Borrowing is taking over words from other languages, e.g. 'croissant' from French, 'piano' from Italian and 'algebra' from Arabic. A special kind of borrowing is loan-translation (calque), a direct translation of the elements of a word into the borrowing language, e.g. French 'gratte-ciel' (scrape-sky) for 'skyscraper'.

  61. Eponyms are another form of coinage. Explain.
    Topic: Morphology and Word Formation

    Eponyms are new words based on the name of a person or a place. When a word is coined from a proper name, it becomes an eponym: 'sandwich' from the Earl of Sandwich, 'jeans' from the city of Genoa, 'watt' from James Watt and 'volt' from Alessandro Volta. Eponyms are one of the forms of coinage, because a completely new word enters the language from a name that already exists.

  62. Define grammatical agreement.
    Topic: Morphology and Word Formation

    The word 'agreement', when referring to a grammatical rule, means that the words a writer or speaker uses need to align in number and in gender (when applicable). The two main types are subject-verb agreement and noun-pronoun agreement. Traditional grammatical analysis has given us categories including number, person, tense, voice and gender, whose role becomes clear in terms of agreement: in 'Cathy loves her dog', the subject is singular so the verb is also singular, and the pronoun 'her' agrees with 'Cathy' in person, number and gender.

  63. What is grammar?
    Topic: Grammar and Syntax

    Grammar is the set of structural rules governing the composition of clauses, phrases and words in any given natural language. It is the system that describes how the forms of a language are arranged to make meaningful sentences: which word orders are possible, how words change their form, and how smaller units combine into larger ones. Every speaker knows the grammar of his language implicitly, and one of the properties of generative grammar is that it has a finite number of rules but is capable of producing an infinite number of sentences.

  64. Discuss traditional grammar.
    Topic: Grammar and Syntax

    Traditional grammar is the collection of prescriptive rules and concepts about the structure of language that is commonly taught in schools. It is based on the grammars of Latin and Greek, which were taken to be the model for other grammars, especially English. The terms 'article', 'adjective' and 'noun' that we use to label the grammatical categories come from traditional grammar, in which a part of speech is a category of words which have similar grammatical properties. The concept of the eight parts of speech was given by Dionysius Thrax in the 2nd century BC. Traditional grammar judges sentences as correct or incorrect according to its rules rather than according to actual usage.

  65. What is traditional analysis?
    Topic: Grammar and Syntax

    In traditional analysis, language is analysed on the basis of the rules of traditional grammar: words are classified into parts of speech, sentences are parsed into subject, predicate, object and so on, and a sentence is considered grammatical if it conforms to the prescriptive rules handed down from Latin grammar. It is a rule-based, prescriptive method, unlike the descriptive methods of structural and constituent analysis used by modern linguistics.

  66. What is the descriptive approach in grammar?
    Topic: Grammar and Syntax

    According to the descriptive approach of grammar, a sentence is considered grammatical if a native speaker produces it in speaking. Instead of imposing rules from outside, the linguist observes and records the regular patterns of the language as it is actually used, and describes them without judging them as right or wrong. Its methods include structural analysis, which classifies forms by the frames they fit, and constituent analysis, which breaks sentences into their immediate constituents.

  67. What is structural analysis in grammar?
    Topic: Grammar and Syntax

    Structural analysis is a type of descriptive approach in which the grammatical categories of a language are established by observing which forms occur in which positions in sentences. The analyst uses test frames — sentences with an empty slot — and the forms that can fill the same slot are grouped into one form class. For example, forms that fit the frame 'The ___ is good' belong to the class of nouns. In this way the structure words and content words of a language are identified from distribution rather than from meaning.

  68. Define test frames.
    Topic: Grammar and Syntax

    Test frames are sentence frames with an empty slot, used in structural analysis to determine the grammatical class of a word. If a word can be placed in the slot of a given frame, it belongs to the class that the frame tests: for instance, 'The ___ is running' tests nouns, and 'He ___ the ball' tests verbs. Words that fit the same test frames have the same distribution and belong to the same form class.

  69. What is constituent analysis?
    Topic: Grammar and Syntax

    Constituent analysis (Immediate Constituent or IC analysis) is designed to show how small constituents (or components) in sentences go together to form larger constituents. A sentence is divided into its two immediate constituents, and each of these is divided again, level by level, until the individual words are reached. Constituents are marked with labeled brackets, and the result is usually displayed in a tree diagram — a static representation of the structure of the sentence, which is the most common way to create a visual representation of syntactic structure.

  70. Discuss Chomsky's view of language.
    Topic: Grammar and Syntax

    Chomsky holds that language is a set of sentences generated by a finite set of rules (generative grammar), and that language learning is innate: a child is born with an inborn capacity — the Language Acquisition Device containing Universal Grammar — which makes acquisition rapid and uniform. He distinguishes competence (the speaker's internal knowledge of the rules) from performance (actual use). Chomsky is a rationalist rather than an empiricist, and his transformational rules relate the deep structure of a sentence (the abstract level where all elements determining interpretation are represented) to its surface structure. Deep and surface structures are used as terms in a simple binary opposition.
