  1. What are the goals of ethics in psychology?
    Topic: Core Subjective Topics

    According to the handouts, the goals include the welfare and protection of the individuals and groups with whom psychologists work, so that their mental, physical and psychological health is not harmed. Ethics also educates members, students and the public regarding the ethical standards of the discipline. It provides a framework for guiding decision-making for all psychologists, creating a uniform set of rules. At the same time, this framework allows sufficient flexibility for a variety of approaches, cultural contexts and methods.

  2. What is the APA Ethical Principles of Psychologists and Code of Conduct, and why is a cost-benefit analysis used to judge research?
    Topic: Core Subjective Topics

    The handouts state that APA has provided Ethical Principles of Psychologists and a Code of Conduct which include sections on clinical practice, education, research and publication, and provide a common set of principles and standards applied across settings. The ethics of a given research project are determined through a cost-benefit analysis in which costs are compared with benefits. If the potential costs appear to outweigh any potential benefits, then the research should not proceed. Risks are evaluated according to the probability and magnitude of harm, which may be physical, psychological, economic, social or legal.

  3. What ethical lessons were learned from the Milgram experiment and Zimbardo's prison study?
    Topic: Core Subjective Topics

    In Stanley Milgram's experiment at Yale University on obedience to authority, participants administered what they believed were electric shocks of increasing intensity to a student (an actor) for wrong answers. Although participants heard the student crying in pain, they continued, and most participants evidenced high levels of stress, making the study unethical. In Philip Zimbardo's prison study, college students became prisoners or guards in a simulated prison, the prison became too real, guards used extreme measures, and the study was stopped after only a few days because of distress to participants. The handouts note that such studies led APA to revise its code to require thorough debriefing, define conditions for deception, and give specific guidelines for research with human and animal subjects.

  4. What ethical problems were shown by the Little Albert, Monster and Willowbrook studies?
    Topic: Core Subjective Topics

    In the Little Albert experiment, Watson and Rayner used classical conditioning to cause a phobia of a rat in a small child, and they were not able to diminish the phobia, causing extreme psychological harm and distress that continued after the experiment. The Monster Study by Wendell Johnson gave negative speech therapy to half of 22 orphan children, belittling them for speech imperfections, and many suffered negative psychological impact and retained speech problems throughout their lives. In the Willowbrook experiments, mentally retarded children were intentionally given hepatitis, parents were put under undue pressure to consent without being informed about the nature of the study, involving exploitation of a vulnerable group and withholding information about risks.

  5. Compare utilitarian and deontological ethical theories.
    Topic: Core Subjective Topics

    Utilitarian ethical theories are based on one's ability to predict the consequences of an action, and the choice that yields the greatest benefit to the most people is considered ethically correct, which is why this ethic is also known as consequentialism. Act utilitarianism performs acts that benefit the most people regardless of personal feelings or societal constraints, while rule utilitarianism seeks to benefit the most people through the fairest and most just means and takes law into account. Deontological ethics, in contrast, states that people should adhere to their obligations and duties when making decisions, because upholding one's duty is ethically correct. A limitation of utilitarianism is that outcomes cannot always be predicted, while deontology faces conflicts when a person's duties conflict with each other.

  6. What are rights ethics and virtue ethics?
    Topic: Core Subjective Topics

    In ethical theories based on rights, the rights established by society are protected and given the highest priority, and rights are considered ethically correct and valid since a large population endorses them. A limitation is that society must determine what rights it wants to give its citizens and what its goals and ethical priorities are, so rights theory must be used with another theory. Virtue ethics, presented by Aristotle, judges a person by his or her character rather than by an action that may deviate from normal behaviour, taking morals, reputation and motivation into account. Its weakness is that it does not take into consideration a person's change in moral character.

  7. Explain Principle A: Beneficence and Nonmaleficence and Principle B: Fidelity and Responsibility.
    Topic: Core Subjective Topics

    Principle A guides psychologists to protect the rights and welfare of those with whom they work professionally, including clients and animals in research. Beneficence involves balancing the benefits of treatment against the risks and costs, whereas nonmaleficence means avoiding the causation of harm, and harm should not be disproportionate to the benefit of treatment. Principle B, Fidelity and Responsibility, suggests that psychologists establish relationships of trust, uphold standards of conduct, clarify their professional roles and obligations, and accept appropriate responsibility for their behaviour. They should also manage conflicts of interest that could lead to exploitation or harm and consult, refer to, or cooperate with other professionals to serve the best interests of those with whom they work.

  8. Explain the principles of Integrity, Justice and Respect for People's Rights and Dignity.
    Topic: Core Subjective Topics

    Principle C, Integrity, includes being honest, truthful, accurate and consistent, promoting accuracy and honesty and not stealing, cheating, or engaging in fraud, subterfuge or intentional misrepresentation of fact. Principle D, Justice, states that fairness and justice entitle all persons to access to and benefit from the contributions of psychology and to equal quality in processes, procedures and services, and psychologists must ensure their biases and limits of competence do not lead to unjust practices. Principle E, Respect for People's Rights and Dignity, recognises the inherent worth of all human beings regardless of differences in social status, ethnic origin, gender or capacities. Psychologists should be aware of and respect cultural, individual and role differences, and consider privacy, confidentiality, consent, issues of power and self-determination.

  9. What does the ethical standard on competence require of psychologists?
    Topic: Core Subjective Topics

    Standard 2.1, Boundaries of Competence, states that it is unethical for psychologists to portray themselves as competent in all areas, and if they have not been trained in a specific area they should make an appropriate referral. Psychologists undertake ongoing efforts to develop and maintain their competence, and their work is based upon established scientific and professional knowledge of the discipline. In emergencies, psychologists may provide services to individuals for whom other mental health services are not available even without the necessary training, but these should be discontinued as soon as the emergency has ended or appropriate services are available. When personal problems may interfere with performance, psychologists should take professional consultation or assistance and determine whether to limit, suspend or terminate their duties.

  10. What are multiple relationships and why must psychologists avoid conflicts of interest and exploitation?
    Topic: Core Subjective Topics

    The handouts state that psychologists having more than one relationship with the client beyond just being a client can be difficult to navigate and can lead to conflicts of interest. Under Standard 3.6, psychologists refrain from taking on a professional role when personal, scientific, professional, legal, financial or other interests could reasonably be expected to impair their objectivity, competence or effectiveness, or expose the person or organisation to harm or exploitation. Psychologists also do not exploit persons over whom they have supervisory, evaluative or other authority, such as clients, patients, students, supervisees, research participants and employees. Risks in practice can be reduced by refraining from sexual contacts or other intimate multiple-role relationships with clients.

  11. What is confidentiality and what are its limits?
    Topic: Core Subjective Topics

    Under Standard 4.1, psychologists have a primary obligation to take reasonable precautions to protect confidential information obtained through or stored in any medium, and researchers may assign codes to participants to protect identities. Psychologists discuss the limits of confidentiality at the start of the relationship and when new circumstances arise. Psychologists may disclose confidential information without consent only as mandated by law, or where permitted by law for a valid purpose such as to provide needed professional services, obtain appropriate professional consultations, protect the client, psychologist or others from harm, or obtain payment, with disclosure limited to the minimum necessary. For example, if a client's condition poses a potentially harmful risk to society and other people, the psychologist must disclose this to relevant people such as family or law enforcement agencies.

  12. What are the three basic protections for human subjects and the role of the Institutional Review Board (IRB)?
    Topic: Core Subjective Topics

    Research regulations provide three basic protections: institutional assurances, review by an Institutional Review Board, and informed consent. Institutional assurances are a mechanism to apply research regulations to all human subject research conducted by an institution such as a hospital, school or university. The IRB reviews research proposals involving human participants and their data to ensure they agree with local and international ethical guidelines, guided by the Belmont Report principles of respect for persons, beneficence and justice. An IRB has authority to approve or disapprove research, modify it, conduct continuing reviews, and suspend or terminate approval if ethics are not maintained.

  13. Which categories of research are exempt from further IRB review?
    Topic: Core Subjective Topics

    Exempt research projects meet specific criteria, are initially reviewed by the IRB, but are then exempted from further review, and the handouts list six categories. These include research in educational settings on instructional strategies, curricula or class management, and research using only educational tests, surveys, interviews or observation of public behaviour where identity is kept confidential or disclosure would not place subjects at risk. Other categories are research involving benign behavioural interventions that are brief, harmless and painless, secondary research using identifiable private information or biospecimens that are publicly available or recorded so identity cannot readily be discovered, research on public benefit or service programs, and taste and food quality and consumer acceptance studies using safe ingredients.

  14. What types of risks can research participants face?
    Topic: Core Subjective Topics

    The handouts state that in social and behavioural sciences risk generally falls into categories including invasion of privacy, breach of confidentiality, and psychological harms. Invasion of privacy can occur if a subject's personal information or participation is revealed without knowledge, while breach of confidentiality, the primary source of risk, is disclosure of information outside the research setting that could negatively affect psychological, social or economic status. Psychological harms include depression, stress, guilt and loss of self-esteem, for example when questions about a sensitive topic or past traumatic event make a participant relive painful memories. Social and economic harms may result in embarrassment, loss of employment or criminal prosecution, for example regarding alcohol or drug abuse, mental illness or illegal activities.

  15. How is risk assessed and minimised in research?
    Topic: Core Subjective Topics

    Risk assessment examines what could cause harm, who or what could be harmed and how, considering two main elements: probability of harm, the likelihood that a specific harm might occur, and magnitude of harm, its intensity. Risks also differ according to situation, time, culture and subject population, so researchers must be prepared to respond to distress. When the data itself is the source of risk, it can be protected by collecting data without identifiers, substituting codes for identifiers, removing direct identifiers as soon as possible, and keeping code lists and data files in separate secure locations. Certificates of Confidentiality protect identifiable research information from forced disclosure, for example about substance abuse, sexual attitudes, genetic information or psychological well-being, but do not overrule mandated reporting of child or elder abuse.

  16. What are the essential elements of informed consent in research?
    Topic: Core Subjective Topics

    Valid informed consent must include disclosure of information, competency of the patient or surrogate to make a decision, and the voluntary nature of the decision. Participants must be told the purposes of the research, expected duration, procedures to be followed and which procedures are experimental, as well as foreseeable risks or discomforts and benefits to the subject or others. They must also be told about alternative procedures, how confidentiality of records will be maintained, compensation and medical treatment for research involving more than minimal risk, and whom to contact about the research and their rights. A statement must confirm that participation is voluntary, refusal involves no penalty or loss of benefits, and the subject may discontinue at any time.

  17. What are the special cases in informed consent involving children, waivers and re-consent?
    Topic: Core Subjective Topics

    Children, typically under 17, cannot provide informed consent, so parents must permit treatments or interventions, which is termed informed permission rather than informed consent, and if the child is above 7 years of age child assent is also mandatory. A waiver or alteration of elements of consent is allowed only if the research involves no more than minimal risk, the waiver will not adversely affect rights and welfare, the research could not practicably be carried out without it, and subjects will be provided additional pertinent information after participation. Re-consent may be needed when new risks are observed, procedures or benefits change significantly, the participant's condition worsens, or pediatric participants reach adulthood during a longitudinal study. Documentation may also be waived where signing would link a participant to a risky study or where the study involves minimal to no risk.

  18. When may deception be used in research and what is debriefing?
    Topic: Core Subjective Topics

    Under Standard 8.6, psychologists do not conduct a study involving deception unless the use of deceptive techniques is justified by the study's significant prospective scientific, educational or applied value and effective non-deceptive alternative procedures are not feasible. Psychologists do not deceive prospective participants about research reasonably expected to cause physical pain or severe emotional distress. Any deception that is an integral feature of the design must be explained to participants as early as is feasible, once the study is completed. Debriefing under Standard 8.7 gives participants a prompt opportunity to obtain appropriate information about the nature, results and conclusions of the research, and psychologists take reasonable steps to correct misconceptions and to minimise harm if procedures have harmed a participant.

  19. What informed consent is required in psychological assessment?
    Topic: Core Subjective Topics

    Psychologists obtain informed consent for assessments, evaluations or diagnostic services and ensure consent is informed by explaining the nature and purpose of the procedures they intend to use and how information will be collected and recorded. They clarify reasonably foreseeable risks, adverse effects and possible disadvantages, and explain confidentiality and its limits. They also explain how, where and for how long information will be stored and who will have access to it. Clients are advised that they may participate, decline to participate, or withdraw, and are told the foreseeable consequences of declining or withdrawing.

  20. What ethical issues arise in the use, scoring and interpretation of psychological tests?
    Topic: Core Subjective Topics

    Psychologists use assessment instruments whose validity and reliability have been established for use with members of the population tested, and use methods appropriate to an individual's language preference and competence. When interpreting results, psychologists take into account the purpose of assessment and various test factors and characteristics of the person assessed that might affect judgments or reduce accuracy. Assessment or intervention decisions cannot be based on tests that are outdated for current purposes, and obsolete tests must be revised. Psychologists who offer scoring services accurately describe norms, validity, reliability and applications, follow the scoring manual properly, and retain responsibility for appropriate application, interpretation and use of assessment instruments.

  21. Why must test security be maintained and how is it protected?
    Topic: Core Subjective Topics

    Test security refers to continuous maintenance and control of all test material within a testing program by only those individuals who are qualified or designated to have access to them. Breaches include unauthorised removal of test materials, posting test answers over the Internet, a test taker asking another person to take the test in his or her place, unauthorised copying of copyrighted materials, and hacking into data storage. A breach decreases validity and reliability, leads to ineffective selection procedures and decreased organisational performance, and lowers confidence in psychological assessment. Methods to secure testing programs include developing alternate or parallel test forms, proctoring testing sites, physically and electronically securing test materials and results, monitoring response data for abnormal patterns, and copyrighting all testing materials.

  22. What is cultural competence and what are its stages?
    Topic: Core Subjective Topics

    Cultural competence is defined as the capacity to increase understanding of cultural differences, the ability to acknowledge cultural assumptions and biases, and willingness to make changes in thought and behaviour to address those biases. It is an ongoing process of examination and change, not a goal attained once, and a culturally competent program shows knowledge of or sensitivity to the first language of clients, respects cultural practices, and uses treatment methods reflecting culture-specific values. The continuum includes six stages: cultural destructiveness, cultural incapacity, cultural blindness, cultural pre-competence, cultural competence, and cultural proficiency. At cultural proficiency, all cultures are held in high esteem and the psychologist continuously advocates for cultural competence.

  23. What are primary, secondary and tertiary credentials in psychology?
    Topic: Core Subjective Topics

    Credentials exist as a tangible indicator of accomplishment in a given field, with a purpose to measure the competence of the holder, and the handouts distinguish three levels. Primary credentials are earned over time by direct contact with trained instructors, based on longitudinal samples of behaviour with supervision and direct observation, for example coursework, graduate training programs or internships, evidenced by transcripts, degrees or certificates. Secondary credentials use primary credentials as prerequisites and incorporate other elements, and include licensing and certification as well as recognition by reputable specialised certification boards. Tertiary credentials are based solely on evidence that primary and secondary credentials have been obtained, for example membership in professional organisations such as APA divisions or listing in the National Register of Health Service Providers in Psychology.

  24. What is psychotherapy and what must a competent psychotherapist do?
    Topic: Core Subjective Topics

    Psychotherapy, or talk therapy, is a way to help people with a broad variety of mental illnesses and emotional difficulties, and can help eliminate or control troubling symptoms so a person can function better and increase well-being and healing. Its functions include a healing function, alleviation of emotional suffering through understanding and support, an educational function promoting growth, insight and maturation, and a technological function applying techniques to change or modify behaviour. To provide competent treatment, therapists only provide services for which they are qualified, accurately represent their credentials and qualifications, and keep up on current information in the field, especially in specialty areas. They should also seek counseling when they have personal issues.

  25. What ethical issues arise in psychotherapy regarding informed consent, group therapy and sexual intimacies?
    Topic: Core Subjective Topics

    When obtaining informed consent to therapy, psychologists inform clients as early as feasible about the nature and anticipated course of therapy, fees, involvement of third parties and limits of confidentiality, and for treatments whose techniques have not been established they also inform clients of the developing nature, potential risks, alternatives and voluntary nature of participation. In group therapy, psychologists describe at the outset the roles and responsibilities of all parties and limits of confidentiality, ensure voluntary participation and conduct proper screening, because hazards include pressures toward cohesion, confrontation and threats to confidentiality. Psychologists do not engage in sexual intimacies with current therapy clients, or with individuals they know to be close relatives, guardians or significant others of current clients. They also do not engage in sexual intimacies with former clients for at least two years after cessation or termination of therapy.

  26. When and how should therapy be terminated?
    Topic: Core Subjective Topics

    Psychologists terminate therapy when it becomes reasonably clear that the client no longer needs the service, is not likely to benefit, or is being harmed by continued service. They may also terminate when threatened or otherwise endangered by the client or another person with whom the client has a relationship. Except where precluded by the actions of clients, psychologists provide pre-termination counseling and suggest alternative services as appropriate. Guidelines include not abandoning a client without warning, being compassionate, clear and direct, never blaming the client, and if necessary referring the client to a highly qualified therapist who specialises in their issues, and a termination letter can record the end of therapy and its reasons.

  27. How should termination be handled when the client has progressed, is unhappy, or repeatedly does not show up?
    Topic: Core Subjective Topics

    When a client achieves clear and specific goals, for example when postpartum depression symptoms go into remission by mutual agreement, therapists should reflect on the client's growth, discuss what went well and what could have been better, and give guidelines for when it might be appropriate to return to therapy. With an unhappy client, therapists should avoid defensiveness, listen to feedback, explain why therapy must end without accusations or blame, and take careful notes if accused of wrongdoing. When a client repeatedly no-shows, makes unreasonable demands or presents practical concerns, the therapist should send a termination notice using the client's preferred method of communication and present any practical issue in non-stigmatising, objective terms, respecting the client's decision if they are not willing to continue.

  28. What are the APA guidelines for record keeping?
    Topic: Core Subjective Topics

    APA provides guidelines to educate psychologists and provide a framework for decisions regarding professional record keeping, and records benefit both client and psychologist through documentation of treatment plans, services provided and client progress. A record includes identifying data, contact information, fees and billing information, documentation of informed consent or assent, presenting complaint and diagnosis, plan for services, and for each contact the date, duration, type of service and nature of intervention. Psychologists have professional and ethical responsibility for maintenance and retention of records, strive to maintain accurate, current and pertinent records, and take reasonable steps to maintain confidentiality of information arising from service delivery. Records should be updated and logically organised for efficient retrieval and continuity of care, retained for the period required by legal, regulatory, institutional and ethical requirements, and electronic records must be protected for security, integrity and confidentiality.
