  1. Define Human-Computer Interaction (HCI). What is its main goal? (Marks: 2)
    Topic: HCI Basics & Human Factors

    Human-Computer Interaction is the discipline concerned with the design, evaluation and implementation of interactive computing systems for human use, and with the study of the major phenomena surrounding them. Its main goal is to develop interactive systems that are usable — effective, efficient, safe and satisfying for the people who use them.

  2. Briefly explain the three input-output channels through which a human interacts with a computer. (Marks: 3)
    Topic: HCI Basics & Human Factors

    (1) Vision: the dominant channel; the eye receives light and perceives size, shape, colour, brightness, pattern and movement. (2) Hearing: the ear receives sound — pitch, loudness and timbre — and can process information in parallel with vision, e.g. warning sounds. (3) Touch (haptic): receptors in the skin and joints sense pressure, texture, position and movement of the fingers and limbs, giving feedback about key presses and pointing actions. The human's output is produced by motor actions such as pressing keys and moving a mouse.

  3. Describe the three types of human memory. Give the capacity and duration of each. (Marks: 5)
    Topic: HCI Basics & Human Factors

    (1) Sensory memory: buffers attached to each sense (iconic for vision, echoic for hearing, haptic for touch). It has a very large capacity but information lasts only a fraction of a second to about 2 seconds before it decays or is passed on. (2) Short-term (working) memory: a scratch-pad for temporary recall. It holds about 7 ± 2 chunks of information (Miller's law), is accessed rapidly (about 70 ms) and information decays in roughly 15-30 seconds unless rehearsed. (3) Long-term memory: the repository of all our knowledge. Its capacity is vast, access is slower (about one-tenth of a second), and forgetting happens slowly; it stores episodic memory (events) and semantic memory (facts and concepts).

  4. Differentiate between slips and mistakes in human error. (Marks: 3)
    Topic: HCI Basics & Human Factors

    Slips are unintentional errors that occur during the execution of a correct intention — the plan is right but the action goes wrong, e.g. pressing the wrong key or clicking the wrong button. Mistakes occur through conscious deliberation: the intention or plan itself is wrong, e.g. a user deliberately deletes a file believing it is the correct one, or applies the wrong rule to a situation. In short, slips are failures of execution; mistakes are failures of intention/formation of the goal.

  5. What is a mental model? Why is it important in interface design? (Marks: 3)
    Topic: HCI Basics & Human Factors

    A mental model is the internal representation or understanding that a user has of how a system works, built up from experience, training and the image the system presents. It is important because users act according to their mental model, not according to how the system actually works. If the designer's conceptual model is not communicated clearly through the system image, the user's mental model will be wrong and the user will make errors and find the system hard to learn and use.

  6. Define attention. Differentiate between focused attention and divided attention. (Marks: 3)
    Topic: HCI Basics & Human Factors

    Attention is the process of selecting things to concentrate on, at a point in time, from the range of possibilities available. Focused attention is the ability to concentrate on a single stimulus or task while ignoring others, e.g. reading a document in a noisy room. Divided attention is the ability to split attention between two or more tasks or stimuli at the same time, e.g. driving a car while listening to the radio. Divided attention is possible only when the tasks are well practised or use different channels.

  7. Explain the three types of reasoning: deductive, inductive and abductive, with examples. (Marks: 5)
    Topic: HCI Basics & Human Factors

    (1) Deductive reasoning derives a logically necessary conclusion from given premises: if the premises are true, the conclusion must be true. Example: 'If it is raining then the ground is wet; it is raining, therefore the ground is wet.' (2) Inductive reasoning generalises from particular cases we have seen to all cases. Example: having seen many white swans, we conclude all swans are white. It is useful but unreliable, because the next case may break the pattern. (3) Abductive reasoning goes from an observed event back to the action or state that caused it, i.e. it explains events. Example: the computer screen is blank, so the user concludes the machine has crashed. Abduction is also unreliable, because the explanation chosen may be wrong (the screen may only be asleep).

  8. What is the Problem Space Framework? State its main strength as a model of human problem solving. (Marks: 3)
    Topic: HCI Basics & Human Factors

    The Problem Space Framework (Newell and Simon) views problem solving as a search through a space of knowledge states: the solver starts in an initial state, applies operators (actions) to move from state to state, and tries to reach the goal state, using sub-goals where necessary. Its main strength is that it operates within the constraints of the human processing system — it models problem solving in a way that respects the limits of human memory and processing, which makes it a realistic model of how people actually solve problems.

  9. Define usability. (Marks: 2)
    Topic: Usability & Evaluation

    Usability is the extent to which a product can be used by specified users to achieve specified goals with effectiveness, efficiency and satisfaction in a specified context of use (ISO 9241). In simple words, a system is usable if it is easy to learn, efficient to use, easy to remember, causes few errors and is subjectively satisfying.

  10. Explain the usability goals of an interactive system. (Marks: 5)
    Topic: Usability & Evaluation

    (1) Effectiveness: a very general goal — how good a system is at doing what it is supposed to do. (2) Efficiency: the way a system supports users in carrying out their tasks, i.e. the resources (time, effort) expended in achieving the goals. (3) Safety: protecting the users from dangerous conditions and from undesired situations, e.g. preventing serious errors. (4) Utility: the extent to which the system provides the right kind of functionality so that users can do what they need to or want to do. (5) Learnability: how easy a system is to learn to use. (6) Memorability: how easy a system is to remember how to use, once it has been learned.

  11. Differentiate between usability goals and user experience goals. (Marks: 3)
    Topic: Usability & Evaluation

    Usability goals are objective: they are concerned with whether the system meets specific usability criteria such as effectiveness and efficiency, and they can be assessed through measurable performance. User experience goals are subjective: they describe how the experience of using the system feels to the user — e.g. satisfying, enjoyable, engaging, pleasurable, exciting, motivating, aesthetically pleasing and emotionally fulfilling. User experience goals are therefore less clearly defined and cannot be measured as directly as usability goals.

  12. What is the Usability Engineering Lifecycle? Who proposed it? (Marks: 3)
    Topic: Usability & Evaluation

    The Usability Engineering Lifecycle was proposed by Deborah Mayhew. It is a structured approach to developing usable systems in three broad stages: (1) Requirements analysis — building user profiles, doing task analysis and setting measurable usability goals; (2) Design, testing and development — designing at increasing levels of detail and carrying out usability testing at each level, iterating the design; (3) Installation — collecting feedback from actual use. Its key features are setting quantitative usability goals early and testing iteratively against them.

  13. Differentiate between formative evaluation and summative evaluation. (Marks: 3)
    Topic: Usability & Evaluation

    Formative evaluation is carried out during the design and development of a system, while it can still be changed. Its purpose is to find usability problems and improve the design, so it is done repeatedly with prototypes. Summative evaluation is carried out on a finished (or nearly finished) product. Its purpose is to assess whether the product meets the specified usability goals or benchmarks, and to compare it with competing products or standards.

  14. What is heuristic evaluation? Who proposed it? (Marks: 3)
    Topic: Usability & Evaluation

    Heuristic evaluation is a usability inspection method proposed by Jakob Nielsen (with Rolf Molich). A small set of evaluators (typically 3-5) independently examine the interface and judge whether it complies with recognised usability principles called heuristics — such as visibility of system status, match between the system and the real world, user control and freedom, consistency and standards, and error prevention. The evaluators' findings are then combined. It is quick and cheap, needs no users, and finds a large proportion of usability problems.

  15. What is usability testing? Explain how a usability test is conducted. (Marks: 5)
    Topic: Usability & Evaluation

    Usability testing is the evaluation of a system by having representative users perform representative tasks with it, while their performance and reactions are observed and recorded. A typical test is conducted as follows: (1) Define the goals of the test and the tasks/scenarios to be performed. (2) Select participants who represent the intended users. (3) Prepare the test material — task instructions, questionnaires and recording setup. (4) Conduct the sessions: the user performs the tasks, often thinking aloud, while observers record completion time, errors and difficulties; no help is given. (5) Measure satisfaction afterwards with questionnaires or interviews. (6) Analyse the results — where users failed or were slow, and why — and report the problems with recommendations for redesign.

  16. What is interaction design? (Marks: 2)
    Topic: Interaction Design & Models

    Interaction design is designing interactive products to support the way people communicate and interact in their everyday and working lives. In other words, it is about creating user experiences that enhance and augment the way people work, communicate and interact.

  17. Explain Norman's execution-evaluation cycle (the seven stages of action). (Marks: 5)
    Topic: Interaction Design & Models

    Norman's model describes an interaction as a cycle of seven stages. Execution side: (1) Establishing the goal — the user decides what to achieve. (2) Forming the intention — deciding to act to achieve the goal. (3) Specifying the action sequence — working out the exact operations needed. (4) Executing the action — actually performing the operations. Evaluation side: (5) Perceiving the state of the world — sensing what has happened. (6) Interpreting the state of the world — making sense of the feedback. (7) Evaluating the state of the world — comparing the outcome with the original goal and intention. The cycle then repeats with a new or refined goal until the task is complete.

  18. Explain the gulf of execution and the gulf of evaluation. (Marks: 3)
    Topic: Interaction Design & Models

    The gulf of execution is the difference between the intentions/goals of the user and the actions that the system allows the user to perform — it measures how easy it is to act on the system. The gulf of evaluation is the difference between the state of the system as presented to the user and the user's expectations — it measures how easy it is to perceive and interpret whether the goal has been achieved. A well-designed interface narrows (bridges) both gulfs, e.g. by providing direct, meaningful controls and clear feedback.

  19. What does GOMS stand for? Explain its four components. (Marks: 3)
    Topic: Interaction Design & Models

    GOMS stands for Goals, Operators, Methods and Selection rules; it is a model of human task performance given by Card, Moran and Newell. (1) Goals: what the user wants to achieve. (2) Operators: the basic perceptual, motor and cognitive actions the user performs, e.g. pressing a key or moving the mouse. (3) Methods: procedures made of sequences of operators and sub-goals that accomplish a goal. (4) Selection rules: the rules by which the user chooses among the competing methods available for a goal. GOMS is used to predict how long an expert user will take to perform a task.

  20. What are personas in goal-directed design? Explain the types of personas. (Marks: 5)
    Topic: Interaction Design & Models

    A persona is a fictional, archetypal representation of a class of real users, created from research into users' behaviour patterns, goals, skills, attitudes and environment, and given a name, face and personal details so that designers can design for a specific 'person' instead of a vague 'user'. Types of personas: (1) Primary persona: the main target of the design; the interface is designed primarily for this persona, and it cannot be satisfied by an interface designed for another persona. (2) Secondary persona: also uses the product; most of its needs are met by the primary interface but it has additional specific needs that must be addressed without disturbing the primary design. (3) Served persona: not an actual user of the product, but directly/indirectly affected by its use, e.g. a manager who receives reports produced by others using the system. (4) Supplemental persona: its needs are a combination of primary and secondary needs and it is fully satisfied by the primary interface. (5) Negative (exclusionary) persona: a user type for whom the product is explicitly NOT being designed.

  21. What are scenarios in goal-directed design? Name their types. (Marks: 3)
    Topic: Interaction Design & Models

    A scenario is a narrative story describing a specific instance of use: a persona, in a specific context, with specific goals, carrying out specific tasks with the product. Scenarios let designers explore and communicate how the product will be used before it is built. In goal-directed design three types are used: (1) Context scenarios — broad, day-in-the-life stories used to explore the product concept and derive requirements. (2) Key path scenarios — detailed descriptions of the persona's primary, most frequent interactions, used to refine the design. (3) Validation scenarios — 'what-if' stories covering less frequent and edge-case situations, used to test the design.

  22. What is meant by affordance? Give an example. (Marks: 2)
    Topic: Interaction Design & Models

    Affordance refers to the perceived and actual properties of an object that suggest how it can be used. For example, a button on a screen looks raised and clickable, so it affords pushing/clicking; a door handle affords pulling, while a flat plate affords pushing. Good affordances make the correct action obvious without instructions.

  23. Explain the phases of the goal-directed design process. (Marks: 5)
    Topic: Interaction Design & Models

    (1) Research: understand the domain and the users through interviews, observation and literature review. (2) Modelling: synthesise the research into models — above all personas, and workflow models of how work is done. (3) Requirements definition: using context scenarios, identify each persona's goals and needs and define the product requirements. (4) Interaction framework: define the overall structure of the product — its form factor, screens/views, functional groups and the basic behaviour of the interface. (5) Refinement: work out the design in full detail — detailed behaviour, look and feel, and information design. (6) Development support: support the developers during implementation, adjusting the design where technical constraints require it, without losing the design vision.

  24. Explain the waterfall model of the software lifecycle. Why is it not suitable for interactive systems? (Marks: 3)
    Topic: Design Process, Standards & Guidelines

    The waterfall model is a linear, sequential lifecycle model in which each phase — requirements, design, implementation, testing and maintenance — must be completed before the next phase can start. It is not suitable for interactive systems because the users' needs and the usability of the interface can only be discovered by trying designs with users; in the waterfall model feedback comes only after implementation, when changes are very expensive. Interactive system design therefore needs an iterative cycle of prototyping and evaluation, which the waterfall model does not allow.

  25. Differentiate between standards and guidelines in HCI. (Marks: 3)
    Topic: Design Process, Standards & Guidelines

    Standards are authoritative requirements produced by national or international bodies (e.g. ISO 9241). They are usually specific, measurable and sometimes legally enforceable, and they tend to deal with matters such as health, safety and performance. Guidelines are recommendations and best-practice advice drawn from research and expert experience (e.g. Shneiderman's golden rules or the Smith and Mosier guidelines). They are more general and flexible than standards, cover a wider range of design issues, and must be interpreted and applied by the designer rather than obeyed literally.

  26. Explain the star lifecycle model of Hartson and Hix. (Marks: 5)
    Topic: Design Process, Standards & Guidelines

    The star lifecycle is a model of interactive system development proposed by Hartson and Hix. The activities of development — task/functional analysis, requirements specification, conceptual and formal design, prototyping, and implementation — are arranged as the points of a star, and evaluation is placed at the centre of the star. The designer may begin at any point and move from any activity to any other, but every move passes through evaluation at the centre: whatever is produced is evaluated before work proceeds. The model therefore emphasises that interactive development is evaluation-driven and iterative rather than a fixed sequence of phases.

  27. List and briefly explain Shneiderman's eight golden rules of interface design. (Marks: 5)
    Topic: Design Process, Standards & Guidelines

    (1) Strive for consistency: similar situations should use similar sequences of actions, terminology and layout. (2) Enable frequent users to use shortcuts: abbreviations, function keys and macros speed up expert work (cater to universal usability). (3) Offer informative feedback: every user action should produce visible system feedback. (4) Design dialogs to yield closure: sequences of actions should have a beginning, middle and end, with feedback confirming completion. (5) Offer error prevention and simple error handling: design so errors cannot occur, and where they do, give simple instructions to correct them. (6) Permit easy reversal of actions: undo facilities relieve anxiety and encourage exploration. (7) Support internal locus of control: make the user feel in charge of the system, not responding to it. (8) Reduce short-term memory load: keep displays simple and avoid making the user remember information from one screen to the next.

  28. What is prototyping? Differentiate between low-fidelity and high-fidelity prototypes. (Marks: 3)
    Topic: Design Process, Standards & Guidelines

    Prototyping is building a preliminary, partial version of a system so that design ideas can be explored and evaluated with users early, before the real system is built. A low-fidelity prototype is rough and cheap — e.g. paper sketches or storyboards; it is quick to produce and change, and is used to test concepts and layout. A high-fidelity prototype looks and behaves much like the final product, usually built in software; it is used to test detailed design, but it costs more time and effort and users may mistake it for the finished system.

  29. Explain the main interaction styles used in interactive systems. (Marks: 5)
    Topic: User Interface Elements & Interaction Styles

    (1) Command language: the user types commands (e.g. DOS commands). It is powerful and flexible for experts but hard to learn and remember. (2) Menus: the user selects from a list of options displayed by the system; it replaces recall with recognition, so it is easy for novices, but can be slow for experts. (3) Form fill-in: the user fills fields on a screen form, as on a paper form; suitable for data entry. (4) Direct manipulation: the user manipulates on-screen objects directly with a pointing device (dragging, resizing icons and windows); actions are visible, incremental and reversible. (5) Natural language: the user speaks or types in ordinary language; it needs no special learning but suffers from ambiguity. (Question-and-answer dialogs, in which the system asks questions and the user answers, are another simple style used in wizards.)

  30. What is direct manipulation? State its features/advantages. (Marks: 3)
    Topic: User Interface Elements & Interaction Styles

    Direct manipulation is an interaction style, identified by Shneiderman, in which the user acts directly on visible objects on the screen instead of typing commands. Its features are: (1) continuous representation of the objects of interest; (2) physical actions and labelled buttons instead of complex syntax; (3) rapid, incremental, reversible operations whose effect on the object is immediately visible. Its advantages are that it is easy to learn and remember, produces fewer errors, encourages exploration, and gives users a high level of satisfaction and sense of control.

  31. What is a WIMP interface? Name its four components. (Marks: 2)
    Topic: User Interface Elements & Interaction Styles

    WIMP stands for Windows, Icons, Menus and Pointers. It is the standard graphical user interface paradigm: Windows are separate areas of the screen used for different tasks or documents; Icons are small pictures representing objects and actions; Menus offer choices of operations and services on the screen; and the Pointer is the cursor, moved by a pointing device such as a mouse, used to select and manipulate objects.

  32. Explain any three types of menus used in graphical interfaces. (Marks: 3)
    Topic: User Interface Elements & Interaction Styles

    (1) Pull-down menus: when the user selects a title in the menu bar at the top of the screen, the menu drops down and the user selects an item from it. (2) Pop-up menus: menus that appear at the pointer's position when invoked (e.g. by a right-click); they are context-sensitive and remain hidden until needed. (3) Cascading menus: a menu item with an arrow opens a further sub-menu to its side, giving a hierarchy of choices. (Pie menus, in which items are arranged in a circle around the pointer, are another type.)

  33. State Fitts' law and explain its implication for interface design. (Marks: 3)
    Topic: User Interface Elements & Interaction Styles

    Fitts' law predicts the time taken to move to and select a target: MT = a + b log2(D/W + 1), where D is the distance from the starting point to the target and W is the width (size) of the target. The design implication is that the time to acquire a target decreases if the target is larger and/or closer. Therefore frequently used controls should be made large and placed close to where the pointer usually is; controls at the edges and corners of the screen are fastest to reach because they behave as if infinitely large; and small, distant targets should be avoided.

  34. What are icons? State the characteristics of good icons. (Marks: 2)
    Topic: User Interface Elements & Interaction Styles

    Icons are small pictures or images displayed on the screen that represent objects, functions or actions, which the user can select to invoke the associated operation. Good icons are: familiar and easy to recognise; clear and unambiguous in meaning; consistent in style with the other icons in the system; clearly distinguishable from one another; of suitable size; and, where the meaning is not obvious, supported by a text label or tooltip.

  35. Explain the different types of keyboards: QWERTY, Dvorak, alphabetical and chord keyboards. (Marks: 3)
    Topic: User Interface Elements & Interaction Styles

    (1) QWERTY keyboard: the standard layout, inherited from typewriters; almost all users are trained on it, so it remains dominant despite not being optimal. (2) Dvorak keyboard: a layout designed for speed and efficiency, placing the most frequently used letters under the strongest fingers; it can give faster typing, but the cost of retraining keeps it rare. (3) Alphabetical keyboard: keys arranged in alphabetical order; it looks easier for beginners, but in practice gives no advantage and is slower for experienced users. (4) Chord keyboard: only a few keys, and a character is entered by pressing several keys at once (in combination, like a piano chord). Chord keyboards are very small and fast after training, but difficult to learn; they are used in specialised devices.

  41. Suppose you are a product designer and have to create a product that satisfies a broad audience. What user-centered design issues arise during product development, and how do personas resolve them?
    Topic: Interaction Design & Models

    Being a product designer keeping in the mind before we going to create a product that satisfy board audience in self referential design which are given : Personas To create a product that must satisfy a broad audience of users, logic tells you to make it as broad in its functionality as possible to accommodate the most people. This logic, however, is flawed. The best way to successfully accommodate a variety of users is to design for specific types of individuals with specific needs. Personas also resolve three User-Centered design issues that arise during product development: The elastic user Self-referential design Design edge cases The elastic user Although satisfying the user is goal, the term user causes trouble when applied to specific design problems and contexts. Its imprecision makes it unusable as a design tool—every person on a product team has his own conceptions of the user and what the user needs. Self-referential design Self-referential design occurs when designers or developers project their own goals, motivations, skills, and mental models onto a product's design. Most "cool" product designs fall into this category: the audience doesn't extend beyond people like the designer, which is fine for a narrow range of products and completely inappropriate for most others. Similarly, programmers apply self-referential design when they create implementation-model products. They understand perfectly how it works and are comfortable with such products. Few non- programmers would concur. Design edge cases Another syndrome that personas help prevent is designing for edge cases—those situations that might possibly happen, but usually won't for the target personas. Naturally, edge cases must be programmed for, but they should never be the design focus. Personas provide a reality check for the design.
