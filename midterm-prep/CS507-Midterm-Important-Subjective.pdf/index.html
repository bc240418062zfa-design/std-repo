  3. DSS are used to take help in Semi structured decisions. What does DSS use as support in order to facilitate Semi Structured Decision making?
    Topic: Decision Making & Decision Support Systems

    A DSS assists management decision making by combining data, sophisticated analytical models and user-friendly software into a single powerful system that can support semi-structured or unstructured decision making. Unstructured problems are novel, non-routine and have no predefined algorithms or solutions. DSS combine data with models to produce various alternative scenarios that can be used for making choices. In large organizations decision making is inherently a group process, and DSS can be designed to facilitate group decision making.

  10. Why Software development life cycle is considered as an on-going process?
    Topic: Systems Development & SDLC

    The Software Development Life Cycle (SDLC) is considered an on-going (cyclic) process because a system is never truly finished. After implementation, the system enters the maintenance phase, where errors are corrected and new requirements keep arising due to changes in the business environment, technology and user needs. Each change or enhancement again passes through the same phases — analysis, design, development and implementation — so the cycle keeps repeating throughout the entire life of the system.

  13. Define an information system and list its basic components.
    Topic: Information Systems Basics & System Concepts

    An information system is a set of interrelated components that collect (input), process, store and distribute information to support decision making, coordination and control in an organization. Basic components: 1. Hardware — the physical equipment (computers, printers, etc.) 2. Software — the programs that process the data 3. Data — the raw facts stored and processed by the system 4. Procedures — the defined set of instructions/rules for using the system 5. People — the users and specialists who operate and use the system

  14. Differentiate between data and information.
    Topic: Information Systems Basics & System Concepts

    Data is the raw, unprocessed facts and figures (e.g. numbers, names, marks obtained by students) which have no meaning on their own. Information is the processed/organized form of data that is meaningful and useful for decision making (e.g. the merit list or result summary prepared from students' marks). In short, data is the input to a system and information is its useful output.

  15. Differentiate between an open system and a closed system.
    Topic: Information Systems Basics & System Concepts

    An open system interacts with its environment: it takes inputs from the environment and depends on external resources and data (e.g. market information) for decision making. A closed system does not interact with its environment; it depends on internal resources and data for decision making rather than the external environment. In practice, most real-world systems are relatively open systems.

  16. What are primary, secondary and tertiary sources of information? Give one example of each.
    Topic: Information & Its Sources

    Primary sources present information in its original form, neither interpreted nor condensed nor evaluated by other writers — e.g. original research reports, diaries, interviews, official records. Secondary sources present information that has been interpreted, condensed or evaluated from primary sources — e.g. textbooks, review articles, commentaries. Tertiary sources contain information in which the information from secondary sources has been digested — reformatted and condensed into a convenient, easy-to-read form, and they list primary and secondary resources of a subject area — e.g. indexes, bibliographies, encyclopedias and directories.

  17. List and briefly explain the phases of the decision making process.
    Topic: Decision Making & Decision Support Systems

    The decision making process (Simon's model) consists of the following phases: 1. Intelligence — searching the environment for conditions calling for a decision; the problem is identified and defined. 2. Design — inventing, developing and analyzing possible courses of action (alternatives). 3. Choice — selecting a particular course of action from the available alternatives. 4. Implementation — putting the selected course of action into practice. 5. Monitoring — evaluating the outcome of the implemented decision and taking corrective action if required.

  18. Differentiate between structured, semi-structured and unstructured decisions.
    Topic: Decision Making & Decision Support Systems

    Structured decisions are repetitive and routine; the procedures to follow can be specified in advance, e.g. inventory re-ordering — they are supported by TPS/MIS. Semi-structured decisions are those where only some phases of the decision process are structured; they combine standard procedures with the manager's judgment, e.g. deciding a marketing budget — they are supported by DSS. Unstructured decisions are novel, non-routine and important; there may be several 'right' answers and no precise/cut-and-dried method to get a right answer, e.g. launching a new product line — they are made mostly at the strategic level and supported by ESS/DSS.

  19. Differentiate between TPS, MIS and DSS.
    Topic: Types of Information Systems

    TPS (Transaction Processing System) records and processes the routine day-to-day business transactions (e.g. sales, payroll) and serves the operational level of the organization. MIS (Management Information System) converts TPS data into summarized, routine reports (e.g. monthly sales reports) that help middle managers in monitoring and controlling the business; it supports structured decisions. DSS (Decision Support System) combines data, sophisticated analytical models and user-friendly software to help managers make semi-structured and unstructured decisions, e.g. through what-if analysis.

  21. What is organizational culture? How does it affect the information systems of an organization?
    Topic: Organizations & Management

    Organizational culture is the specific collection of values and norms that are shared by people and groups in an organization and that control the way they interact with each other and with stakeholders outside the organization. It affects information systems because a new system must fit the culture of the organization: if the system conflicts with the shared values, work habits and power structure of the employees, they may resist or reject it, causing system failure; a culture that supports sharing and openness, on the other hand, makes system adoption and use more successful.

  22. Compare the Waterfall model and the Spiral model of system development.
    Topic: Systems Development & SDLC

    Waterfall model: development proceeds in a linear, sequential manner — each phase (analysis, design, coding, testing, maintenance) is completed fully before the next begins; it is simple but rigid, and going back to a previous phase is difficult. Spiral model: development is a series of short waterfall cycles, each producing an early prototype representing a part of the entire project; it is iterative and risk-driven, allowing user feedback and changes at every cycle, but it is more complex and costly to manage.

  32. What do you understand by Rational-Man Model?
    Topic: Decision Making & Decision Support Systems

  33. Differentiate between Empire Building and Garbage Can Model.
    Topic: Decision Making & Decision Support Systems

    Empire Building: Empire building is a business term that refers to a common problem in larger organizations, in which managers attempt to gather more administrative and financial power. Power can only be shared in an organization with key employees in terms of their responsibilities & functions. Such employees are the key players in the decision making process. Hence decisions taken in an organization are a result of collective efforts of the leaders involved. This model is also termed as Political Model. Garbage Can Model: This model states that organizations are not rational. Decisions made are largely on accidental basis. Hence wrong solutions may be applied to wrong problems in an organization and critical mistakes may occur. Information systems should be designed to support and assist in relevant decision making, instead of making unrelated and wrong decisions.

  52. Identify the basic purpose of DFD and identify the steps of designing a DFD for any process.
    Topic: Systems Development & SDLC

    A data flow diagram (DFD) is a graphical representation of the "flow" of data through an information system. The purpose of data flow diagrams is to provide a linking bridge between users and systems developers. Steps to draw a Data Flow Diagram: 1. Start from the context diagram. Identify the parent process and the external entities with their net inputs and outputs. 2. Place the external entities on the diagram. Draw the boundary. 3. Identify the data flows needed to generate the net inputs and outputs to the external entities. 4. Identify the business processes to perform the work needed to generate the input and output data flows. 5. Connect the data flows from the external entities to the processes. 6. Identify the data stores. 7. Connect the processes and data stores with data flows. 8. Apply the Process Model Paradigm to verify that the diagram addresses the processing needs of all external entities. 9. Apply the External Control Paradigm to further validate that the flows to the external entities are correct. 10. Continue to decompose to the nth level DFD. Draw all DFDs at one level before moving to the next level of decomposing detail.

Key Areas of Feasibility: • Technical Feasibility — is the available technology sufficient to support the proposed project? Can the technology be acquired or developed? (Response times, volume of transactions which can be processed within the given time, capacity to hold files or records of a certain size, number of users supported) • Operational Feasibility — compliance and adjustability with the way organization works with attitude to change or chains of command. Reduction in job stress, job satisfaction, quality of output by employees.

  59. Identify different phases of Spiral model.
    Topic: Systems Development & SDLC

    There are usually four distinct phases of the spiral model software development approach: 1. Determine objectives, alternatives, constraints. 2. Evaluate alternatives, identify and resolve risks — This involves Risk analysis and Prototyping 3. Develop, verify next-level product 4. Plan next phase

  60. Identify the Characteristics of the Incremental Model.
    Topic: Systems Development & SDLC

    Characteristics of the Incremental Model: • The system development is broken into many mini development projects • Partial systems are successively built to produce a final total system. • Highest priority requirements tackled early on. • Once an incremented portion is developed, requirements for that increment are frozen.

  61. Differentiate between Incremental and iterative models with the help of one example each.
    Topic: Systems Development & SDLC

    Incremental vs. Iterative: These sound similar, and sometimes are equated but there is a subtle difference: • Incremental: add to the product at each phase • Iterative: re-do the product at each phase Example — Building a House: • Incremental: Starts with a modest house, keep adding rooms and upgrades to it. • Iterative: The design/construction map of the house is amended and improved and repeated until all the requirements are fulfilled.

  62. Why prototypes are used?
    Topic: Systems Development & SDLC

    In many fields, there is great uncertainty as to whether a new design will actually do what is desired. New designs often have unexpected problems. A prototype is built to test the function of the new design before starting production of a product. Building the full design is often expensive and can be time-consuming. A prototype allows manufacturers to rapidly and inexpensively test the parts of the design that are most likely to have problems, solve those problems, and then build the full design.
