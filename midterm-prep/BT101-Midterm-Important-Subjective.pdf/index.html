  1. Define ecosystem.
    Topic: Ecosystem Basics & Habitats

    An ecosystem is a community of living organisms in conjunction with the nonliving components of their environment, interacting as a system. These biotic and abiotic components are linked together through nutrient cycles and energy flows.

  2. What factors control an ecosystem?
    Topic: Ecosystem Basics & Habitats

    Ecosystems are dynamic entities controlled both by external and internal factors. External factors, such as climate and the parent material that forms the soil, control the overall structure of an ecosystem and the way things work within it, but are not themselves influenced by the ecosystem.

  3. What are the characteristics of a habitat?
    Topic: Ecosystem Basics & Habitats

    Characteristics of habitats are the abiotic and biotic factors that influence a plant. Among the abiotic factors are chemical and physical values like light, climate, soil composition, soil consistency and exposition to the sun.

  4. What is habitat distribution?
    Topic: Ecosystem Basics & Habitats

    Habitat distribution models (HDMs) predict where suitable habitat may exist for a species, based in part on verified observations of the species in their natural environments.

  5. Define commensalism.
    Topic: Species Interactions

    One species gains benefit while the other species is neither benefited nor harmed. Example: remora fish and sharks.

  6. What is mutualism?
    Topic: Species Interactions

    Mutualism is a long-term relationship between individuals of different species in which both individuals get benefit. Example: hermit crab and sea anemone.

  7. Define parasitism.
    Topic: Species Interactions

    One species gains benefit and the other species is harmed. There is intimate contact (the parasite feeds off the host); parasites usually do not kill the host (parasitoids do). Parasitism enhances the fitness of the parasite but reduces the fitness of the host.

  8. What are the major categories of parasites?
    Topic: Species Interactions

    Protozoa, helminths, and ectoparasites.

  9. What are the types of parasites?
    Topic: Species Interactions • 5 marks marks

    1. Microparasites: reproduce inside the host — bacteria, viruses. 2. Macroparasites: release juveniles outside the host, e.g. trematodes. 3. Endoparasites: e.g. liver fluke, tapeworm, nematodes. 4. Ectoparasites: e.g. ticks, mites, fleas, mosquito, lice.

  10. Explain coevolution with the example of the aphid and the ant.
    Topic: Species Interactions

    Coevolution means that two or more species living in the same habitat reciprocally affect each other's evolution. Ants protect aphids from parasites and predators, and aphids produce honeydew, which is a source of nutrition for ants. They are specifically coevolved for ants.

  11. What are the types of population growth?
    Topic: Population Ecology

    Exponential growth and logistic growth.

  12. What is exponential growth?
    Topic: Population Ecology

    Exponential growth is a specific way that a quantity may increase over time. It occurs when the instantaneous rate of change (that is, the derivative) of a quantity with respect to time is proportional to the quantity itself.

  13. What is logistic growth?
    Topic: Population Ecology • 5 marks marks

    In logistic population growth, the carrying capacity of the environment affects population growth. There is environmental resistance, and the growth rate gets smaller as the population approaches the carrying capacity. Environmental factors affecting population growth are climate, food, space and competition.

  14. What is the effect of population growth and size in terms of logistic growth?
    Topic: Population Ecology

    In logistic growth the population first grows almost exponentially while resources are plentiful. As the population size approaches the carrying capacity (K) of the environment, environmental resistance — shortage of food and space, competition and accumulation of waste — increases, so the growth rate slows down. When the population size reaches K, growth stops and the population stabilises, giving an S-shaped (sigmoid) curve; if the size rises above K, the population declines back towards K.

  15. What is carrying capacity (K)?
    Topic: Population Ecology

    The maximum population size of the species that the environment can sustain indefinitely. It is the environment's maximal load; population size decreases above carrying capacity.

  16. Name the limiting factors that control population (factors affecting the rate of growth and size of a population).
    Topic: Population Ecology

    Limiting factors regulate population size: rate of birth, rate of death/mortality, dispersal, food resources, space to grow, and environmental factors.

  17. Write a detail note on limiting factors.
    Topic: Population Ecology

    A limiting factor is a resource or environmental condition which limits the growth, distribution or abundance of an organism or population within an ecosystem. A limiting factor restricts organisms from occupying their fundamental niche and results instead in the fulfilment of their actual or realized niche. Limiting factors include a low food supply and lack of space; they can lower birth rates, increase death rates, or lead to emigration.

  18. What are the causes of overpopulation?
    Topic: Population Ecology

    The leading causes are: poverty (believed to be the leading cause of overpopulation), poor contraceptive use, child labour, reduced mortality rates, fertility treatment, immigration, lack of water, and lower life expectancy.

  19. Write the properties of K-selected and r-selected species, and the difference between them.
    Topic: Population Ecology • 5 marks marks

    r-selected species have high growth rates, typically exploit less crowded ecological habitats, produce many offspring, and have a relatively low probability of surviving to adulthood; their high reproductive rate supports their survival, especially in unstable environments. K-selected species produce few 'expensive' offspring and live in stable environments. The two evolutionary 'strategies' are termed r-selection, for species that produce many 'cheap' offspring and live in unstable environments, and K-selection, for species that produce few 'expensive' offspring and live in stable environments.

  20. What are the different forms of energy in an ecosystem?
    Topic: Energy Flow, Food Chains, Food Webs & Decomposition

    When it comes to the flow of energy in ecosystems, there are two types of organisms: producers and consumers. Plants are a common example of producers in all populations. They are able to convert carbon dioxide into oxygen and glucose, a common sugar consumed by most organisms.

  21. Describe the flow of energy in an ecosystem.
    Topic: Energy Flow, Food Chains, Food Webs & Decomposition

    The cycle of energy is based on the flow of energy through different trophic levels in an ecosystem. At the first trophic level, primary producers use solar energy to produce organic material through photosynthesis. The herbivores at the second trophic level use the plants as food, which gives them energy.

  22. What are the feeding levels in a food chain?
    Topic: Energy Flow, Food Chains, Food Webs & Decomposition

    Food energy passes through a sequence of feeding (trophic) levels. Example: plants → grasshopper → frog → snake → hawk. Many plant species are eaten by insects, and many insect species are eaten by frogs. The levels are: Level 1 — primary producer; Level 2 — primary consumer; Level 3 — secondary consumer; Level 4 — tertiary consumer; Level 5 — apex predator.

  23. What is a food web?
    Topic: Energy Flow, Food Chains, Food Webs & Decomposition

    A food web (or food cycle) is the natural interconnection of food chains and a graphical representation (usually an image) of what-eats-what in an ecological community. Another name for a food web is a consumer-resource system. Food webs are complexly interconnected food chains, and they represent only a tiny portion of the complexity of real ecosystems. The fundamental purpose of food webs is to describe feeding relationships among species in a community: the second trophic level consists of herbivores (the first consumers), and the remaining trophic levels include carnivores that consume animals at trophic levels below them.

  24. Define trophic cascade.
    Topic: Energy Flow, Food Chains, Food Webs & Decomposition

    Species-level cascade: a subset of the food web (dynamic) is impacted by a change in population numbers. Community-level cascades: a change in population numbers has a dramatic effect on the entire food web, e.g. distribution of plant biomass.

  25. What is the top-down effect?
    Topic: Energy Flow, Food Chains, Food Webs & Decomposition

    In the top-down effect, the effect flows down through a trophic chain — from the apex predator to the lower trophic levels.

  26. What is a consumer-resource system?
    Topic: Energy Flow, Food Chains, Food Webs & Decomposition • 5 marks marks

    A consumer-resource system is a direct or indirect balance between resources and consumption. Predators indirectly increase plant growth: they prevent overgrazing by suppressing herbivores. The net effect of direct and indirect relations is called trophic cascades.

  27. What are consumers in an ecosystem?
    Topic: Energy Flow, Food Chains, Food Webs & Decomposition • 5 marks marks

    A consumer is a category that belongs within the food chain of an ecosystem. It refers predominantly to animals. Consumers are unable to make their own energy, and instead rely on the consumption and digestion of producers or other consumers, or both, to survive.

  28. Describe the decomposition process and its stages.
    Topic: Energy Flow, Food Chains, Food Webs & Decomposition

    Decomposition is the process by which organic substances are broken down into simpler organic matter. The process is a part of the nutrient cycle and is essential for recycling the finite matter that occupies physical space in the biosphere. Animals, such as worms, also help decompose the organic materials. Decomposition plays an important role in the nitrogen and carbon cycles. Its stages are: 1. Autolysis — breaking down of tissues by the body's own internal chemicals and enzymes. 2. Putrefaction — breakdown of tissues by bacteria, releasing compounds such as cadaverine and putrescine, which cause the decaying odour.

  29. Which organisms are the primary decomposers, and what is their role?
    Topic: Energy Flow, Food Chains, Food Webs & Decomposition

    Fungi are the primary decomposers. They release digestive enzymes by their hyphae, absorb organic matter and release CO2.

  30. What are the different types of autotrophs?
    Topic: Producers, Autotrophs & Productivity

    Photoautotrophs: organisms that get the energy to make organic materials from sunlight. Chemoautotrophs: organisms that obtain chemical energy through oxidation. Examples of autotrophs include plants and green algae.

  31. What is the role of autotrophs?
    Topic: Producers, Autotrophs & Productivity

    An autotroph is an organism that serves as a primary producer in a food chain. Autotrophs obtain energy and nutrients by harnessing sunlight through photosynthesis (photoautotrophs) or, more rarely, obtain chemical energy through oxidation (chemoautotrophs) to make organic substances from inorganic ones.

  32. What are heterotrophs?
    Topic: Producers, Autotrophs & Productivity

    A heterotroph is an organism that cannot manufacture its own food by carbon fixation and therefore derives its intake of nutrition from other sources of organic carbon, mainly plant or animal matter. In the food chain, heterotrophs are secondary and tertiary consumers.

  33. What is the net primary productivity of wetlands, and how do rainfall and flooding affect it?
    Topic: Producers, Autotrophs & Productivity

    Net primary production (NPP) is highest in wetlands receiving nutrient enrichment or with high nutrient turnover. The degree, duration and periodicity of flooding affect wetland productivity more than rainfall or temperature. Drainage can promote enhanced productivity by increasing nutrient mineralization.

  34. What is meant by the formation of inorganic plant and algal nutrition?
    Topic: Producers, Autotrophs & Productivity

    Chemicals such as nitrogen and phosphorus that stimulate the growth of plants and algae; they are harmful in large concentrations.

  35. Name the layers of the atmosphere.
    Topic: Atmosphere & Air Pollution

    The atmosphere is comprised of layers based on temperature. These layers are the troposphere, stratosphere, mesosphere and thermosphere. A further region at about 500 km above the Earth's surface is called the exosphere.

  36. What is the height of the stratosphere at the equator and at the poles?
    Topic: Atmosphere & Air Pollution

    20 km (66,000 ft; 12 mi) at the equator, 10 km (33,000 ft; 6.2 mi) at midlatitudes, and 7 km (23,000 ft; 4.3 mi) at the poles.

  37. What are the heights of the upper and lower boundaries of the mesosphere?
    Topic: Atmosphere & Air Pollution

    The mesosphere is the layer directly above the stratosphere and directly below the thermosphere. Its lower boundary is 50 to 65 kilometres and its upper boundary is around 85 to 100 kilometres; it is called 'near space'. It extends from about 50 to 85 km above our planet, and temperature decreases with height throughout the mesosphere.

  38. Write three properties of the thermosphere.
    Topic: Atmosphere & Air Pollution

    Temperature increases with altitude due to absorption of highly energetic solar radiation; temperature can rise to 1700 °C or more; the highly diluted gas in this layer can reach 2,500 °C during the day.

  39. What was the composition of the first atmosphere?
    Topic: Atmosphere & Air Pollution

    The first atmosphere, about 4 billion years ago, had a composition of gases like the solar nebula (a disc of dust and gases): hydrogen, methane, helium, ammonia and water vapours.

  40. In which atmospheric layer does conventional aviation take place, and what does that layer contain?
    Topic: Atmosphere & Air Pollution

    Conventional aviation takes place in the troposphere. It contains 75% of the atmosphere's mass and 99% of the total mass of water vapour and aerosols.

  41. What are the benefits of the atmosphere?
    Topic: Atmosphere & Air Pollution

    The Earth's atmosphere protects and sustains the planet's inhabitants by providing warmth and absorbing harmful solar rays. In addition to containing the oxygen and carbon dioxide which living things need to survive, the atmosphere traps the sun's energy and wards off many of the dangers of space.

  42. What is tropospheric ozone?
    Topic: Atmosphere & Air Pollution

    Tropospheric ozone (O3) is a short-lived photochemical pollutant formed in the atmosphere during complex chemical reactions between motor vehicle and other emissions in combination with sunlight. Tropospheric (ground-level) ozone is found in the troposphere, the layer of air closest to the Earth's surface; ozone does not naturally occur at harmful levels in the troposphere, whereas the ozone layer higher up protects plants, animals and us by blocking the most harmful rays of the sun.

  43. Name the ozone-depleting substances.
    Topic: Atmosphere & Air Pollution • 5 marks marks

    Halocarbon refrigerants, solvents, propellants in aerosols, chlorofluorocarbons (CFCs), hydrochlorofluorocarbons (HCFCs), methyl bromide, carbon tetrachloride and methyl chloroform.

  44. What are the major types of air pollutants?
    Topic: Atmosphere & Air Pollution

    The EPA identified six major types of air pollutants deemed especially harmful to human health: carbon monoxide, sulfur dioxide, nitrogen oxides, ozone, particulate matter and lead. Since the Clean Air Act of 1970 and the identification of these pollutants, air pollution has decreased markedly.

  45. What are the stages of the water cycle?
    Topic: Water Cycle, Water Pollution & Wastewater • 5 marks marks

    There are four main stages in the water cycle: evaporation, condensation, precipitation and collection. Evaporation: warmth from the sun causes water from oceans, lakes, streams, ice and soils to rise into the air and turn into water vapour (gas). Condensation: the water vapour cools and turns back into liquid droplets, forming clouds. Precipitation: water falls back to the Earth as rain, snow or other forms. Collection: the fallen water gathers in oceans, lakes, rivers and underground stores, from where the cycle begins again.

  46. Define percolation, transpiration and groundwater.
    Topic: Water Cycle, Water Pollution & Wastewater

    Percolation (infiltration) is an important process: rain water soaks into (infiltrates) the ground, into the soil and underlying rock layers, and some of this water ultimately returns to the surface at springs or in low spots downhill. Transpiration is the loss of water vapour from the leaves of plants to the atmosphere. Groundwater is the water present beneath Earth's surface in soil pore spaces and in the fractures of rock formations; a unit of rock or an unconsolidated deposit is called an aquifer when it can yield a usable quantity of water.

  47. What are the categories of water pollution?
    Topic: Water Cycle, Water Pollution & Wastewater

    Nutrient pollution (some wastewater, fertilizers and sewage contain high levels of nutrients); surface water pollution; oxygen depleting; microbiological; suspended matter; chemical water pollution; and oil spillage.

  48. What are the major types of water pollution? Write a short note on water (H2O) pollution.
    Topic: Water Cycle, Water Pollution & Wastewater

    Surface water pollution: when harmful substances invade water bodies such as oceans, rivers, seas and lakes. Oxygen depletion: when too much biodegradable material promotes microorganism growth, and the microorganisms use almost all the oxygen in the water.

  49. Write a detail note on water pollution.
    Topic: Water Cycle, Water Pollution & Wastewater • 5 marks marks

    Water pollution is the contamination of water bodies, usually as a result of human activities. Water bodies include, for example, lakes, rivers, oceans, aquifers and groundwater. Releasing inadequately treated wastewater into natural water bodies can lead to degradation of aquatic ecosystems.

  50. What are the sources of wastewater?
    Topic: Water Cycle, Water Pollution & Wastewater

    Wastewater is a byproduct of domestic, industrial, commercial or agricultural activities.

  51. Write a note on the treatment of wastewater, including primary and secondary treatment.
    Topic: Water Cycle, Water Pollution & Wastewater • 5 marks marks

    Wastewater treatment is the process of converting wastewater — water that is no longer needed or is no longer suitable for use — into water that can be discharged back into the environment. Wastewater is formed by a number of activities including bathing, washing, using the toilet, and rainwater runoff. Primary treatment consists of temporarily holding the sewage in a quiescent basin where heavy solids can settle to the bottom while oil, grease and lighter solids float to the surface; the settled and floating materials are removed and the remaining liquid may be discharged or subjected to secondary treatment. Secondary treatment is a treatment process for wastewater (or sewage) to achieve a certain degree of effluent quality by using a sewage treatment plant with physical phase separation to remove settleable solids and a biological process to remove dissolved and suspended organic compounds.

  52. What are the sources of water for treatment and day-to-day use?
    Topic: Water Cycle, Water Pollution & Wastewater

    In general, these resources can be classified as groundwater or surface water, and commonly include underground aquifers, creeks, streams, rivers and lakes.

  53. How can pollution be classified?
    Topic: Pollution: Classification, Land, Noise, Light & Mining

    Pollution can be classified by its physical nature, by its source, by its recipient, by the sector affected or by its effects. Pollution may be in the form of a gas, liquid, solid or energy. There are different types of pollution: water pollution, air pollution, solid waste pollution and noise pollution.

  54. What are the main causes of land pollution?
    Topic: Pollution: Classification, Land, Noise, Light & Mining

    Deforestation, agriculture, industry, mining, landfills and waste, urbanization, and other causes.

  55. What are the effects of noise pollution?
    Topic: Pollution: Classification, Land, Noise, Light & Mining

    Noise pollution can damage physiological and psychological health. It causes high blood pressure, stress-related illness, sleep disruption, hearing loss and productivity loss. It can also cause memory loss, severe depression and panic attacks.

  56. What is light pollution, and what are its environmental effects?
    Topic: Pollution: Classification, Land, Noise, Light & Mining • 5 marks marks

    Light pollution, also known as photopollution, is excessive, misdirected or obtrusive artificial light. It is a major side-effect of urbanization and is blamed for compromising health, disrupting ecosystems and spoiling aesthetic environments. Its effects on animals include negative impacts on plant and animal physiology, sleep, foraging behaviour, predation, reproduction and animal navigation; it can alter competitive interactions and change predator–prey relations.

  57. What is the role of mines in the ecosystem?
    Topic: Pollution: Classification, Land, Noise, Light & Mining

    Mining operations often contaminate the soil with toxic heavy metals and acids. Acids can lower the pH of the soil, preventing plants and soil microorganisms from thriving, and can also react with various minerals in the soil that are required by plants, such as calcium and magnesium.

  58. What is the role of mine wastewater?
    Topic: Pollution: Classification, Land, Noise, Light & Mining

    Mine wastewater, including acid mine drainage, carries acids, heavy metals and suspended solids from mining sites into nearby soil and water bodies. It lowers the pH of the water, is toxic for aquatic plants and animals, contaminates drinking-water and irrigation sources, and so damages the whole aquatic ecosystem; it must be treated before it is discharged.

  59. How does CO2 come from the burning of gasoline?
    Topic: Pollution: Classification, Land, Noise, Light & Mining

    20% and 13% of carbon dioxide emissions come from the burning of gasoline in the internal combustion engines of cars and trucks.

  60. What is the carbon cycle?
    Topic: Nutrient Cycles: Carbon & Nitrogen

    The carbon cycle is the biogeochemical cycle by which carbon is exchanged among the biosphere, pedosphere, geosphere, hydrosphere and atmosphere of the Earth. Carbon is the main component of biological compounds as well as a major component of many minerals such as limestone.

  61. What are the sources of nitrogen?
    Topic: Nutrient Cycles: Carbon & Nitrogen

    Lightning, inorganic fertilizers, nitrogen fixation, animal residues, crop residues and organic fertilizers.

  62. Name the microorganisms that take part in nitrogen fixation.
    Topic: Nutrient Cycles: Carbon & Nitrogen

    Two kinds of nitrogen-fixing bacteria are recognized. The first kind, the free-living (nonsymbiotic) bacteria, includes the cyanobacteria (or blue-green algae) Anabaena and Nostoc, and genera such as Azotobacter, Beijerinckia and Clostridium.

  63. What is hibernation?
    Topic: Adaptations & Animal Behaviour

    Hibernation involves decreased metabolism and lowered body temperature, and may last for weeks or months. Examples: rodents, shrews.

  64. What is aestivation?
    Topic: Adaptations & Animal Behaviour

    Aestivation is survival in an extended period of drying; it avoids temperature damage and dehydration. Examples: insects, frog, turtle.

  65. What adaptations do animals have to avoid dehydration?
    Topic: Adaptations & Animal Behaviour

    Behavioural adaptations: nocturnal mode of life, living in burrows and hiding. Physiological adaptation: osmoregulation.

  66. What are the camouflage strategies?
    Topic: Adaptations & Animal Behaviour

    1. Crypsis — objects hide and are hard to see. 2. Mimicry — disguising themselves as something else. 3. Motion dazzle — using visual illusions. Some strategies protect without hiding.

  67. How is mimicry different from camouflage?
    Topic: Adaptations & Animal Behaviour

    The first difference is that mimicry involves morphological, behavioural and physiological adaptations, whereas camouflage only involves morphological characteristics. Second, in most cases, camouflage relates to the blending of the animal or plant with its environment.

  68. What is aposematism?
    Topic: Adaptations & Animal Behaviour • 5 marks marks

    Aposematism is the advertising by an animal to potential predators that it is not worth attacking or eating. This unprofitability may consist of any defences which make the prey difficult to kill and eat, such as toxicity, venom, foul taste or smell, sharp spines, or aggressive nature.

  69. What is foraging?
    Topic: Adaptations & Animal Behaviour

    Foraging is searching for wild food resources. It affects an animal's fitness because it plays an important role in an animal's ability to survive and reproduce. Foraging theory is a branch of behavioural ecology that studies the foraging behaviour of animals in response to the environment where the animal lives.

  70. What are the stages of foraging?
    Topic: Adaptations & Animal Behaviour

    These stages form a foraging cycle: 1. Search, 2. Assessment, 3. Capture, 4. Handling.

  71. What are the feeding strategies? Describe grazers, browsers and the types of grazing.
    Topic: Adaptations & Animal Behaviour • 5 marks marks

    The major feeding strategies are: 1. Grazers — feed on plants such as grasses, soft flowering plants (forbs) and algae; the plants are not killed. Examples: deer and snails. Graminivory is feeding primarily on grass, specifically 'true' grasses in the family Poaceae; examples: horses, cattle, hippopotamus, grasshoppers. 2. Browsers — feed on non-grass plant material: leaves, soft shoots, twigs, fruits and woody plants such as shrubs. Examples: goats, deer, giraffe, elephants. Over-browsing results from the introduction of herbivores, low productivity of native plants and less predation pressure.

  72. Name the types of omnivores with examples.
    Topic: Adaptations & Animal Behaviour

    Frugivores: wolves and orangutans. Insectivores: swallows and pink fairy armadillos. Granivores: large ground finches and mice.

  73. What is habitat destruction?
    Topic: Habitat Destruction, Fragmentation, Extinction & Invasive Species

    Habitat destruction is the process by which a natural habitat becomes incapable of supporting its native species. In this process, the organisms that previously used the site are displaced or destroyed, reducing biodiversity.

  74. What is habitat fragmentation? (Loss and fragmentation of habitat)
    Topic: Habitat Destruction, Fragmentation, Extinction & Invasive Species

    Habitat destruction and fragmentation is a process that describes the emergence of discontinuities (fragmentation) or the loss (destruction) of the environment inhabited by an organism. A habitat needs to be an intact body; least interference is required to protect the niches of organisms, and many species are too sensitive to tolerate changes in their niches, e.g. insects.

  75. What is the effect of fragmentation on species?
    Topic: Habitat Destruction, Fragmentation, Extinction & Invasive Species

    Minor fluctuations in climate, resources or other factors that would be unremarkable and quickly corrected in large populations can be catastrophic in small, isolated populations. Thus fragmentation of habitat is an important cause of species extinction. Additionally, habitat fragmentation leads to edge effects.

  76. What are the causes of species extinction?
    Topic: Habitat Destruction, Fragmentation, Extinction & Invasive Species

    Humans can cause extinction of a species through overharvesting, pollution, habitat destruction, introduction of invasive species (such as new predators and food competitors), overhunting, and other influences. Explosive, unsustainable human population growth is an essential cause of the extinction crisis.

  77. What are invasive species?
    Topic: Habitat Destruction, Fragmentation, Extinction & Invasive Species • 5 marks marks

    Colonization, a natural process by which a species expands its geographic range, occurs in many ways — for example, seed dispersal by birds, lowering of sea levels joining two isolated land masses, and flooding. Types of invasive species: 1. Animals, 2. Plants, 3. Pathogens. They cause an imbalance in the natural environment and outcompete the native species.

  78. What is overexploitation?
    Topic: Habitat Destruction, Fragmentation, Extinction & Invasive Species

    Overexploitation, also called overharvesting, refers to harvesting a renewable resource to the point of diminishing returns. Continued overexploitation can lead to the destruction of the resource.

  79. What is the fundamental niche?
    Topic: Niche, Succession, Evolution & Biological Clocks

    The fundamental niche is the entire niche that a species is capable of using, based on its physiological tolerance limits and resource needs.

  80. What is a (standard) niche?
    Topic: Niche, Succession, Evolution & Biological Clocks

    A niche is the role and position that a species occupies in its environment — how it meets its needs for food and shelter, how it survives and how it reproduces. A species' niche includes all of its interactions with the biotic and abiotic factors of its environment: what it eats, where it lives, when it is active, and how it responds to environmental conditions.

  81. What are the types of succession?
    Topic: Niche, Succession, Evolution & Biological Clocks

    There are two main types of succession: primary and secondary. Primary succession is the series of community changes which occur on an entirely new habitat which has never been colonized before, for example a newly quarried rock face or sand dunes.

  82. Who proposed the theory of natural selection, and when?
    Topic: Niche, Succession, Evolution & Biological Clocks

    Darwin, influenced by Malthus's ideas, proposed the groundbreaking 'Theory of Natural Selection' in 1859.

  83. What are biological clocks? Name their types.
    Topic: Niche, Succession, Evolution & Biological Clocks

    Biological clocks regulate the diurnal distribution and sleep–wake cycle, annual reproduction and migration. Their types are: circadian rhythms — biological cycles that occur about every twenty-four hours (sleep follows a circadian rhythm); infradian rhythms — biological cycles that take longer than twenty-four hours; and ultradian rhythms — biological cycles that occur more than once a day.

  84. What is global warming? What are its effects?
    Topic: Global Warming, Acid Rain & Biomagnification • 5 marks marks

    Global warming is the long-term rise in the average temperature of the Earth's climate system. It is a major aspect of climate change, and has been demonstrated by direct temperature measurements and by measurements of various effects of the warming. Global warming and climate change are often used interchangeably.

  85. What is acid rain?
    Topic: Global Warming, Acid Rain & Biomagnification • 5 marks marks

    Acid rain is rain or any other form of precipitation that is unusually acidic, meaning that it has elevated levels of hydrogen ions (low pH). Acid rain is caused by emissions of sulfur dioxide and nitrogen oxide, which react with the water molecules in the atmosphere to produce acids.

  86. What is biomagnification?
    Topic: Global Warming, Acid Rain & Biomagnification

    Biomagnification, also known as bioamplification or biological magnification, is any concentration of a toxin, such as pesticides, in the tissues of tolerant organisms at successively higher levels in a food chain.

  87. What is the role of the University of Wisconsin–Madison in restoration ecology?
    Topic: Restoration Ecology

    The University of Wisconsin–Madison Arboretum has pioneered restoration ecology. The restoration of its prairie was at an early stage in November 1935, and a picture taken at approximately the same location today shows the restored prairie.

  88. Define food pyramid.
    Topic: Energy Flow, Food Chains, Food Webs & Decomposition • 2 marks marks

    A food pyramid is a graphical representation of the flow of energy through different trophic levels of an ecosystem.

  89. Define trophic level with an example.
    Topic: Energy Flow, Food Chains, Food Webs & Decomposition

    A trophic level is the position in an ecosystem that an organism occupies in relation to primary sources of energy and the food chain. The first trophic level is always composed of primary producers that convert either solar or chemical energy into biomass.

  90. Define detritivores with examples.
    Topic: Energy Flow, Food Chains, Food Webs & Decomposition

    Detritivores are heterotrophs that obtain their nutrition by feeding on detritus. The detritus they consume includes decomposing plant and animal parts, as well as fecal matter. These organisms play an important role in all ecosystems by getting rid of decaying organic matter left behind by other organisms. In food webs, detritivores commonly play the role of decomposers. Examples: earthworms, which eat rotten plant leaves and other debris; dung beetles, which eat feces; millipedes, woodlice, slugs and sea stars.

  91. Write a short note on survivorship curves.
    Topic: Population Ecology • 5 marks marks

    In a survivorship curve, the Y-axis is a logarithmic plot of the number of survivors and the X-axis is a linear plot of age. There are three kinds of survivorship curves. Type I (Convex): populations survive to an old age and then die rapidly; environmental factors are unimportant in influencing mortality and the potential life span is reached, e.g. humans. Type II (Diagonal): there is a constant probability of death throughout life; mortality is independent of age, e.g. birds and rodents. Type III (Concave): there is high juvenile mortality due to environmental factors and the low resistance of juveniles, while mortality is lower in adults, e.g. fish.

  92. What is reproductive potential? What are the major factors affecting it?
    Topic: Population Ecology • 5 marks marks

    Reproductive potential is the ability of individuals to reproduce under ideal or optimum conditions. (Exponential growth cannot occur indefinitely.) Factors influencing reproductive potential are: (1) number of offspring produced; (2) the likelihood of survival to reproductive age; (3) duration of the reproductive period; (4) length of time it takes to reach maturity.

  93. What is the difference between habitat and niche?
    Topic: Niche, Succession, Evolution & Biological Clocks • 2 marks marks

    The actual place where an organism lives is called its habitat or environment. A habitat is an ecological or environmental area that is inhabited by a particular species of animal, plant or other type of organism. Each organism in a community confronts the challenge of survival in a different way and occupies a specific functional role and place called its niche. The niche an organism occupies is the total of all the ways it uses the resources of its environment.

  94. Differentiate between prokaryotes and eukaryotes.
    Topic: Ecosystem Basics & Habitats • 3 marks marks

    Prokaryotes: A prokaryote is a single-celled organism that lacks a membrane-bound nucleus, mitochondria, or any other membrane-bound organelle. In prokaryotes, all the intracellular water-soluble components (proteins, DNA and metabolites) are located together in the cytoplasm enclosed by the cell membrane, rather than in separate cellular compartments. Prokaryotes are divided into two domains, Archaea and Bacteria. Eukaryotes: A eukaryote is any organism whose cells contain a nucleus and other organelles enclosed within membranes. The defining feature of eukaryotic cells is the membrane-bound nucleus, which contains the genetic material and is enclosed by the nuclear envelope. Eukaryotic cells also contain other membrane-bound organelles such as mitochondria and the Golgi apparatus; plants and algae also contain chloroplasts. Eukaryotic organisms may be unicellular or multicellular.

  95. Define weathering and also enlist its types.
    Topic: Ecosystem Basics & Habitats • 5 marks marks

    Weathering breaks down and loosens the surface minerals of rock so they can be transported away by agents of erosion such as water, wind and ice. There are two types of weathering: (1) Mechanical weathering — the disintegration of rock into smaller and smaller fragments. Its major types are thermal expansion, abrasion and impact, exfoliation or pressure release, frost weathering, salt-crystal growth, and plant and animal activities. (2) Chemical weathering — the breakdown of rock by chemical reactions.

  96. What is the function of the cell membrane?
    Topic: Ecosystem Basics & Habitats

    The cell membrane is selectively permeable to ions and organic molecules and controls the movement of substances in and out of cells. The basic function of the cell membrane is to protect the cell from its surroundings. It consists of the phospholipid bilayer with embedded proteins.

  97. What are the cell membrane and the cell wall?
    Topic: Ecosystem Basics & Habitats

    The cell membrane is a biological membrane which is semi-permeable; it is present in all cells and controls the movement of substances in and out of the cell. The cell wall is the outermost covering of the cell. It is present in bacteria, fungi, algae and plant cells, and is absent in animal cells and protozoa.

  98. Write a note on soil horizon.
    Topic: Ecosystem Basics & Habitats

    A soil horizon is a layer generally parallel to the soil surface (soil crust), whose physical characteristics differ from the layers above and beneath it. Each horizon forms as a result of soil-forming processes and differs in texture, colour and composition from the neighbouring horizons.

  99. Write a note on the types of soil horizon.
    Topic: Ecosystem Basics & Habitats • 5 marks marks

    O-horizon: This layer generally forms above the mineral soil or occurs in an organic soil profile. The 'O' stands for organic matter; it is a surface layer dominated by large amounts of organic material derived from dead plant and/or animal residue in varying stages of decomposition. It is most prominent in forested areas, where it is commonly called the forest floor, and is generally absent in grassland regions. A-horizon: The A horizon is the topmost mineral horizon, often referred to as the 'topsoil'. It contains enough partially decomposed (humified) organic matter to give the soil a darker colour than the lower horizons, and largely consists of minerals (sand, silt and clay) with appreciable amounts of organic matter. B-horizon: B horizons form below an O or A horizon and are commonly referred to as the 'subsoil'. In humid regions, B horizons are the layers of maximum accumulation of materials such as silicate clays, iron (Fe) and aluminium (Al) oxides, and organic material. C-horizon: The C horizon (parent material) lies below the B horizon. It is little affected by soil-forming processes and is the least weathered horizon; it is the unconsolidated, loose parent material underlying the solum (A and B horizons).

  100. What is a manatee?
    Topic: Ecosystem Basics & Habitats • 2 marks marks

    The manatee is a large aquatic mammal (a herbivore) that feeds on plants. It is a primary consumer and eats about 150 pounds of plants each day.

  101. Define eutrophication.
    Topic: Water Cycle, Water Pollution & Wastewater

    Eutrophication, or hypertrophication, is the process by which a body of water becomes overly enriched with minerals and nutrients, which induce excessive growth of algae. This process may result in oxygen depletion of the water body.

  102. What are the services of marine water?
    Topic: Water Cycle, Water Pollution & Wastewater

    The main goods and services provided by marine ecosystems are: resilience and resistance; disturbance prevention; nutrient cycling; gas and climate regulation; bioremediation of waste; food provision; raw materials, including ornamental resources; and leisure.

  103. Enlist some effects of ozone depletion.
    Topic: Atmosphere & Air Pollution

    Effects of ozone depletion include: increased UV penetration; skin cancer in humans — basal and squamous cell carcinomas have been strongly linked to UVB exposure; malignant melanoma, a lethal skin cancer related to UVA and UVB; cortical cataracts linked with UVB exposure; increased tropospheric ozone; and increased production of vitamin D linked with UVB. Cyanobacteria are sensitive to UV, so crops are indirectly affected.

  104. Define ectotherms and enlist their characteristics.
    Topic: Adaptations & Animal Behaviour • 5 marks marks

    An ectotherm is an organism in which internal physiological sources of heat are of relatively small or quite negligible importance in controlling body temperature. Such organisms (for example, frogs) rely on environmental heat sources, which permit them to operate at very economical metabolic rates. The ectotherms include the fishes, amphibians, reptiles and invertebrates. Many reptiles regulate their body temperature by basking in the sun or seeking shade when necessary. In contrast, endotherms rely largely on heat from internal metabolic processes. Characteristics of ectotherms: (1) low metabolic rate; (2) they generate body heat from the external environment; (3) body insulation is very poor.

  105. Define torpor with an example.
    Topic: Adaptations & Animal Behaviour • 2 marks marks

    Torpor is a state of decreased physiological activity in an animal, usually by a reduced body temperature and metabolic rate. Torpor is a mechanism that is under physiological control, and animals can regain their normal body activities in a short period of time after remaining in a state of torpor. Torpor enables animals to survive periods of reduced food availability. A torpor bout may last days to weeks in a hibernator, or less than 24 hours, as in 'daily torpor'. Examples: animals that undergo daily torpor include birds (even tiny hummingbirds) and many marsupial species.

  106. What is the difference between plankton and phytoplankton?
    Topic: Producers, Autotrophs & Productivity • 2 marks marks

    Plankton: Microscopic swimming and free-floating organisms in water are termed as planktons. Saltwater and freshwater life zones contain several major types of organisms, one of which consists of weakly swimming, free-floating plankton. Phytoplankton: In oceans, lakes and slow-moving streams, phytoplanktons are the major producers. Phytoplanktons are simply microscopic floating plants. Fish and other aquatic animals eat the phytoplankton as it floats through the water, so phytoplankton is a great source of food for a wide range of aquatic creatures including whales, shrimp, snails and jellyfish.

  107. Differentiate between endemic and indigenous species with examples.
    Topic: Habitat Destruction, Fragmentation, Extinction & Invasive Species

    Endemic species: An endemic species is one whose habitat is restricted to a particular area. The term could refer to an animal, a plant, a fungus, or even a microorganism. Endemic species are often endangered. Example: the blind Indus dolphin is only found in the Indus river of Pakistan. Indigenous species: A species is defined as indigenous to a given region or ecosystem if its presence in that region is the result of only natural processes, with no human intervention. Every natural organism has its own natural range of distribution in which it is regarded as indigenous. Example: the snow leopard is found in Pakistan, Nepal, India, Russia and some other countries of Central Asia.
