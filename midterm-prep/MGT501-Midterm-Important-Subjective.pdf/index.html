  1. Define human resource management and describe its main functions. (Marks: 5)
    Topic: Introduction to HRM, Management & the Organization

    Human resource management (HRM) is the process of acquiring, training, appraising and compensating employees, and of attending to their labor relations, health and safety, and fairness concerns. It is the part of management that is concerned with the 'people' dimension of an organization. Its main functions are: (1) Human resource planning — determining the number and types of employees the organization will need. (2) Recruitment and selection — attracting a pool of qualified candidates and choosing the best suited persons for jobs. (3) Training and development — improving employees' skills for their present and future jobs. (4) Performance appraisal — evaluating employees' performance against set standards. (5) Compensation and benefits — designing fair pay, incentives and benefit packages. (6) Safety, health and employee relations — providing a safe workplace and maintaining good relations with employees and unions. Together these functions ensure that the organization has competent, motivated people to achieve its goals.

  2. Differentiate between personnel management and human resource management. (Marks: 5)
    Topic: Introduction to HRM, Management & the Organization

    Personnel management and human resource management are closely related — historically the two terms referred to the same thing, and 'personnel management' gradually evolved into 'human resource management' as the field matured. However, the modern view shows some differences. Personnel management was traditional and routine in nature: it focused on administrative work such as record keeping, hiring, payroll and enforcing rules, and it reacted to problems as they arose. Employees were treated mainly as a cost or a factor of production. Human resource management is strategic and proactive: employees are treated as valuable resources and assets in which the organization invests. HRM is linked with the overall strategy and goals of the organization, emphasizes employee development, participation and commitment, and plans for future needs instead of only reacting to present problems. In short, personnel management was concerned with managing people at work in a narrow administrative sense, while HRM manages people as the most important resource for organizational success.

  3. Distinguish between line authority and staff authority. Give examples from an organization. (Marks: 5)
    Topic: Introduction to HRM, Management & the Organization

    Line authority is the authority to give orders and make decisions within the chain of command. A line manager directs the work of subordinates and is directly responsible for achieving the organization's basic goals; for example, a production manager who supervises workers on the factory floor and decides how production targets will be met. Staff authority is the authority to advise, assist and support line managers rather than to command. Staff specialists provide expert help in their own field; for example, the human resource department advises line managers on recruitment, selection, training and legal matters. The HR manager normally has staff authority in relation to other departments, but has line authority within the HR department itself, over his or her own subordinates. In addition, HR sometimes exercises functional authority — for example, when it lays down a selection procedure that all departments must follow. A proper balance of line and staff authority is necessary so that expert advice is used without weakening the line manager's responsibility for results.

  4. What are the basic components of an organization? Explain each briefly. (Marks: 3)
    Topic: Introduction to HRM, Management & the Organization

    An organization has four basic components. (1) Task — the work to be done; it is the organization's purpose and the set of activities performed to achieve its goals, for example producing goods or providing services. (2) People — the human resource of the organization; the individuals and groups who perform the tasks. (3) Structure — the formal pattern of relationships, authority and communication through which tasks are divided, grouped and coordinated, shown in the organizational chart. (4) Technology — the intellectual and mechanical process (tools, machines, knowledge and techniques) used to transform inputs into outputs. These four components are interdependent: a change in any one of them, such as new technology, affects all the others.

  5. Describe the three components of an attitude. (Marks: 3)
    Topic: People, Behavior, Groups & Teams

    An attitude is a tendency to respond positively or negatively toward an object, person or situation, and it has three components. (1) Cognitive component — the beliefs, opinions, knowledge and information a person holds about the object; for example, 'my friend is kind, charismatic and funny' describes what a person believes. (2) Affective component — the feelings or emotions associated with the object; for example, 'Ahmad is feeling happy on getting a new job.' (3) Behavioral component — the tendency or intention to act in a certain way toward the object; for example, an employee who dislikes his supervisor may decide to avoid him or resign. Managers study attitudes such as job satisfaction, job involvement and organizational commitment because they influence employee behavior at work.

  6. Explain stereotyping and halo effect as errors in perception. (Marks: 3)
    Topic: People, Behavior, Groups & Teams

    Perception is the mental process through which a person interprets his or her environment, and it can be distorted by errors. Stereotyping means judging a person on the basis of the group to which he or she belongs rather than on individual characteristics; for example, assuming that 'women cannot do important or heavy jobs.' Stereotyping saves effort but is often inaccurate and can lead to unfair decisions in selection and appraisal. The halo effect occurs when one positive or negative characteristic of a person dominates our overall impression of that person; for example, an interviewer who is impressed by a candidate's good dressing may rate him high on intelligence and ability as well, or rate a person low on all items because of one weak trait. Both errors distort judgment, so managers should evaluate individuals on complete, job-related information.

  7. Differentiate between a group and a team. (Marks: 3)
    Topic: People, Behavior, Groups & Teams

    A group is two or more interacting and interdependent individuals who come together to achieve particular objectives; its members may simply share information and help each other perform within their own areas of responsibility. A team is a more developed form of group in which members are committed to a common purpose and common performance goals, possess complementary skills, and hold themselves mutually accountable for results. In a group, accountability is individual, the leader is clearly appointed, and the work products are individual. In a team, accountability is both individual and mutual, leadership may be shared, and the team produces collective work products. Team performance also creates synergy — the total output is greater than the sum of individual outputs. Hence, every team is a group, but a group is not always a team.

  8. Chenab is a manufacturing company. It is considering upgrading its technology by purchasing computer-driven equipment to manufacture its products. As an HR manager, which HR aspects must be considered while making this decision? (Marks: 3)
    Topic: HRM in a Changing Environment & Workplace Diversity

    As an HR manager I would consider the following aspects. First, the effect on jobs: computer-driven equipment will change job contents, so the affected jobs must be re-analyzed and job descriptions and specifications revised. Second, the skills gap: existing employees may not be able to operate the new equipment, so training needs must be assessed and proper training and development programs arranged. Third, staffing effects: automation may create a surplus of some workers and a shortage of skilled technicians, so human resource planning must arrange redeployment, retraining or, as a last resort, humane rightsizing, and recruitment of new technical staff. Fourth, employee resistance: workers may fear job loss, so management should communicate the change honestly and involve employees to reduce anxiety. Finally, cost and safety aspects — training costs, revised pay for higher skills, and safe operating procedures for the new machines must be planned.

  9. Why do HR managers study and understand new trends at the workplace? (Marks: 3)
    Topic: HRM in a Changing Environment & Workplace Diversity

    The workplace is continuously changing because of technology, globalization, workforce diversity, new laws and changing employee expectations. HR managers study new trends so that HR policies and practices remain effective and up to date. Understanding trends helps them to anticipate challenges — such as skill shortages, new forms of work like contingent and remote work, and changing legal requirements — and to prepare the organization in advance instead of reacting late. It also helps in revising recruitment, training and compensation practices so the organization can attract and retain talented people, and in advising top management on strategic decisions. An HR manager who ignores new trends will apply outdated practices, which can reduce productivity, increase turnover and expose the organization to legal problems.

  10. How can an organization improve its recruiting efforts to achieve diversity? (Marks: 3)
    Topic: HRM in a Changing Environment & Workplace Diversity

    An organization can improve its recruiting efforts for diversity in several ways. It should widen its recruitment sources beyond the usual channels — for example, advertising in media read by minority groups, contacting women's and minority professional associations, educational institutions and community organizations. Job advertisements should use neutral language and clearly state that the organization is an equal opportunity employer, so that no group feels excluded. The recruitment and selection staff should be trained to avoid bias and stereotyping, and selection criteria should be strictly job-related. The organization can also set diversity goals in its human resource planning, use diverse interview panels, and make the workplace visibly welcoming — for instance by publicizing its diversity policies and success stories. Finally, it should monitor the results of its recruiting efforts to check whether diverse candidates are actually being attracted and selected.

  11. Enlist three positive points which organizations gain by having a diverse workforce. (Marks: 3)
    Topic: HRM in a Changing Environment & Workplace Diversity

    A diverse workforce benefits an organization in many ways; three positive points are: (1) Better problem solving and creativity — employees from different backgrounds bring different viewpoints, knowledge and experiences, which improves the quality of decisions and generates new ideas. (2) Better service to diverse customers — a workforce that reflects the diversity of the market understands different customers' needs and languages better, which helps the organization compete in local and global markets. (3) Larger talent pool and better image — by recruiting from all segments of society without discrimination, the organization can select from more qualified candidates, and it also earns a reputation as a fair, socially responsible employer, which helps attract and retain good employees.

  12. "Rules & policies are developed to protect employees only." Do you agree with this statement? Justify your answer. (Marks: 5)
    Topic: Legal Context of HR Decisions

    I do not agree with this statement. Rules and policies certainly protect employees — they guarantee fair and consistent treatment, protect employees from arbitrary action by supervisors, safeguard their health and safety, and give them a clear basis for raising grievances. But rules and policies are not developed for employees alone; they protect the organization as well. They guide managers and supervisors in making consistent decisions, maintain discipline and orderly behavior, and help achieve organizational goals efficiently. They also protect the organization legally: documented policies on equal employment opportunity, safety and discipline help the organization comply with laws and defend itself in court if a decision is challenged. In addition, clear rules protect customers and the public by ensuring quality and safe conduct. Therefore, rules and policies serve both sides — they protect employees' rights and at the same time protect the organization's interests, consistency and legal position.

  13. What is equal employment opportunity (EEO)? Why is it important in HR decisions? (Marks: 3)
    Topic: Legal Context of HR Decisions

    Equal employment opportunity means that anyone, regardless of race, color, sex, religion, national origin or age, has an equal chance for a job or promotion based only on his or her qualifications and ability to perform the work. Under EEO, employment decisions such as hiring, promotion, training, transfer and termination must not be affected by illegal discrimination. EEO is important in HR decisions because, first, it is a legal requirement and violating it can result in lawsuits, penalties and damage to the organization's reputation. Second, it is morally fair and gives every individual a chance to compete on merit. Third, it benefits the organization itself: selecting purely on merit widens the pool of talent, improves the quality of decisions, and builds a diverse workforce. HR managers therefore ensure that job descriptions, tests and interview questions are job-related and free from bias.

  14. As an HR manager, what steps would you take to make human resource planning more effective? (Marks: 3)
    Topic: Human Resource Planning

    To make human resource planning more effective I would take the following steps. First, link HR planning closely with the organization's strategic plans and goals, so that staffing needs are derived from business needs. Second, forecast demand and supply of human resources carefully, using techniques such as trend analysis, ratio analysis and managerial judgment, and maintain accurate data through a human resource information system (HRIS). Third, involve line managers in the planning process, because they know the actual work requirements of their departments. Fourth, prepare concrete action plans for expected shortages (recruitment, training) and surpluses (redeployment, reduced hours) instead of only making forecasts. Finally, review and update the plan regularly, because conditions change; a plan that is monitored and revised remains realistic and useful.

  15. Describe the process of human resource planning and the techniques used for forecasting the demand for employees. (Marks: 5)
    Topic: Human Resource Planning

    Human resource planning is the process of systematically reviewing human resource requirements to ensure that the required number of employees, with the required skills, is available when they are needed. The process has four main steps. (1) Assessing the current human resources — preparing an inventory of available employees and their skills, often maintained through an HRIS. (2) Forecasting demand and supply — estimating how many employees of each type will be needed in the future, and how many will be available from internal sources (promotions, transfers) and external sources. (3) Matching demand and supply — identifying gaps; a shortage leads to recruitment and training plans, while a surplus leads to redeployment, reduced working hours or rightsizing. (4) Action plans and control — implementing the plans and evaluating the results. Common demand forecasting techniques include trend analysis (studying past employment levels to predict the future), ratio analysis (forecasting on the basis of a ratio, such as sales volume per employee), the scatter plot method (graphing the relationship between a business factor and staffing levels), and zero-based forecasting, which uses the organization's current level of employment as the starting point for future staffing needs.

  16. Traditionally, a job is defined into four segregates (parts): job, position, duty and task. Compare these four parts and find the relationship among them. (Marks: 5)
    Topic: Job Analysis

    These four terms describe different levels of work units in an organization, from the smallest to the largest. A task is the smallest, distinct work activity performed by an employee — an identifiable unit of work with a definite beginning and end, such as typing a letter or tightening a bolt. A duty is a larger work segment consisting of several related tasks performed by one person; for example, maintaining office records is a duty made up of tasks like filing, updating and retrieving documents. A position is the set of tasks and duties assigned to a particular person — there are as many positions in an organization as there are employees. A job is a group of positions that are identical or very similar with respect to their major tasks and duties; for example, all typists in different departments hold the same job but different positions. The relationship among them is hierarchical and cumulative: tasks combine to form duties, a set of duties forms a position, and similar positions grouped together form a job. Job analysis studies work at all these levels to prepare job descriptions and job specifications.

  17. Develop a 'Job Specification' for the position of HR Executive. (Marks: 3)
    Topic: Job Analysis

    Job Specification — HR Executive. Education: Master's degree in Business Administration (MBA) with specialization in Human Resource Management, or MPA/MSc in a relevant field from a recognized university. Experience: at least 2–3 years of experience in human resource functions such as recruitment, selection, training or record management; fresh candidates with internship experience may be considered for junior level. Knowledge and skills: sound knowledge of HR policies, labor laws and recruitment techniques; good communication and interpersonal skills; interviewing skills; proficiency in MS Office and HR information systems; ability to maintain confidential records. Personal characteristics: integrity and fairness, pleasant personality, ability to work under pressure and in a team, and strong organizational skills. Physical requirements: normal health sufficient for office work and occasional travel. The incumbent must possess the qualifications above as the minimum acceptable requirements to perform the job of HR Executive satisfactorily.

  18. A record-keeping clerk is to be hired. His job specification describes: intermediate qualification, six months' experience in a financial institution, ability to enter data at 30 words per minute, and good knowledge of MS Word and Excel. Draft his job description. (Marks: 5)
    Topic: Job Analysis

    Job Description. Job title: Record-Keeping Clerk. Department: Accounts/Administration. Reports to: Office Superintendent / Accounts Officer. Job summary: The record-keeping clerk is responsible for maintaining, updating and retrieving the financial and administrative records of the organization accurately and in an orderly manner. Duties and responsibilities: (1) Maintain files, ledgers and registers of financial transactions and official correspondence. (2) Enter data into the computer at the required speed and accuracy, using MS Word and MS Excel. (3) Prepare and update record lists, indexes and summaries as directed. (4) Retrieve and provide records and documents whenever required by officers. (5) Check entries for errors and report discrepancies to the supervisor. (6) Ensure safe custody and confidentiality of records. Working conditions: normal office environment; work is performed on a computer for most of the working day. Job context: the job requires an intermediate qualification, six months' experience in a financial institution, data entry speed of 30 words per minute, and good working knowledge of MS Word and Excel (as given in the job specification).

  19. What is job analysis? Describe its main uses in human resource management. (Marks: 5)
    Topic: Job Analysis

    Job analysis is the thorough and detailed study of jobs within an organization — the procedure through which we determine the duties and responsibilities of a job and the kind of person who should be hired for it. It produces two documents: the job description, which states what the job holder does, how it is done and why it is done, and the job specification, which states the minimum qualifications (education, experience, skills and traits) the job holder must possess. Job analysis information is the foundation of almost all HR activities. It is used in human resource planning (knowing what jobs exist and what skills are needed), recruitment (preparing accurate advertisements), selection (setting criteria and designing valid tests and interviews), training (identifying the skills employees must learn), performance appraisal (setting standards for each job), compensation (job evaluation is based on job analysis for deciding the worth of jobs), and safety (identifying hazards connected with a job). Without accurate job analysis, all these HR decisions would be based on guesswork.

  20. Describe the methods used for collecting job analysis information. (Marks: 5)
    Topic: Job Analysis

    Several methods are used to collect job analysis information, and often a combination is used. (1) Observation — the analyst directly observes the employee performing the job and records the tasks; it is most suitable for jobs consisting of observable physical activity, such as assembly line work, but not for mental work. (2) Interview — the analyst interviews the job holder and his supervisor about the duties, responsibilities and requirements of the job; interviews can be individual or group, structured or unstructured. (3) Questionnaire — employees fill in structured questionnaires describing their tasks and the time spent on each; this method can cover many jobs quickly and cheaply, for example through the Position Analysis Questionnaire. (4) Diary or log — the job holder records his activities at regular intervals over a period of time. (5) Technical conference — a group of job experts ('subject matter experts') discusses and identifies the characteristics of the job. Each method has strengths and weaknesses, so job analysts usually combine two or more methods to obtain complete and accurate information.

  21. Why might an organization use the services of employment agencies rather than doing its own recruiting? (Marks: 3)
    Topic: Recruitment

    An organization may use employment agencies for several reasons. First, the organization may not have its own developed HR department or recruiting machinery, especially if it is small. Second, agencies specialize in recruitment: they maintain large databases of candidates, advertise widely and screen applicants, so they can fill vacancies — particularly specialized, technical or urgent ones — faster than the organization itself. Third, using an agency saves the organization's time and cost of advertising, short-listing and initial interviewing. Fourth, agencies are useful for hiring temporary or contingent workers and for confidential searches, for example when a company does not want competitors to know it is hiring for a sensitive position. Finally, an agency's wider reach can provide candidates from other cities or industries that the organization's own efforts might not attract.

  22. The Government College of Commerce has vacant positions. Suggest the most appropriate source of recruitment for each of the following posts and justify briefly: Head of Department; a college professor who has just received his/her PhD; Lecturer; Entry-level lab assistant; Senior librarian. (Marks: 5)
    Topic: Recruitment

    (1) Head of Department — internal source: promotion from among the senior professors of the college. The post requires knowledge of the institution, its staff and its academic culture, and promotion also motivates senior faculty. (2) Professor who has just received his/her PhD — internal source: promotion/upgrading of the existing lecturer or assistant professor on the basis of the new qualification, since the qualified person is already available inside the college. (3) Lecturer — external source: open advertisement in newspapers and on the college/education department website, followed by the prescribed selection process, so that the best qualified candidates from the whole market can compete on merit. (4) Entry-level lab assistant — external but local sources: local newspaper advertisement, the public employment exchange, or applications from walk-in candidates; the job needs basic qualifications, so an inexpensive local search is sufficient. (5) Senior librarian — first internal source: promotion of the senior-most librarian already serving in the college library, who knows its collection and systems; if no suitable internal candidate exists, advertise externally among qualified library science professionals. In general, senior and specialized posts are filled internally where suitable talent exists, while teaching and entry-level posts are advertised externally to obtain the best merit-based selection.

  23. Compare internal and external sources of recruitment. (Marks: 5)
    Topic: Recruitment

    Internal sources fill vacancies from within the organization — through promotions, transfers, job postings (notifying present employees about openings), employee referrals and rehiring former employees. Their advantages are that the candidates are already known and their performance is on record, recruitment is quicker and cheaper, it motivates employees by offering career growth, and new appointees need less orientation because they know the organization. Their disadvantages are a limited choice of candidates, possible inbreeding of ideas, and disappointment and rivalry among employees who are not selected. External sources attract candidates from outside — through advertisements, employment agencies, educational institutions, job fairs, professional associations and walk-in applicants. Their advantages are a much larger pool of talent, fresh ideas and skills that do not exist inside, and no internal rivalries. Their disadvantages are higher cost and longer time, the risk that an outsider may not fit the culture, and a demoralizing effect on existing employees who expected promotion. Most organizations use a combination: internal sources for continuity and motivation, and external sources when new skills or a wider choice are needed.

  24. Describe the steps of the selection process. (Marks: 5)
    Topic: Selection

    Selection is the process of choosing, from the pool of applicants, the individual best suited to a particular position and to the organization. Its typical steps are: (1) Preliminary screening — an initial interview or review of applications to eliminate clearly unqualified candidates. (2) Application form — candidates complete a standardized form giving their education, experience and references; it provides comparable information on all applicants. (3) Selection tests — written or practical tests (intelligence, aptitude, job knowledge, personality, work sample) measure the abilities required for the job. (4) Employment interview — a formal, in-depth evaluation in which the interviewer assesses the candidate and the candidate learns about the job and organization. (5) Reference and background checks — verifying the authenticity of the information provided, from previous employers and referees. (6) Medical/physical examination — ensuring the candidate is physically fit for the job and establishing a health baseline. (7) Final selection and job offer — the best candidate is approved by the concerned manager and offered appointment. A systematic selection process improves person-job fit and reduces costly hiring mistakes.

  25. Describe the main types of selection tests used by organizations. (Marks: 5)
    Topic: Selection

    Organizations use different tests to predict an applicant's job performance. (1) Intelligence (cognitive ability) tests measure mental abilities such as reasoning, memory and comprehension; they are good predictors of performance in many jobs. (2) Aptitude tests measure a person's capacity to learn a particular skill or job in the future, for example clerical or mechanical aptitude. (3) Achievement or job knowledge tests measure what the candidate already knows about the job's duties — for example, a test on application software for a computer operator. (4) Work sample tests require the candidate to actually perform a sample of the job's tasks, such as a typing test or a sample lecture by a lecturer candidate; they have high content validity. (5) Personality tests assess traits such as sociability, emotional stability and conscientiousness. (6) Vocational interest tests indicate the person's inclination toward a particular occupation that provides satisfaction. (7) Psychomotor tests measure physical abilities such as coordination, speed and reaction time, needed in routine production jobs. Whatever the type, a test must be reliable (give consistent results) and valid (measure what it is supposed to measure) to be useful in selection.

  26. In an interview for an entry-level managerial position, the interviewer asks: "What will you do if your boss asks you to do something that is against your principles?" What would be your answer? (Marks: 5)
    Topic: Selection

    My answer would be balanced and honest. I would say: 'I respect my boss and the chain of command, and I would first make sure I have understood the instruction correctly, because sometimes an apparent conflict is only a misunderstanding. If the task is legal and within organizational policy but merely against my personal preference, I would discuss my concern respectfully, offer an alternative way of achieving the same goal, and then accept my boss's final decision. However, if the instruction requires something illegal, unethical or against the organization's own rules — for example, falsifying records — I would politely decline to do that part, explain my reasons with reference to company policy and the law, and suggest a lawful alternative. If the pressure continued, I would use the proper internal channels, such as the HR department or higher management, rather than acting against my conscience or defying my boss openly.' This answer shows integrity, respect for authority, knowledge of proper channels, and a problem-solving attitude — qualities an employer seeks in a future manager.

  27. New employees have been recruited and you have to organize an orientation program for them. What topics will you cover in the employee orientation program? (Marks: 5)
    Topic: Socialization & Orientation

    The orientation program should cover the following topics. (1) The organization: its history, mission, goals, products or services, structure, and major policies. (2) The employee's own job: duties and responsibilities, performance standards, work schedule, and how the job relates to other jobs in the department. (3) Rules and procedures: attendance, leave, discipline, dress code, safety rules and use of facilities. (4) Pay and benefits: salary payment method and dates, allowances, insurance, provident fund, holidays and other benefits. (5) Introduction to people: the immediate supervisor, co-workers, and key officials such as the HR staff whom the employee can approach for help. (6) Facilities and layout: a tour of the workplace — work area, canteen, prayer area, first aid and emergency exits. (7) Employment terms: probation period, confirmation, and the contents of the employee handbook, a copy of which is given to each new employee. A well-planned orientation reduces the new employee's anxiety, clarifies expectations and starts the socialization process.

  28. Do you agree or disagree with the statement: "Employees subjected to socialization programs perform better than those who have not undergone such training"? Provide justification for your answer. (Marks: 3)
    Topic: Socialization & Orientation

    I agree with this statement. Socialization is the process through which new employees learn the values, norms, knowledge and expected behaviors of the organization. An employee who has gone through a proper socialization program understands his job expectations clearly, knows the organization's culture and rules, and develops relationships with supervisors and co-workers. This reduces the anxiety and uncertainty that new employees normally feel, so they settle into their jobs faster and make fewer mistakes. Socialized employees also develop greater commitment and job satisfaction, which lowers turnover. In contrast, employees left to learn everything by trial and error may adopt wrong habits, feel isolated, and take much longer to reach full productivity. Therefore, other things being equal, socialized employees generally perform better, especially in the early period of their employment.

  29. What is socialization? Describe its stages. (Marks: 3)
    Topic: Socialization & Orientation

    Socialization is the process by which a new employee learns and adapts to the values, norms, culture and expected behaviors of the organization — in simple words, the process of becoming part of the organization by learning its norms and values. It has three stages. (1) Pre-arrival stage — before joining, the individual forms expectations about the job and the organization from information gathered during recruitment and selection; realistic job previews make these expectations accurate. (2) Encounter stage — the new employee actually joins and experiences the real job, which may confirm or contradict his expectations; orientation programs and the help of supervisors and co-workers reduce this 'reality shock.' (3) Metamorphosis stage — the employee successfully internalizes the organizational norms, masters his job, feels accepted by the group and becomes a full member of the organization. Effective socialization produces committed and productive employees.

  30. How would you conduct the task analysis phase of the training needs assessment for the job of Admin Manager? (Marks: 3)
    Topic: Training & Development

    Task analysis studies the job itself to determine what the employee must be able to do. For the Admin Manager I would proceed as follows. First, I would collect information about the job from its job description and job specification, and by interviewing the Admin Manager, his supervisor and his subordinates, and observing the work. Second, I would break the job into its main tasks — for example, managing office correspondence and records, coordinating administrative support services, supervising clerical staff, scheduling meetings, handling supplies and facilities, and preparing administrative reports. Third, for each task I would identify the knowledge, skills and abilities required to perform it to the required standard, and the level of performance expected. Finally, I would list these required skills in priority order; this list becomes the basis for deciding the training content — the areas where training is required — for the Admin Manager.

  31. Explain the three levels of training needs assessment. (Marks: 3)
    Topic: Training & Development

    Training needs assessment determines whether training is needed, where it is needed and what it should contain. It is carried out at three levels. (1) Organizational analysis — examines the organization as a whole: its goals, resources, climate and problems, to decide where training emphasis should be placed; for example, a decision to computerize operations creates a company-wide need for computer training. Previous company records and customer satisfaction surveys can reveal deficiencies that require training. (2) Task analysis — studies the tasks of a particular job (using job descriptions and specifications) to identify the knowledge, skills and abilities employees must have to perform that job properly. (3) Person (individual) analysis — identifies which individual employees need training and in what areas, by comparing each employee's actual performance with the required standard, using appraisal data, tests and supervisors' reports. Together, the three levels ensure that training is given to the right people, in the right skills, in line with organizational needs.
