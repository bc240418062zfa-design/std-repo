  1. "A bike is heavy" is uncertain or not? Explain.
    Topic: Introduction to Artificial Intelligence

    It is uncertain whether the statement "A bike is heavy" is true or not, as the weight of a bike can vary depending on the specific model, material, and other factors. Some bikes may be heavy, while others may be lightweight. In order to determine whether a particular bike is heavy or not, more information about the bike in question would be needed. Such vague statements are handled in AI using fuzzy logic.

  2. What is Strong AI?
    Topic: Introduction to Artificial Intelligence

    Strong AI, also known as artificial general intelligence, is a type of artificial intelligence that is capable of performing any intellectual task that a human being can. It is a hypothetical form of AI that is able to understand or learn any intellectual task that a human being can, rather than being designed to perform a specific task or range of tasks. Strong AI is still largely in the realm of science fiction and is not yet a reality, but researchers and scientists are working towards its development.

  3. What is Weak AI?
    Topic: Introduction to Artificial Intelligence

    Weak AI, also known as artificial narrow intelligence, is a type of artificial intelligence that is designed to perform a specific task or range of tasks, rather than being capable of learning or understanding any intellectual task that a human being can. It is the most common form of AI used today and is found in a wide range of applications, including personal assistants, language translation, and image recognition. Weak AI is limited by its narrow scope and is not able to learn or adapt to new tasks or situations outside of its programmed capabilities.

Another approach is to use robotics and engineering techniques to design the machine to move and interact with its environment in a human-like manner. Designing such a machine requires a multidisciplinary approach and expertise in fields such as computer science, artificial intelligence, robotics, engineering, and psychology.

AI can also automate routine tasks and processes, freeing up human workers to focus on more complex and value-added activities. By harnessing the power of AI, industries and companies can gain a competitive advantage and drive innovation and growth.

  9. Which search strategy will you use if there are a large number of states in your solution space and you can't visit each state, and why?
    Topic: Search Strategies

    In this scenario, it would be most efficient to use the best-first search strategy. This is because best-first search always selects the path that appears most promising, based on a heuristic evaluation function. This means that it will prioritize exploring states that are likely to lead to the solution, rather than just blindly searching every state. This can help to reduce the overall number of states that need to be considered and make the search more efficient in cases where there are a large number of states.

  10. What are the drawbacks of the optimal search strategy? Give an example.
    Topic: Search Strategies

    One of the main drawbacks of optimal search strategies is that they can be very time- and resource-intensive, especially in large or complex search spaces. This is because they guarantee that the solution found is the optimal one, but in order to do so, they must explore the entire search space and compare all possible solutions. An example of an optimal search strategy is branch and bound, which is a search algorithm that explores all possible solutions and selects the optimal one.

  11. Discuss the A* procedure.
    Topic: Search Strategies

    A* is an algorithm used to find the shortest path between two nodes in a graph. It maintains an open list of nodes to be explored and a closed list of nodes already visited. At each step, it expands the search to the lowest-cost neighbor and updates the cost of neighbors already on the open list if a shorter path is found. A* uses a heuristic function to guide the search, evaluating nodes by f(n) = g(n) + h(n), and is more efficient than Dijkstra's algorithm.

  12. Write short notes on A* search.
    Topic: Search Strategies

    A* (A-star) is a popular algorithm used for pathfinding and graph traversal. It is an extension of Dijkstra's algorithm and is used to find the shortest path between two nodes in a graph. It evaluates nodes using f(n) = g(n) + h(n), where g(n) is the cost from the start node and h(n) is the heuristic estimate of the cost to the goal.

  13. What is beam search?
    Topic: Search Strategies

    Beam search is similar to breadth-first search, but instead of expanding the entire frontier of nodes at each step, it only expands a predetermined number of the most promising nodes, called the "beam." It expands the most promising paths and prunes less promising ones in order to find the most likely solution to a problem. It is used to reduce the search space and make the algorithm more efficient by avoiding the exploration of unlikely paths.

  14. DFS or BFS — which one is best?
    Topic: Search Strategies

    DFS (Depth-First Search) and BFS (Breadth-First Search) are two algorithms for traversing and searching a graph or tree data structure. DFS begins at the root node and explores as far as possible along each branch before backtracking. BFS starts at the root node and explores all the neighboring nodes at the current depth level before moving on to the next depth level. BFS is generally used to find the shortest path between two nodes, while DFS is used for tasks such as topological sorting and checking the connectivity of a graph. In short, it depends on the situation: if the node is close to the goal, BFS is best; if the node is far down, DFS is best.

  15. Write down one benefit of DFS and one benefit of BFS.
    Topic: Search Strategies

    One benefit of BFS is that it is guaranteed to find the shortest path between two nodes in a graph, provided that the edges of the graph have uniform costs. One benefit of DFS is that it can be used to traverse and search a graph or tree data structure efficiently, as it only needs to store the current path being explored and does not need to store the entire breadth of the search tree, so it uses less memory.

  16. Illustrate Minimax in an AI system.
    Topic: Adversarial Search and Games

    Minimax is a decision-making algorithm used in artificial intelligence systems, particularly in the domain of game playing. It involves choosing the next best move by considering all possible moves and their potential outcomes, and selecting the move that will minimize the maximum potential loss (i.e., the worst-case scenario). This involves assigning a value to each possible move, such as a win or lose, and using these values to determine the best move to make. Minimax can be used in a wide variety of situations, including games, financial decision making, and other applications where the goal is to minimize risk or loss.

  17. Describe the minimax tree of Min and Max.
    Topic: Adversarial Search and Games

    In a minimax tree, the root is a MAX node representing the player who wants to maximize the score. Its children are MIN nodes representing the opponent, who tries to minimize the score; the levels alternate MAX, MIN, MAX, and so on down to the leaf (terminal) nodes, which are assigned utility values. Values are then backed up the tree: each MAX node takes the maximum of its children's values and each MIN node takes the minimum, so the value at the root gives the best achievable outcome for MAX assuming optimal play by both sides.

  18. What is alpha-beta pruning? Explain with the mouse and cheese scenario.
    Topic: Adversarial Search and Games

    Alpha-beta pruning is a technique used to improve the efficiency of the minimax decision-making algorithm. It uses two values, alpha and beta, to represent the best possible outcomes for the max and min players, and prunes any branches of the search tree that cannot improve upon these values. In the mouse and cheese scenario, the mouse (MAX) searches for the path to the cheese while the cat (MIN) tries to block it; once a branch is found to be worse than an already examined alternative, the remaining branches are cut off, so the same decision is reached by examining far fewer nodes.

  19. Differentiate between adversarial algorithms and genetic algorithms.
    Topic: Adversarial Search and Games

    Adversarial algorithms are a type of algorithm used to model decision-making scenarios in which two or more agents or players act in opposition to each other. Examples of adversarial algorithms include minimax, alpha-beta pruning, and Monte Carlo tree search. Genetic algorithms, on the other hand, are optimization algorithms that use the principles of natural evolution to find solutions to problems involving the combination of variables or parameters; they evolve a population of candidate solutions using selection, crossover and mutation.

  20. In adversarial search, is an evaluation function used to score/number the nodes? Give a reason.
    Topic: Adversarial Search and Games

    Yes. In adversarial search, a situation analyzer converts all judgments about board situations into a single, overall quality number. This situation analyzer is also called a static evaluator, and the score or number calculated by the evaluator is called the static evaluation of that node. Positive numbers, by convention, indicate favor to one player; negative numbers indicate favor to the other player. The player hoping for positive numbers is called the maximizing player or maximizer, and the other player is called the minimizing player or minimizer.

  21. In the Eight Queens problem, the first three queens are already placed in appropriate positions following the sequence 4, 5, 8, etc. How will you place the remaining queens?
    Topic: Problem Solving and Constraint Satisfaction

    The remaining queens are placed one row at a time. For each next row, a column is chosen such that the new queen is not attacked by any queen already placed — i.e., no other queen shares its column or either of its diagonals. If a row has no safe column, the algorithm backtracks and moves the previous queen to its next safe position. Continuing in this way (for example, one complete valid arrangement of the eight queens by row is columns 1, 5, 8, 6, 3, 7, 2, 4) all eight queens are placed on the board so that no queen can attack any other.

  22. State the rules of the Two-One (TO) problem.
    Topic: Problem Solving and Constraint Satisfaction

    The rules of the Two-One problem are: (1) 1's move only to the right; (2) 2's move only to the left; (3) sweeping is allowed; (4) a hop or jump is allowed; (5) only one move is allowed at a time.

  24. What does GA stand for?
    Topic: Genetic Algorithms

    GA stands for Genetic Algorithm, which is a type of optimization algorithm that uses the principles of natural evolution to find solutions to problems involving the combination of variables or parameters. It works by maintaining a population of solutions and using genetic operators to explore the solution space and search for good solutions.

  25. Differentiate between crossover and mutation.
    Topic: Genetic Algorithms

    Crossover is a genetic operator used in genetic algorithms to combine the genetic material of two parents to create offspring with traits from both. Mutation is a random change in the genetic material of an organism, which can potentially introduce new traits or alter existing ones. Crossover typically occurs between two individuals, while mutation can occur within a single individual. Crossover and mutation are both used to introduce diversity into a population and allow for the evolution of new solutions to a problem.

  26. What will be the issue if crossover is used without mutation in a genetic algorithm?
    Topic: Genetic Algorithms

    If crossover is used without mutation in a genetic algorithm, the algorithm may become stuck in local optima and be unable to explore the full solution space. This can make it harder to find the global optimum solution. Additionally, a lack of mutation can lead to a loss of diversity in the population of solutions, which can make the algorithm less effective at finding good solutions.

  27. Write the steps of a genetic algorithm.
    Topic: Genetic Algorithms

    The steps of a genetic algorithm are: (1) Initialize a population of solutions (initial population). (2) Evaluate the fitness of each solution (calculate fitness). (3) Select the fittest solutions to reproduce (selection). (4) Apply genetic operators — crossover to create offspring and mutation to introduce variation. (5) Repeat steps 2–4 for a specified number of generations or until a satisfactory solution is found.

  28. Explain mutation and evolution.
    Topic: Genetic Algorithms

    Mutation is the process by which genetic material changes or mutates. Mutation can occur spontaneously due to errors in DNA replication or due to environmental factors such as radiation or chemical exposure. Evolution is the process by which populations of living organisms change over time in response to their environment through the process of natural selection.

  29. What are the applications of genetic algorithms?

Common applications include: machine learning — optimizing the parameters of models such as neural networks; scheduling and routing — optimizing schedules and routes for logistics and transportation problems; optimization of complex systems — finding the optimal configuration of systems such as an electrical grid or a supply chain; search and rescue — searching for missing persons or objects in a large, complex environment; and data analysis and modeling — analyzing and modeling large data sets to extract insights and make predictions.

  30. How are knowledge representation and reasoning closely coupled in the AI cycle?
    Topic: Knowledge Representation and Reasoning

    Knowledge representation (KR) and reasoning are closely coupled components; each is intrinsically tied to the other. A representation scheme is not meaningful on its own; it must be useful and helpful in achieving certain tasks. The same information may be represented in many different ways, depending on how you want to use that information. For example, in mathematics, if we want to solve problems about ratios, we would most likely use algebra, but we could also use simple hand-drawn symbols. To say half of something, you could use 0.5x or you could draw a picture of the object with half of it colored differently. Both convey the same information, but the former is more compact and useful in complex scenarios where reasoning is to be performed on the information. As an AI system designer, one has to consider this relationship when coming up with any solution.

  31. What is shallow (heuristic) knowledge and structural knowledge?
    Topic: Knowledge Representation and Reasoning

    Heuristic knowledge is rule-of-thumb knowledge, e.g. if I start seeing shops, I am close to the market. Heuristic knowledge is sometimes called shallow knowledge, and it is empirical as opposed to deterministic. Structural knowledge describes structures and their relationships, e.g. how the various parts of a car fit together to make a car, or knowledge structures in terms of concepts, sub-concepts, and objects.

Heuristic knowledge is rule-of-thumb knowledge, e.g. if I start seeing shops, I am close to the market.   33. Differentiate between monotonic and non-monotonic reasoning.
    Topic: Knowledge Representation and Reasoning

    Non-monotonic reasoning is used when the facts of the case are likely to change after some time. For example, the rule: IF the wind blows THEN the curtains sway. When the wind stops blowing, the curtains should sway no longer. However, if we use monotonic reasoning, this would not happen: the fact that the curtains are swaying would be retained even after the wind stopped blowing, because in monotonic reasoning facts once derived are never withdrawn. In non-monotonic reasoning, we have a truth maintenance system. It keeps track of what caused a fact to become true. If the cause is removed, that fact is removed (retracted) also.

  34. Convert the statement (A v B) -> (C -> D) into Conjunctive Normal Form (CNF).
    Topic: Knowledge Representation and Reasoning

    Step 1: Eliminate implication: (A v B) -> (C -> D) becomes ~(A v B) v (~C v D). Step 2: Move negation inwards using De Morgan's law: (~A ^ ~B) v ~C v D. Step 3: Distribute v over ^ to get CNF: (~A v ~C v D) ^ (~B v ~C v D). So the CNF form is: (¬A ∨ ¬C ∨ D) ∧ (¬B ∨ ¬C ∨ D).

  35. Are perception and knowledge representation tightly coupled? Give a reason.
    Topic: Knowledge Representation and Reasoning

    Yes, perception and knowledge representation are tightly coupled. An AI system has a perception component that allows the system to get information from its environment. As with human perception, this may be visual, audio or other forms of sensory information. The system must then form a meaningful and useful representation of this information internally. This knowledge representation may be static, or it may be coupled with a learning component that is adaptive and draws trends from the perceived data.

  36. Do you think the deduction mechanism is feasible in practical systems or not? Give a reason.
    Topic: Knowledge Representation and Reasoning

    Pure deduction alone is not fully feasible in practical systems. Deduction is sound, but it requires a complete and consistent knowledge base and can only derive conclusions that are already implicit in the given facts. In real-world problems the knowledge is usually incomplete and uncertain, and the number of possible deductions grows combinatorially, so an unrestricted deductive search becomes too slow and expensive. That is why practical systems do not rely on deduction alone; they control it with strategies such as forward and backward chaining, heuristics and conflict resolution, and combine it with other forms of reasoning.

  37. Does the knowledge which is gained by a person to drive a truck best suit to be called "Declarative Knowledge" or "Procedural Knowledge"?
    Topic: Knowledge Representation and Reasoning

    It is Procedural Knowledge. Procedural knowledge is the knowledge of how to perform a task (know-how), such as driving a truck, riding a bicycle or swimming. It is a skill acquired through practice and is demonstrated by performing the actions, rather than a set of facts that can be stated. Declarative knowledge, in contrast, is knowledge about facts and things (know-that), e.g. "a truck has four or more wheels".

  38. "It is probably rainy today" is fuzzy or not?
    Topic: Reasoning and Fuzzy Logic

    "It is probably rainy today" is a fuzzy statement because it involves uncertainty and imprecision. Fuzzy logic is a branch of mathematics that deals with reasoning under uncertainty and allows for the representation and manipulation of vague or imprecise information.

  39. "Riding a horse is same as riding a donkey." Explain which reasoning is best suited.
    Topic: Reasoning and Fuzzy Logic

    The statement "riding a horse is the same as riding a donkey" is an analogy that compares the two activities based on their shared characteristic of being types of riding. Analogy is a form of reasoning that involves making comparisons in order to understand or explain something. It is closely related to inductive reasoning, which involves making a generalization based on specific examples or observations. In this case, the generalization being made is that riding a horse and riding a donkey are similar experiences.

  40. Differentiate between inductive and deductive reasoning.
    Topic: Reasoning and Fuzzy Logic

    Inductive reasoning involves moving from specific observations or examples to a general conclusion. It is based on the idea that if a certain pattern or relationship holds true for a number of specific cases, it is likely to hold true in other cases as well. Deductive reasoning, on the other hand, involves moving from a general principle or premise to a specific conclusion. It is based on the idea that if a certain statement is true, and certain other statements follow logically from it, then those statements must also be true. Deductive reasoning is often used to test the validity of a hypothesis or to prove or disprove a theory.

Software engineer — designs and implements the software that powers the expert system. Artificial intelligence researcher (knowledge engineer) — designs and implements the AI algorithms and techniques that enable the expert system to reason and make decisions. Domain expert — provides the knowledge and expertise needed to build the expert system in a specific domain, such as medicine, finance, or engineering. Other team members may include quality assurance engineers, user experience designers, and technical writers.

  44. Elaborate the importance of the Knowledge Base in an expert system.
    Topic: Expert Systems and CLIPS

    The knowledge base is the part of an expert system that contains the domain knowledge, i.e. problem facts and rules, concepts, and relationships. The power of an expert system lies to a large extent in its richness of knowledge. Therefore, one of the prime roles of the expert system designer is to act as a knowledge engineer. The knowledge engineer must overcome the knowledge acquisition bottleneck and find an effective way to get information from the expert and encode it in the knowledge base, using one of the knowledge representation techniques.

  45. What are the conflict resolution strategies in a rule-based expert system?
    Topic: Expert Systems and CLIPS

    To overcome the conflict problem, we may choose one of the following conflict resolution strategies: (1) Fire first rule in sequence (rule ordering in list): all rules are ordered, imposing prioritization; when more than one rule matches, we simply fire the first in the sequence. (2) Assign rule priorities (rule ordering by importance): explicit priorities are assigned to rules to allow conflict resolution. (3) More specific rules (more premises) are preferred over general rules, because a rule with more premises has, in a sense, more evidence or votes from its premises. (4) Prefer rules whose premises were added more recently to working memory (timestamping): this prioritizes recently added facts over older facts. (5) Parallel strategy (view-points): we do not resolve the conflict by selecting one rule; instead we branch execution into a tree, with each branch operating in parallel on multiple threads of reasoning, maintaining multiple view-points concurrently.

  46. Write the step-by-step procedure of backward chaining.
    Topic: Expert Systems and CLIPS

    1. Start with the goal. 2. The goal may be in working memory (WM) initially, so check — and you are done if found. 3. If not, search for the goal in the THEN part of the rules (match conclusions rather than premises). This type of rule is called a goal rule. 4. Check to see if the goal rule's premises are listed in the working memory. 5. Premises not listed become sub-goals to prove. 6. The process continues in a recursive fashion until a premise is found that is not supported by any rule, i.e. a premise is called a primitive if it cannot be concluded by any rule. 7. When a primitive is found, ask the user for information about it. Backtrack and use this information to prove the sub-goals and subsequently the goal.

  47. Explain the structure of an expert system using the analogy of a human expert.
    Topic: Expert Systems and CLIPS

    To understand the structure of an expert system, we use the analogy of an expert (say a doctor) solving a problem. The expert has: a focused area of expertise; specialized knowledge (long-term memory, LTM); case facts (short-term memory, STM); the ability to reason with these to form new knowledge; and thus solves the given problem. An expert system mirrors this structure: the knowledge base acts as the long-term memory containing the specialized domain knowledge, the working memory holds the case facts like short-term memory, and the inference engine reasons with the knowledge base and case facts to derive new knowledge and reach a solution.

  48. What is "defrule" in CLIPS syntax? Give an example.
    Topic: Expert Systems and CLIPS

    Rules are entered into CLIPS using the defrule construct. Example: (defrule isSon "An example rule" (father (fathersName "ali") (sonsName "ahmed")) => (assert (son (sonsName "ahmed") (fathersName "ali")))). The construct consists of a rule header (defrule followed by the rule name and an optional comment), the patterns (IF part) that are matched against the facts in working memory, the => arrow, and the actions (THEN part) that are executed when the rule fires.

  49. Which is better in terms of cost: an expert system or a human expert?
    Topic: Expert Systems and CLIPS

    In terms of cost, an expert system is better than a human expert, as an expert system has low cost: once developed, it can be copied and used repeatedly at little extra cost, whereas a human expert is expensive and not always available.

  50. What is the difference between a conventional system and an expert system?
    Topic: Expert Systems and CLIPS

    An expert system is different from conventional programs in the sense that program control and knowledge are separate. We can change one while affecting the other minimally. This separation is manifest in the expert system structure: knowledge base, working memory and inference engine. Separation of these components allows changes to the knowledge to be independent of changes in control, and vice versa.

  51. What is forward chaining? Describe its approach.
    Topic: Expert Systems and CLIPS

    Forward chaining is an inference strategy that begins with a set of known facts, derives new facts using rules whose premises match the known facts, and continues this process until a goal state is reached or until no further rules have premises that match the known or derived facts. It is a data-driven approach — just as a doctor asks the patient for symptoms and then infers a diagnosis from the symptoms. Approach: 1. Add facts to working memory (WM). 2. Take each rule in turn and check to see if any of its premises match the facts in the WM. 3. When matches are found for all premises of a rule, place the conclusion of the rule in WM. 4. Repeat this process until no more facts can be added. Each repetition of the process is called a pass.

  52. Which is best in learning ability: a human expert or an expert system?
    Topic: Expert Systems and CLIPS

    The learning ability of a human expert is best. A human expert can learn from experience and update his or her knowledge, whereas an expert system cannot learn on its own; its knowledge base must be updated by a knowledge engineer.

  53. Describe the appropriate domains for expert systems.
    Topic: Expert Systems and CLIPS

    When analyzing a particular domain to see if an expert system may be useful, the system analyst should ask the following questions: Can the problem be effectively solved by conventional programming? If not, an expert system may be the choice, because expert systems are especially suited to ill-structured problems. Is the domain well-bounded? For example, a headache diagnosis system may eventually have to contain domain knowledge of many areas of medicine, because it is not easy to limit diagnosis to one area; where the domain is too wide, building an expert system may not be a feasible proposition. What are the practical issues involved? Is some human expert willing to cooperate? Is the expert's knowledge especially uncertain and heuristic? If so, an expert system may be useful.

  54. What are the issues in forward chaining?
    Topic: Expert Systems and CLIPS

    The main issues in forward chaining are: (1) Undirected search — forward chaining may derive many facts that are irrelevant to the goal. (2) Conflict resolution — when several rules match, a strategy is needed to decide which rule fires. (3) Conflict resolution strategies — an appropriate strategy (rule ordering, priorities, specificity, timestamping, or parallel view-points) must be selected.
