  1. Discuss the relationship between language and society.
    Topic: Introduction to Sociolinguistics • 5 marks marks

    Language and social interaction have a reciprocal relationship: language shapes social interactions and social interactions shape language. Language is a social, cultural and geographical phenomenon, and it is in society that man acquires and uses language. Sociolinguistics is the study of the connection between language and society and of the way people use language in different social situations. Problems in this relationship include communities which develop a standard language, the reactions of minorities (as in Belgium, India, Pakistan or Wales), the need to educate people to a linguistic level where they can cope with a variety of social situations, and linguistic change in response to social factors.

  2. Define sociology of language. How does it differ from sociolinguistics?
    Topic: Introduction to Sociolinguistics • 3 marks marks

    Sociology of language is the study of the relations between language and society. The difference between the two is quite clear: sociolinguistics focuses on the effect of society on language, while sociology of language focuses on the effect of language on society. Sociology of language seeks to understand the way social dynamics are affected by individuals; language is considered a social value within this field, which researches social groups for phenomena like multilingualism and language conflict. It deals with who is 'authorized' to use what language, with whom and under what conditions, and with how individual or group identity is established by the language available for people to use.

  3. How is sociolinguistics related to other disciplines?
    Topic: Introduction to Sociolinguistics

    Sociolinguistics is closely related to several other disciplines, in particular: (1) Discourse Analysis, which studies language in use beyond the sentence; (2) Pragmatics, which studies how context contributes to meaning; and (3) Ethnography, which studies the communicative behaviour of communities in their cultural context.

  4. Differentiate between linguistics and sociolinguistics.
    Topic: Introduction to Sociolinguistics

    Linguistics is the study of language itself, and includes branches such as psycholinguistics, historical linguistics, morphology, syntax and semantics. It takes account only of the structure of language, to the exclusion of the social contexts in which language is learned and used. Sociolinguistics, on the other hand, is the study of the variation of language in use: we use different words and grammatical forms depending on the context, so sociolinguistics studies how society affects language and how language is used in different social situations.

  5. Discuss sociolinguistic phenomena in relation to the changing world.
    Topic: Introduction to Sociolinguistics • 5 marks marks

    To understand sociolinguistic phenomena in relation to the changing world, we need to focus on the nature of sociolinguistic phenomena and on the dynamics of today's rapidly changing world, including the sociolinguistics of globalization and its challenges. Two important features of today's world are super diversity and multilingualism, so linguistic communication has to be rethought in the ever-changing globalization context. There are also significant aspects of linguistic hegemony: some hegemonic languages such as English have spread worldwide; the world is more interconnected; population and population mobility are increasing; and the changing world scenario brings heterogeneity of language practices.

  6. Comment on the relationship between sociolinguistic phenomena and a 'real but exotic world'.
    Topic: Introduction to Sociolinguistics

    A good way to study language in relation to society is to take a sample area as a real but exotic world, for example the north-west Amazon described by Sorensen (1971) and Jackson (1974). Geographically, the area is half in Brazil and half in Colombia, coinciding more or less with the area in which Tukano can be relied on as a lingua franca, a trade language widely spoken as a non-native language. Most of the people are indigenous Indians, divided into over twenty tribes, which are in turn grouped into five phratries (groups of related tribes). Two crucial facts are: (1) each tribe speaks a different language, sufficiently different to be mutually incomprehensible and in some cases genetically unrelated; and (2) the five phratries (and thus all twenty-odd tribes) are exogamous, i.e. a man must not marry a woman from the same phratry or tribe.

  7. What is linguistic exogamy?
    Topic: Introduction to Sociolinguistics

    Linguistic exogamy is the rule, found among the tribes of the north-west Amazon, that a person must marry someone from a different language group: because the phratries and tribes are exogamous, one must marry outside one's own tribe and therefore outside one's own language. It is one main source of the complexity of that region, the other being the amount of individual bilingualism (or, more accurately, multilingualism), which makes it hard to decide who is a speaker of a given language and who is not.

  8. Why was the idea of a homogeneous speech community rejected?
    Topic: Introduction to Sociolinguistics

    For purely theoretical purposes, some linguists hypothesized the existence of an 'ideal' speech community; this is what Chomsky (1965) proposes as a 'completely homogeneous speech community'. However, such a speech community is a theoretical construct employed for a narrow purpose, whereas real speech communities exist in a 'real' world, so we need an alternative view: to study language in society rather than language as necessitated by abstract linguistic theorizing. Using linguistic characteristics alone to determine what is or is not a speech community has proved quite impossible, because people do not necessarily feel any direct relationship between linguistic characteristics and speech community membership. What we can be sure of is that speakers use linguistic characteristics — along with social, cultural, political and ethnic characteristics — to achieve group identity with, and group differentiation from, other speakers.

  9. How do social networks explain speakers' language use?
    Topic: Introduction to Sociolinguistics

    The social networks model offers a far less abstract framework than that of the speech community. It focuses on the social ties that specific speakers have with each other and examines how these ties affect speakers' linguistic usage. A key component is network strength, measured by classifying networks as 'dense' or 'loose', and as 'uniplex' or 'multiplex' (Milroy, 2001). A network is dense if the members interact with each other; if members know each other in more than one way — for example, they work together and are members of the same family — the links are multiplex rather than uniplex. Dense and multiplex networks tend to support localized linguistic norms and function as a method of norm reinforcement, whereas in loose and uniplex networks language change is more likely to occur. Migration, war, industrialization and urbanization have caused disruption of close-knit, localized networks.

  10. What is meant by 'varieties of language'? Define 'variety'.
    Topic: Varieties of Language

    If one thinks of 'language' as a phenomenon including all the languages of the world, the term variety of language can be used to refer to different manifestations of it, just as we speak of 'varieties of music'. What makes one variety of language different from another is, in the simplest answer, the linguistic items. A variety of language may be defined as a set of linguistic items with similar social distribution. According to the Oxford Companion to the English Language (1992), there are two broad types of language variety.

  11. Define the term 'lect'.
    Topic: Varieties of Language

    In sociolinguistics, a variety is also called a lect. It is a specific form of a language or language cluster, and may include languages, dialects, registers, styles or other forms of language, as well as a standard variety. The term 'variety' (and 'lect') is advantageous because it helps to avoid the term 'language', which has its own issues, and the term 'dialect', which is used for non-standard varieties and is thought of as less prestigious or 'correct' than the standard language. 'Lect' avoids the problem, in ambiguous cases, of deciding whether two varieties are distinct languages or dialects of a single language.

  12. Discuss language and dialects in Pakistan.
    Topic: Varieties of Language

    Pakistan is an amalgamation of heterogeneous groups with widely different linguistic characteristics, and a large number of languages are spoken. The official languages are Urdu and English: Urdu is the official (national) language, though it is not the first language of the majority of the population, and English is limited to a small proportion of the population; apart from these, no language is commonly understood. The national language Urdu has a number of handicaps: it is not indigenous to Pakistan, and the regional languages — Punjabi, Baluchi, Sindhi and Pashto — though rich in literature, rely heavily on oral tradition and lack a unitary system of speech form (Mansoor, 1993). No less than 24 languages and not less than 70 dialects are spoken in this region. Within languages there exist styles and dialects with varying degrees of mutual intelligibility. In the Pakistani context these linguistic differences are a divisive force of great sociolinguistic significance, while the same or closely similar written text is a source of unity in this diversity; the tension between Urdu and local languages on one level, and Urdu and English on the other, has given rise to a hierarchy of languages.

  13. Write a note on the official and national languages of Pakistan.
    Topic: Varieties of Language

    Urdu is the official and national language of Pakistan, but it is not the first language of the majority. Urdu is written in a modified version of the Persian script, from right to left; it is not indigenous to Pakistan and in its literary form emphasizes words of Persian and Arabic origin. It enjoys great prestige as a symbol of the Pakistan movement and as the national language, and the greatest concentration of its speakers is in Punjab and Karachi. Within the hierarchy of languages, English comes on top and the second position goes to Urdu. Urdu is preferred against all the regional and local languages for use in all formal situations, though with the passage of time English is taking its place in the formal sphere of life. In spite of its status as the national language, only a few percent of Pakistanis speak Urdu as their first language.

  14. Discuss language policy in Pakistan.
    Topic: Varieties of Language

    After independence, as in other post-colonial states, a need was felt in Pakistan for a national language to foster national unity, under the idea of 'one nation, one language' (1947–1977). Urdu, the mother tongue of the Mohajirs who had migrated from northern India to urban Sindh, was declared the national language of Pakistan; this hurt the sentiments of the Bengalis and subsequently became one reason for a separatist national movement in East Pakistan (now Bangladesh). The ruling elite were able to function well in English due to prior training under British rule, and Ayub Khan openly declared his preference for English. The Sharif Commission (1959) recommended that the medium of instruction in primary and secondary schools in the public sector should change to Urdu, while English should continue in higher education; however, subsequent policies continued the two streams of English- and Urdu-medium education, creating two classes of people — the ruling elite and the masses. The Constitution of 1973 declared Urdu the national language, set a time frame of 15 years for the replacement of English by Urdu, and at the same time recognized the linguistic rights of speakers of regional and minority languages.

  15. Halliday distinguishes three dimensions of register. Discuss them.
    Topic: Register, Style and Dialects

    Halliday (1978) distinguishes three general types of dimension in register: field, mode and tenor. Field is the purpose and subject-matter of the communication — it refers to 'why' and 'about what' a communication takes place. Mode refers to the means by which communication takes place — notably, by speech or writing; it is about 'how'. Tenor depends on the relations between the participants — it is about 'to whom', i.e. how the speaker views the person addressed. The relations between speaker and addressee involve dimensions such as 'power' (whether the addressee is subordinate, equal or superior to the speaker) and 'solidarity' (which distinguishes relatively intimate relations from distant ones).

  16. What is a regional dialect?
    Topic: Register, Style and Dialects • 3 marks marks

    A regional dialect is a distinct form of a language spoken in a particular geographical area; regional dialects are the most straightforward variety differences based on geography. Since the 19th century, dialectologists in Europe and the United States have been studying the geographical distribution of linguistic items, such as pairs of synonymous words (for example, pail versus bucket) or different pronunciations of the same word (such as farm with or without the /r/). Their results are plotted on a map showing which items were found in which villages, since dialect geography tends to concentrate on rural areas to avoid the complexities of towns. The identification of boundaries between different varieties on the basis of clusters of similar and different features in particular regions is called regional dialectology.

  17. What is a social dialect?
    Topic: Register, Style and Dialects

    Dialect differences are not only geographical. Dialectologists therefore speak of social dialects, or sociolects, to refer to non-regional differences: a social dialect is a variety of speech associated with a particular social class or occupational group within a society. A speaker may be more similar in language to people from the same social group in a different area than to people from a different social group in the same area. In a hierarchical social structure such as Britain's, where social class takes precedence over geography as a determinant of speech, there is far more geographical variation among people in the lower social classes than among those at the 'top' of the social heap; people who have passed through the public school system typically have no regional traits at all in their language.

  18. Differentiate between dialect, jargon, accent and register.
    Topic: Register, Style and Dialects • 5 marks marks

    Dialect: a dialect varies from other dialects of the same language simultaneously on at least three levels of organization — pronunciation, grammar (syntax) and vocabulary. For example, a Northern American might say 'hello' where a Southern American might say 'howdy'. Jargon: the special words or expressions used by a profession or group that are difficult for others to understand; it is the type of language used in a particular context and may not be well understood outside that context (for example, 'a posteriori'). Accent: a distinctive way of pronouncing a language, especially one associated with a particular country, area or social class; accents typically differ in quality of voice, pronunciation and distinction of vowels and consonants. Register: register shows what you are doing; it is used to describe changing how a person speaks based on the situation — for example, pronouncing words ending in -ing 'walking' as 'walkin'.

  19. Write a note on the work of Labov in sociolinguistics.
    Topic: Register, Style and Dialects

    William Labov (1927) has been a prominent voice in American linguistics since the early 1960s. He pioneered an approach to investigating the relationship between language and society and developed a field known as 'variationist sociolinguistics'. A central doctrine of this field is that variation is inherent to linguistic structure: the way a language is spoken (and written) differs across individuals as well as across situations encountered by the same individual, and Labov asserted that such differences are not only normal but also necessary to a language's functioning. This view challenges the traditionally dominant thinking from Saussure to Chomsky, in which variation is downplayed as a superficial phenomenon. Labov's research demonstrates that linguistic variation is pervasive and highly structured, revealing regular patterns of co-occurrence between language forms, such as the pronunciation of a particular vowel, and social categories, such as socioeconomic classes. His approach is distinguished by its reliance on quantitative methods.

  20. Write a note on the Martha's Vineyard study.
    Topic: Register, Style and Dialects

    The first social dialect study was conducted in the summer of 1961 on Martha's Vineyard, an island off the coast of Massachusetts in the north-eastern United States, by William Labov. Martha's Vineyard was already something of a summer playground for people who live most of the year on the mainland US: in the 1960s the number of residents during the summer increased nearly seven times over the winter population (by the year 2000 the year-round population was 14,000, but in summer it ballooned to 100,000). There is a big discrepancy between the circumstances of the summer-only people and the year-rounders: housing on the Vineyard is fabulously expensive, driven up by the intense demand of summer residents, yet the island has the second-lowest per capita income in the entire state of Massachusetts, and many year-rounders struggle to get by providing services for the summer visitors. Labov studied how the islanders' pronunciation expressed their identity in this social setting.

  21. Define isogloss.
    Topic: Register, Style and Dialects

    An isogloss, also called a heterogloss, is the geographic boundary of a certain linguistic feature, such as the pronunciation of a vowel, the meaning of a word, or the use of some morphological or syntactic feature. The word comes from Greek iso- ('same') and gloss- ('tongue').

  22. What is the family tree model? Discuss its value in linguistic history.
    Topic: Register, Style and Dialects

    The family tree model is the traditional approach to historical change, based on the assumption that over time languages gradually diverge from a common ancestor. Its value for historical linguistics is that it clarifies the historical relations among the varieties concerned and gives a clear idea of the relative chronology of the historical changes by which these varieties have diverged. The model allows a very important prediction regarding isoglosses, namely that they should not intersect. A further advantage is that a family tree shows a hierarchical relation among varieties which makes no distinction between 'languages' and 'dialects': it is common in historical linguistics to refer to the varieties descended from Latin as 'dialects' of Latin (the Romance dialects), although they include such obvious 'languages' (in the prestige sense) as Standard French. The model has also been widely applied to explain the historical origins of pidgin and creole languages (the 'monogenetic hypothesis').

  23. Which factors are discussed in language variation?
    Topic: Codes and Bilingualism

    The factors that cause or condition language variation include: (1) the nature of the participants and their relationship (socio-economic, sexual, occupational, etc.); (2) the number of participants (face-to-face, or one addressing a large audience, etc.); (3) the role of the participants (teacher/student, priest, father/son, husband/wife, etc.); (4) the function of the speech event (persuasion, request for information, ritual, verbal, etc.); and (5) the nature of the medium (speech, writing, scripted speech, speech reinforced by gesture, etc.).

  24. What is social class? Discuss its components.
    Topic: Codes and Bilingualism

    Social class is a central concept in sociolinguistic research, one of the small number of social variables by which speech communities are stratified. Trudgill (1974) asserts that most members of our society have some kind of idea, intuitive or otherwise, of what social class is. Social class is a measure of status which is often based on occupation, income and wealth, and theories of social class are associated with figures like Karl Marx and Max Weber. Its components are of two kinds: (1) the objective, economic measures of property ownership and the power and control it confers on its possessor; and (2) the subjective measures of prestige, reputation and status. Despite the lack of a consensus definition, social class is a significant variable in sociolinguistic studies.

  25. What is politeness?
    Topic: Codes and Bilingualism • 3 marks marks

    In linguistics and pragmatics, politeness is somewhat different from its general concept. Politeness encodes social distance: it tells us who is speaking to whom. It is a social norm and operates on the basis of a set of prescriptive social rules. When we say we can encode social distance through politeness, it means that the language choices made by speakers tell us about their social distance from the other interlocutor.

  26. Define bilinguality.
    Topic: Codes and Bilingualism • 3 marks marks

    Bilinguality is the psychological state of an individual who has access to more than one linguistic code as a means of social communication. It should assess a psychological state and therefore account for its specificity; bilinguality is a matter of 'psychomeasurement', whereas bilingualism is the state of a linguistic community (in the MCQ sources phrased as the logical state of the individual who has access to more than one linguistic code).

  27. Differentiate between bilingualism and bilinguality.
    Topic: Codes and Bilingualism

    Bilingualism refers to the state of a linguistic community in which two languages are in contact, with the result that two codes can be used in the same interaction and that a number of individuals are bilingual (societal bilingualism). Titone (1972) says bilingualism is the individual's capacity to speak a second language while following the concepts and structures of that language rather than paraphrasing his or her mother tongue. Bilinguality, on the other hand, is the psychological state of an individual who has access to more than one linguistic code as a means of social communication. The degree of access varies along a number of dimensions: (1) relative competence, (2) cognitive organisation, (3) age of acquisition, (4) exogeneity, and (5) socio-cultural status.

  28. Differentiate between monolingualism and multilingualism.
    Topic: Codes and Bilingualism • 5 marks marks

    Monolingualism is the use of only one language by an individual or community; it has been taken as the natural human condition. Multilingualism is the use of more than one language by a single individual or community. Yet, both historically and currently, most of the world's communities and a majority of speakers are multilingual to a greater or lesser extent, so in the popular imagination and in linguistic theory multilingualism is often assumed to be an anomalous, exceptional practice, whereas in reality monolingualism is not the norm.

  29. Differentiate between code switching and code mixing.
    Topic: Codes and Bilingualism

    Code switching is the alternation between varieties or codes across sentences or clause boundaries: the switch or shift from one language to the other involves longer stretches or units of language at the clause or sentence boundary. Example: 'Very soon I'll be a big star in Bollywood; mein naumeed nehin houngi' (Urdu/English). Code mixing is the alternation between languages or varieties within a clause or phrase, i.e. the mixing of words or phrases of one language into the other within a single sentence. Language hybridization is a product of code mixing.

  30. Differentiate between inter-sentential and intra-sentential switching.
    Topic: Codes and Bilingualism

    Intra-sentential switching takes place within the boundaries of a sentence or a clause; it is limited to the mixing of words or phrases of one language into the other. Inter-sentential switching takes place at the boundary of sentences or clauses: the speaker switches from one language to the other as he or she moves from one sentence or clause to the other.

  31. How does prestige motivate code mixing? What are the reasons for code mixing?
    Topic: Codes and Bilingualism

    The motivational force behind mixing is the prestige factor: prestige is attached to a language, and people insert the words of that language into their base language. Also, in order to develop intimacy and communication friendliness with the other participant(s), a speaker may code-mix; it is used as a tool for creating linguistic solidarity and functions as a bridge that builds solidarity. A switch or a mix can also be made to convey a specific attitude to the listener, and it can be equated with the change in degrees of formality in the speech of monolingual speakers. The factors behind code mixing are: (1) motivation, (2) discourse functions, (3) social standing of the group, (4) the speaker, and (5) the setting.

  32. Discuss Fairclough's two types of power.
    Topic: Language, Power and Policy

    Fairclough (1989) discusses two types of power: coercion and consent. He mentions ideology as the means to obtain consent, since through ideology individuals are led to view reality uncritically, alienating themselves from the objective facts. Coercive or direct power may face resistance since the exploitative intention becomes explicit. In the context of the lives of nations, issues of language, ideology and power are interconnected; for example, in our own historical context, language ideology and the creation of Pakistan are closely connected.

  33. What is the critical period theory?
    Topic: Language, Power and Policy

    According to the critical period theory, if learning takes place after the critical period (usually considered around puberty) for acquiring native-like pronunciation, an individual is unlikely to acquire a native-like accent. Social factors also play a role in this regard: when a group defines a standard pronunciation, speakers who deviate from it are often said to 'speak with an accent'. People from the United States would 'speak with an accent' from the point of view of an Australian, and vice versa.

  34. Discuss language policy and planning.
    Topic: Language, Power and Policy

    Language is a key element in the construction of human groups: all children are socialized into their respective language groups, and adults teach children the structures and lexis, thus protecting and promoting language to a certain degree, so forms of language policy and language planning (LPLP) occur in all societies. Nationalism, which is based on culturally and linguistically homogeneous people differentiated from neighbours, is one political setting in which LPLP has been undertaken in a particularly rigorous and systematic way, and the role of LPLP in nation building is quite significant. The 1970s was a time of intense LPLP activity, as the governing classes of newly independent states considered how to manage language matters. The classic division of language planning and language policy falls into three types: status planning, corpus planning and acquisition planning (Cooper, 1989).

  35. Name any three drawbacks of language and education in Pakistan.
    Topic: Language, Power and Policy • 3 marks marks

    Three drawbacks in the situation of language and education in Pakistan are: (1) little understanding of curricular objectives; (2) inexperienced teachers; and (3) defective methods.

  36. Discuss the process of standardization of a language.
    Topic: Language, Power and Policy

    Standard languages are the result of a direct and deliberate intervention by society; this intervention, the process of standardization, produces a standard language out of dialects or non-standard varieties. A typical standard language passes through four processes: (1) Selection — a particular variety is selected to be developed into a standard language; it may be an existing variety, such as the one used in an important political or commercial centre, or an amalgam of various varieties, and the choice is a matter of great social and political importance because the chosen variety and its speakers gain prestige. (2) Codification — some agency, such as an academy, codifies the variety; dictionaries and grammar books 'fix' it, so that everyone agrees on what is correct. (3) Elaboration of function — the variety must be usable in all the functions associated with central government and institutions: parliament and law courts, bureaucratic, educational and scientific documents, and literature; this may require extra linguistic items, especially technical words, and new conventions of use. (4) Acceptance — the variety is accepted by the community as the standard.

  37. Write a note on language in Canada.
    Topic: Language in the World

    Under the Official Languages Act of 1969, both English and French have official federal status throughout Canada. Canada's linguistic diversity extends beyond the two official languages: 4.7 million people (14.2% of the population) reported speaking a language other than English or French most often at home. The relationship between the English and French languages is the central or defining aspect of the Canadian experience. English is the mother tongue of 56.9% of the population and French the mother tongue of 21.3%, according to the 2011 census. In total, 85.6% of Canadians have a working knowledge of English and 30.1% have a working knowledge of French.

  38. Discuss the language situation in the USA.
    Topic: Language in the World

    Over 500 languages are spoken by the U.S. population. The most commonly used language is English (specifically American English), which is the de facto national language of the United States. Since the 1965 Immigration Act, Spanish is the second most common language in the country. The state government of Louisiana offers services and documents in French, as does New Mexico in Spanish. There are many languages indigenous to North America or to U.S. states or holdings in the Pacific region: Hawaiian is an official language along with English in the state of Hawaii, and Alaska recognizes English and twenty native languages as official. The American Community Survey 2016 (population aged 5+) shows: English only — 237.8 million; Spanish — 40.5 million; Chinese — 3.4 million; Tagalog — 1.7 million.

  39. Explain the language situation of Great Britain.
    Topic: Language in the World • 5 marks marks

    English, in various dialects, is the most widely spoken language of the United Kingdom; it is the official language and is spoken by approximately 59.8 million residents, or 98% of the population over the age of three (2011 Census). However, a number of regional languages are also spoken: 11 indigenous languages are spoken across the British Isles — 5 Celtic, 3 Germanic and 3 Romance. Many immigrant languages are also spoken in the British Isles; more than 300 different languages and dialects are now spoken in British schools, and almost a million people in the country do not speak English at all or not very well (2011 Census).

  40. Write a note on language in North and South Korea.
    Topic: Language in the World

    Korean is the language of the Korean Peninsula in northeast Asia. The Democratic People's Republic of Korea (DPRK, or North Korea) has 20 million speakers, and the Republic of Korea (ROK, or South Korea) has 42 million speakers; Korean is also spoken by almost 2 million people in China, in the provinces bordering North Korea. It is interesting that there are no language minorities in North or South Korea: traditionally it has been an essentially monolingual region, among the very few monolingual states in the world. Two standard varieties are found: the Seoul dialect in South Korea and the Pyongyang dialect in North Korea. The Korean writing system is called Hangul, which has twenty-four basic symbols representing the sounds of Korean.

  41. Name the factors that affect language shift.
    Topic: Language Change: Shift, Death and Creoles

    Factors that affect language shift include: (1) religious and educational background; (2) settlement patterns; (3) ties with the homeland; (4) the extent of exogamous marriage; and (5) majority and minority language groups. Fishman (1991) asserts that a typical case of language shift can happen over three generations: the grandparents are speakers of language X; the parents have a passive knowledge of X but are fluent speakers of Y, so their household is bilingual; and the grandchildren are full speakers of Y, knowing a few words of X at the most.

  42. What is language desertion?
    Topic: Language Change: Shift, Death and Creoles

    Language desertion is the act of deserting, or willful abandonment. Language desertion, or code desertion, refers to the phenomenon of abandoning the use of one language in favour of another, which may ultimately result in the death of the deserted language. Language shift, language transfer, language replacement and language assimilation are all used as alternative terms for this process, in which a population changes from using one language to another.

  43. Write a note on language death.
    Topic: Language Change: Shift, Death and Creoles • 5 marks marks

    Just as ecosystems host a wide variety of species whose loss creates a 'biodiversity crisis', a similar crisis is happening in linguistic diversity. At the moment, linguists believe, around 6,000 languages are spoken. Michael Krauss has predicted that by the end of this century 90 per cent of them will be extinct. The Foundation for Endangered Languages estimates that half the world's languages are moribund and are no longer being passed on to younger generations. According to Crystal (2000, 2003), a language dies every two weeks. In Australia, for example, out of the 260 Aboriginal languages originally spoken, 100 are already extinct, 100 are nearly extinct and only around twenty are being passed on to children.

  44. What are the types of language death?
    Topic: Language Change: Shift, Death and Creoles

    One type is the gradual 'death' in which a language develops into new languages: Latin became the modern Romance family (French, Portuguese, Italian), Ancient Greek became Modern Greek, and Old English became Modern English — this kind of 'death' is inevitable, and only some such languages left written records. A more radical type of extinction is sudden death, which can be caused by the scattering of an entire community of speakers or by natural disasters. Today, however, the most common cause of language death is not population death but language shift: speakers of a language do not pass it on to their children, who instead acquire a different language, and gradually the language of the entire community 'shifts'. Unlike the extinction of an animal species, the people do not die — only their language does.

  45. Why are people leaving their mother tongue?
    Topic: Language Change: Shift, Death and Creoles • 3 marks marks

    A linguistic consequence of language shift in migrant and minority communities is that a child's mother does not teach her own language to the child, but rather a language which she herself speaks only as a foreigner — as though everyone in Britain learned their English from a foreign au-pair girl. One can thus hardly call the children's first language their 'mother tongue' except by a stretch of the imagination. The reports of such communities do not, however, mention any widespread disruption in language learning or general 'deterioration' of the languages concerned.

  46. Write about language accommodation.
    Topic: Language Change: Shift, Death and Creoles

    Accommodation is the process by which participants in a conversation adjust their accent, diction or other aspects of language according to the speech style of the other participant. Communication Accommodation Theory (CAT), developed by Howard Giles, concerns the behavioural changes people make to attune their communication to their partner, and the extent to which people perceive their partner as appropriately attuning to them. Speakers accommodate for reasons such as solidarity, accommodation to listeners, choice of topic, and perceived social and cultural distance; the motivation of the speaker is important, though it need not be conscious. Doing this helps the sender gain approval from the receiver, increases efficiency in communication, and helps the sender maintain a positive social identity. Accommodation is one way of explaining how individuals and groups relate to each other.

  47. What is decreolisation?
    Topic: Language Change: Shift, Death and Creoles

    Decreolisation happens when a creole is spoken in a country where other people speak the creole's lexical source language, for example English. Since the latter has so much more prestige than the creole, creole speakers tend to shift towards it, producing a range of intermediate varieties. Decreolization can thus be termed as changes which bring a creole closer to its superstrate language. Black English in the United States is a variety in the late stages of decreolization, and the range of varieties spanning between basilect and acrolect is called a post-creole continuum.

  48. What is a creole? Discuss creolisation.
    Topic: Language Change: Shift, Death and Creoles • 3 marks marks

    A creole is the native language of some of its speakers: creolization occurs only when a pidgin, for some reason, becomes the variety of language that children must use in situations in which use of a 'full' language is effectively denied them. For example, this must have happened in Haiti when French was effectively denied to the masses and the African languages brought by the slaves fell into disuse. By contrast, many guest workers in Germany developed pidginized varieties of German to communicate with one another, but their children did not creolize these varieties; they acquired Standard German, since they had to go to school and be educated in German.

  49. Differentiate between pidgin and creole.
    Topic: Language Change: Shift, Death and Creoles • 5 marks marks

    A pidgin is a variety created for very practical and immediate purposes of communication between people who otherwise would have no common language whatsoever; it is learned by one person from another within the communities concerned as the accepted way of communicating with members of the other community (for example, Tok Pisin: 'Bai em i no lukim mi' — 'He will not see me'). A pidgin which has acquired native speakers is called a creole language, or creole, and the process whereby a pidgin turns into a creole is called creolisation. It is easy to see how pidgins acquire native speakers: by being spoken by couples who have children and rear them together. This happened on a large scale among the African slaves taken to the New World, and is happening on a smaller scale in urban communities in places like Papua New Guinea. In short, a pidgin expands and develops into a creole.

  50. What is a community of practice?
    Topic: Language Change: Shift, Death and Creoles • 5 marks marks

    An aggregate of people who come together around mutual engagement in some common endeavour may be termed a community of practice. In contrast with speech communities and social networks, in the communities of practice model there is a distinct focus on examining language as a form of practice. Communities of practice can develop out of formal or informal enterprises, and members can be either 'core' or 'peripheral', depending on their levels of integration. They can survive changes in membership, can be small or large, and can come into existence and go out of existence. Eckert (2000) argues that a community of practice is defined simultaneously by its membership and by the shared practices its members partake in; the value of the construct rests on 'the focus it affords on the mutually constitutive nature of the individual, group, activity and meaning' (2000, p. 35). The notion can also extend to more global communities, such as academic fields, religions or professions.

  51. What are the implications of language attitudes in sociolinguistics?
    Topic: Language Change: Shift, Death and Creoles

    Language attitudes have several implications: (1) language attitudes usually entail attitudes to the speakers of the particular language or dialect; (2) there is evidence that language attitudes influence sound change; (3) language attitudes may influence how teachers deal with pupils; and (4) attitudes about language may affect second language learning.

  52. What is diachronic variation? Discuss the significance of diachronic linguistics.
    Topic: Language Change: Shift, Death and Creoles

    Diachronic linguistics is one of the two main temporal dimensions of language study identified by Ferdinand de Saussure; diachronic variation deals with language change over time ('dia' means 'throughout' and 'chron' means 'time'), as opposed to synchronic study, which focuses on a language without reference to its earlier or later stages. Its significance is that there are patterns which simply cannot be discerned without looking at a language at multiple points in time: diachronic linguistics provides insights into language change at all levels — phonetic (Old English had the sound u-umlaut, no longer present in Modern English), phonemic (in Old English /v/ was an allophone of /f/, while in Modern English it is a phoneme itself), syntactic (Old and Middle English had V2 word order) and semantic (change in one of the meanings of a word over time). Tracing the development of English from the Old English period to the twentieth century is an example of a diachronic study.

  53. Why is the armchair approach considered dangerous?
    Topic: Language and Society: Further Topics

    The 'armchair approach' — basing conclusions on one's own experience rather than on facts collected systematically — can be termed dangerous for two reasons if it is applied to personal experience alone. Firstly, most of us are not consciously aware of the vast range of variation in speech which we hear and react to in our everyday lives. Secondly, personal experience is a very limited base from which to generalize about language in society, since it does not take account of all the other societies where things are arranged very differently. The rapid growth of interest in sociolinguistics is due not to armchair theorizing but to the empirical discoveries made in systematic research projects.

  54. What is language contact? Discuss its constraints.
    Topic: Language and Society: Further Topics • 3 marks marks

    Language contact occurs when speakers of two or more languages or varieties interact and influence each other; the study of language contact is called contact linguistics. The most common products of contact are pidgins, creoles, code-switching and mixed languages, and contact also produces language convergence, borrowing and relexification. However, language contact is not as free as it seems: there are constraints on its outcomes other than the principle of localisation — a constraint relating to the aspect of space, another concerned with speakers' attitudes, another of social factors, and yet another of social rigidity where an innovation is being introduced.

  55. What is ethnography of communication?
    Topic: Language and Society: Further Topics • 3 marks marks

    Hymes is best known for his founding role in the ethnography of communication. He first proposed the term 'ethnography of speaking' but later amended it to 'ethnography of communication' (Hymes, 1962, 1964); it was a new approach to understanding language in use. Ethnographies of communication must 'discover and explicate the competence that enables members of a community to conduct and interpret speech' (Hymes, 1972). The exploration and documentation of communicative competence within a speech community is the essence of the ethnography of communication, and Hymes defines the social units and units of analysis for such ethnographies.

  56. Write a note on 'age as a social correlate'.
    Topic: Language and Society: Further Topics

    Of all global categories employed in investigations of language variation, age is perhaps the least examined and the least understood in sociolinguistic terms. Unlike gender, ethnicity or social class, age is often approached uncritically and treated as a biological fact with which to categorize speakers. Yet age is a fundamental dimension of our social and personal identities: legislatively, our age influences what we should and should not do — it determines whether we can vote, drive, marry and go to school or work — and it can influence the types of clothes we wear, the places we go and the ways we speak. Our age is more than a number: it marks our position in, and our movement through, the trajectory of life, which is seen in relation to societal norms of behaviour, obligation and responsibility. The life span is commonly divided into four stages. A close estimate of someone's age from their voice quality and linguistic behaviour is quite possible, from cues in phonetic/phonological features, grammatical structures and lexical items.

  57. Discuss the relationship between language, dialect and identity.
    Topic: Language and Society: Further Topics • 5 marks marks

    We each have a sense of who we are and, equally, of who we want to be. We choose, from situation to situation and even from second to second, how to express ourselves; it is a continuous process, usually unintentional, triggered by a place, subject of conversation or company. But these choices are not random: they are guided by our sense of belonging and are shaped by our identity. For example, a Yorkshire man telling someone to 'wait while five o'clock', using 'while' for standard 'until', declares his own local credentials and puts the other at their ease — unless the other is an outsider, in which case they are either accidentally baffled or intentionally excluded. This matter of inclusion or exclusion — movement towards someone or erection of a barrier against them — is at the heart of how we maintain differences in speech. In multilingual societies, ethnic identities, regional identities and political perspectives likewise influence language choice, and language choices are dominated by powerful ideological positions.

  58. What is language ideology?
    Topic: Language and Society: Further Topics

    The ideology of a standard language is a complex issue. Many widely used languages, such as English, French and Spanish, each possess a standard variety, and this affects how speakers think about their own language and about language in general. Speakers of such languages live in 'standard language cultures'. In such cultures, language attitudes are dominated by powerful ideological positions that are largely based on the supposed existence of this standard form; taken together, these can be said to constitute the standard language ideology, or 'ideology of the standard language'. An ideology itself is a system of ideas and ideals, especially one which forms the basis of economic or political theory and policy.
