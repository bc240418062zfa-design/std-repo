  1. What is paleontology?
    Topic: Foundations of Paleontology • 2 marks marks

    Paleontology is the scientific study of life in the geologic past, especially through the study of animal and plant fossils. It is the branch of science concerned with fossil animals and plants — the study of fossils to determine organisms' evolution and ecology.

  2. What is micropaleontology?
    Topic: Foundations of Paleontology • 2 marks marks

    Micropaleontology is the branch of paleontology that studies microfossils, or fossils that require the use of a microscope to see the organism, its morphology and its characteristic details. It is a multidisciplinary science focused on the study of microorganisms or the microscopic parts of larger organisms.

  3. What is the usefulness of the microfossil record?
    Topic: Foundations of Paleontology • 5 marks marks

    Studying microfossils has a lot of uses. Microfossils are used to determine how old a piece of rock is and to determine if there is gas or oil in the area. They are also used to see what kinds of major geological events took place, such as earthquakes or major weather changes such as ice storms.

  4. What is taphonomy?
    Topic: Foundations of Paleontology

    Taphonomy is the study of how organisms decay and become fossilized.

  5. What are anatomic filters? How do they help to preserve the fossil record?
    Topic: Foundations of Paleontology • 3 marks marks

    Organisms are likely to be preserved only if they have hard parts, a skeleton of some kind. Entirely soft-bodied organisms, such as worms and jellyfish, are only preserved in rare cases. In this way anatomic filters control which organisms enter the fossil record.

  6. What are the types of fossils?
    Topic: Foundations of Paleontology

    Body fossils: the partial or complete remains of plants or animals. Trace fossils: the remains of the activity of ancient organisms, such as burrows and tracks. Five modes of preservation include: (a) insect preserved in amber, (b) petrified wood (permineralization), (c) cast and mold of a clam shell, (d) pyritized ammonite, and (e) compression fossil of a fern.

  7. Differentiate between scavenging and decay.
    Topic: Foundations of Paleontology

    When large animals feed on dead plant or animal tissues, the process is termed scavenging. When microbes, such as fungi or bacteria, transform the tissues of the dead organism, the process is termed decay.

  8. Differentiate between volatiles and refractories.
    Topic: Foundations of Paleontology

    Soft parts of animals are made from volatiles, forms of carbon that break down readily. Refractories are the organic carbons much less liable to break down, such as cellulose.

  9. What is the Burgess Shale?
    Topic: Foundations of Paleontology

    The Burgess Shale is a fossil-bearing deposit exposed in the Canadian Rockies of British Columbia, Canada. It is famous for the exceptional preservation of the soft parts of its fossils. At 508 million years old, it is one of the earliest fossil beds containing soft-part imprints.

  10. What are Lagerstätten?
    Topic: Foundations of Paleontology

    A Lagerstätte is a sedimentary deposit that exhibits extraordinary fossils with exceptional preservation — sometimes including preserved soft tissues.

  11. What is the Signor–Lipps effect?
    Topic: Foundations of Paleontology

    The Signor–Lipps effect is a paleontological principle proposed by Philip W. Signor and Jere H. Lipps, which states that, since the fossil record of organisms is never complete, neither the first nor the last organism in a given taxon will be recorded as a fossil.

  12. Who introduced the concept of induction? Explain it.
    Topic: Foundations of Paleontology • 2 marks marks

    The concept of induction was introduced by Sir Francis Bacon. The enquirer might hope to see common patterns among the observations, and these common patterns would point to an explanation, or law of nature.

  13. What is the role of amateurs in paleontology?
    Topic: Foundations of Paleontology

    Amateurs are not paid to work as paleontologists, yet they contribute greatly by finding and collecting fossils. Collaboration between different sciences is the key to progress in paleontology.

  14. What is thermal maturity?
    Topic: Foundations of Paleontology

    Thermal maturity is the extent of heat-driven reactions that alter the composition of organic matter (e.g., conversion of sedimentary organic matter to petroleum, or cracking of oil to gas). It helps in identifying the levels of thermal maturity of rocks.

  15. What is biostratigraphy?
    Topic: Stratigraphy & Biostratigraphy • 2 marks marks

    Biostratigraphy is the branch of stratigraphy concerned with fossils and their use in dating rock formations. It is the establishment of fossil-based successions and their use in stratigraphic correlation.

  16. What are biozones in the context of biostratigraphy?
    Topic: Stratigraphy & Biostratigraphy

    Biozones are the main operational units of biostratigraphy. Measurements of the stratigraphic ranges of fossils, or assemblages of fossils, form the basis for the definition of biozones. Biostratigraphic units (biozones) are intervals of geological strata defined on the basis of their characteristic fossil taxa; the International Commission on Stratigraphy recognizes range zones, subdivided into taxon-range zones and concurrent-range zones.

  17. What is the known range of a zone fossil?
    Topic: Stratigraphy & Biostratigraphy • 3 marks marks

    The known range of a zone fossil is the time between its first and last appearances in a specific rock section — from its First Appearance Datum (FAD) to its Last Appearance Datum (LAD).

  18. What are index fossils?
    Topic: Stratigraphy & Biostratigraphy

    Index fossils (also known as guide fossils or indicator fossils) are fossils used to define and identify geologic periods (or faunal stages). Index fossils must have a short vertical range, wide geographic distribution and rapid evolutionary trends.

  19. Differentiate between a bed and a member.
    Topic: Stratigraphy & Biostratigraphy

    A bed is a lithologically distinct layer within a member or formation and is the smallest recognizable stratigraphic unit. A member is a more local lithologic development, usually part of a formation.

  20. Differentiate between lithostratigraphy and chronostratigraphy.
    Topic: Stratigraphy & Biostratigraphy

    Lithostratigraphy is a sub-discipline of stratigraphy, the geological science associated with the study of strata or rock layers; in general a stratum will be primarily igneous or sedimentary, relating to how the rock was formed. Chronostratigraphy is the branch of stratigraphy that studies the age of rock strata in relation to time; its ultimate aim is to arrange the sequence of deposition and the time of deposition of all rocks within a geological region, and eventually the entire geologic record of the Earth.

  21. Describe the origin of life in the early Precambrian world.
    Topic: Origin of Life & Early Life • 5 marks marks

    The Precambrian is divided into the Hadean, Archaean and Proterozoic eons. The Hadean Eon spans from the origin of the Earth, 4.57 to about 4 Ga; at its beginning, surface temperatures were too high and the crust too unstable for any form of carbon-based life to exist. As the Earth's surface cooled, the lithosphere began to differentiate. The Archaean lasted from about 4 to 2.5 Ga; the oldest rocks are from Canada, dated at 3.8–4 Ga, some mineral grains from Australia are dated to 4.4 Ga, and the oldest sedimentary rocks are from the Isua Group in Greenland, dated at 3.8–3.7 Ga. In the Proterozoic Eon (2.5 Ga to 542 Ma), atmospheric oxygen levels rose at 2.4 Ga to one-hundredth or one-tenth of modern levels, with a second rise around 0.8–0.6 Ga indicated by increased levels of marine sulfate.

  22. Name any two models of the origin of life and explain one of them.
    Topic: Origin of Life & Early Life

    The scientific models of the origin of life include: (1) spontaneous generation, (2) the inorganic model, (3) extraterrestrial origins, (4) the biochemical model, and (5) the hydrothermal model. In the inorganic model, complex organic molecules arose gradually on a pre-existing, non-organic replication platform — silicate crystals (Graham Cairns-Smith of Glasgow University); it is not conclusive.

  23. Where on Earth are the oldest rocks found? What are their ages?
    Topic: Origin of Life & Early Life • 5 marks marks

    The oldest rocks are from Canada and are dated at 3.8–4 Ga, and some mineral grains from Australia have even been dated to 4.4 Ga. The oldest sedimentary rocks are from the Isua Group in Greenland, dated at 3.8–3.7 Ga. The Archaean Eon lasted from about 4 to 2.5 Ga.

  24. What are the oldest fossils and their ages?
    Topic: Origin of Life & Early Life • 5 marks marks

    The oldest eukaryote fossil may be Grypania, a coiled, spaghetti-like organism reported from rocks as old as 1.85 Ga. The oldest locomotory trace fossils are from about 550 Ma in northwest Russia, and fecal strings from rocks some 600 Ma old suggest still older life. Bacteria in rocks up to 3.2 Ga are indicated by stromatolites.

  25. What is Bangiomorpha?
    Topic: Origin of Life & Early Life

    Bangiomorpha pubescens is a red alga and one of the oldest multicellular organisms (about 1.2 Ga). It is the first known sexually reproducing organism. A multicellular fossil of Bangiomorpha pubescens was recovered from the Hunting Formation in Somerset Island, Canada, and strongly resembles the modern red alga Bangia, despite occurring in rocks dating to 1,047 million years ago.

  26. Write the characteristics of protozoans.
    Topic: Origin of Life & Early Life

    Protozoa are notable for their ability to move independently, a characteristic found in the majority of species. They usually lack the capability for photosynthesis, although the genus Euglena is renowned for motility as well as photosynthesis (and is therefore considered both an alga and a protozoan).

  27. Write a note on the Cambrian explosion.
    Topic: Origin of Life & Early Life

    The Cambrian explosion generated a range of new body plans during a relatively short time interval. It was the evolutionary event in which most major animal groups first appeared in the fossil record.

  28. What is the endosymbiotic theory?
    Topic: Origin of Life & Early Life

    The endosymbiotic theory (Margulis, 1970s) is an evolutionary theory which assumes that endosymbiosis between prokaryotic endosymbionts and a eukaryotic host cell is the means by which organelles such as mitochondria and chloroplasts occur within eukaryotic cells: a prokaryote was consumed by, or invaded, another prokaryote, and the two species evolved together in a mutually beneficial way.

  29. Write four characteristic features of Phylum Mollusca.
    Topic: Invertebrate Groups • 5 marks marks

    (1) Unsegmented soft body with bilateral symmetry. (2) Presence of an internal or external shell. (3) A toothed tongue (made mostly of chitin) called the radula. (4) A mantle, which is a fold in the body wall that lines the shell. (5) Muscular foot (and/or tentacles in some).

  30. Write a note on Cephalopoda.
    Topic: Invertebrate Groups

    A cephalopod is any member of the molluscan class Cephalopoda, such as a squid, octopus or nautilus. These exclusively marine animals are characterized by bilateral body symmetry, a prominent head, and a set of arms or tentacles modified from the primitive molluscan foot. Cephalopods are the most advanced molluscs.

  31. Write the distinguishing features of Arthropoda.
    Topic: Invertebrate Groups

    The members of this phylum, despite their incredible diversity and sheer numbers, share a number of important distinguishing characteristics: exoskeleton; segmented bodies; jointed appendages; bilateral symmetry; and an open circulatory system.

  32. What are the Trilobitomorpha? What are their different body parts?
    Topic: Invertebrate Groups • 3 marks marks

    Trilobitomorpha are highly derived arthropods that lack specialized mouthparts. Their tagmata comprise the cephalon, thorax and pygidium. They are mainly the trilobites, and over 15,000 species are known; they were common throughout the Paleozoic and became extinct at the end of the Permian.

  33. What are helicoplacoids?
    Topic: Invertebrate Groups

    Helicoplacus is the earliest well-studied fossil echinoderm. Fossil plates are known from several regions, and complete specimens were found in Lower Cambrian strata of the White Mountains of California. The animal was a cigar-shaped creature up to 7 centimetres long that stood upright on one end.

  34. Describe the water vascular system of echinoderms.
    Topic: Invertebrate Groups

    The water vascular system is a hydraulic system used by echinoderms, such as sea stars and sea urchins, for locomotion, food and waste transportation, and respiration. The system is composed of canals connecting numerous tube feet.

  35. Why do paddle worms (polychaetes) have a complete fossil record?
    Topic: Invertebrate Groups • 3 marks marks

    A diverse polychaete fauna has been described from the Burgess Shale, which even contains Canadia spinosa, similar to some living polychaetes. Paddle worms have the most complete fossil record because their record is enhanced by the relatively common preservation of elements of the phosphatic jaw — the scolecodonts.

  36. Differentiate between infaunal and epifaunal organisms.
    Topic: Invertebrate Groups

    Infaunal organisms live beneath the sediment–water interface, while epifaunal organisms live above it.

  37. What is nannoplankton?
    Topic: Invertebrate Groups • 2 marks marks

    Nannoplankton are defined as plankton less than 63 μm across, the smallest standard mesh size for sieves — very small unicellular plankton at the limits of resolution of light microscopy. They include organic-walled and siliceous forms. Like other phytoplankton, nanophytoplankton are microscopic organisms that obtain energy through photosynthesis and must therefore live in the upper sunlit layer of the ocean or other bodies of water.

  38. What are deuterostomes?
    Topic: Invertebrate Groups • 5 marks marks

    Deuterostomes (“second mouth”) are animals in which the true blastopore develops as the anus and the mouth arises from a secondary opening. Deuterostomia is a subtaxon of the Bilateria, distinguished from protostomes by this deuterostomic embryonic development: in deuterostomes the first opening (the blastopore) becomes the anus, while in protostomes it becomes the mouth. The group includes the phyla Echinodermata, Hemichordata and Chordata.

  39. What is the triploblastic body plan?
    Topic: Invertebrate Groups • 3 marks marks

    Few basic body plans have appeared in the fossil record, and most animals have triploblastic architecture: three fundamental body layers — from the outside in, the ectoderm, mesoderm and endoderm.

  40. Write a note on insects and their origin.
    Topic: Invertebrate Groups

    Insects first appeared during the Early Devonian and diversified rapidly. There are probably 10 million species of living insects. Insects had probably already evolved flight before the Mid Carboniferous, when giant dragonflies patrolled the forests.

  41. Write a note on corals and coral reefs.
    Topic: Invertebrate Groups

    Coral reefs are the most diverse and most threatened ecosystems. Shallow-water coral reefs occur in the zone extending 30° north and south of the equator, in shallow water (about 30 m) where temperatures remain at or above about 18 °C.

  42. Name the “Big Five” mass extinction events.
    Topic: Evolution, Extinction & Zoogeography • 5 marks marks

    During mass extinctions, 20–90% of species were wiped out; these include a broad range of organisms, and the events appear to have happened rapidly. The “big five” Phanerozoic mass extinctions occurred at the: (1) End-Ordovician, (2) Late Devonian, (3) End-Permian, (4) End-Triassic, and (5) End-Cretaceous.

  43. Name the animal groups hit by the end-Permian mass extinction event.
    Topic: Evolution, Extinction & Zoogeography • 3 marks marks

    The end-Permian mass extinction was the largest of all time, probably caused by massive volcanic eruptions leading to acid rain and global anoxia. During the event a number of animal groups became extinct, including the trilobites, tabulate and rugose corals, and blastoids. Amphibians and reptiles continued to be the dominant land animals, and gymnosperms replaced ferns, clubmosses and horsetails as the dominant plants.

  44. What is the Great Ordovician Biodiversification Event (GOBE)?
    Topic: Evolution, Extinction & Zoogeography • 5 marks marks

    The Great Ordovician Biodiversification Event (GOBE) was the evolutionary radiation of animal life during the Ordovician Period, about 40 million years after the Cambrian explosion. It followed a series of Cambrian–Ordovician extinction events. The fauna went on to dominate the Palaeozoic, and marine diversity increased, with a staggering increase in biodiversity at the family, genus and species levels.

  45. Define speciation and write the names of two models of speciation.
    Topic: Evolution, Extinction & Zoogeography • 2 marks marks

    Speciation is the process of splitting of a population to form two species. It often occurs by the establishment of a barrier and the isolation of part of a previously interbreeding population. Two models are the allopatric (“other homeland”) model and the geographic model, based on the establishment of geographic barriers. Evolution takes place both within species lineages (phyletic gradualism) and at the time of speciation (punctuated equilibrium).

  46. What is sexual dimorphism?
    Topic: Evolution, Extinction & Zoogeography • 3 marks marks

    Sexual dimorphism is the condition where the two sexes of the same species exhibit different characteristics beyond the differences in their sexual organs — males and females may show different sizes and different specialized features. The condition occurs in many animals and some plants.

  47. Define a dimorphic organism with an example.
    Topic: Evolution, Extinction & Zoogeography

    Dimorphic fungi are fungi that can exist in the form of both mold and yeast. An example is Penicillium marneffei, a human pathogen that grows as a mold at room temperature and as a yeast at human body temperature.

  48. Differentiate between allometry and isometry.
    Topic: Evolution, Extinction & Zoogeography

    Allometry is the study of the differential growth rates of the parts of a living organism's body; the word comes from the Greek for “different measure”, and most organisms grow allometrically. Isometric growth is growth in which the proportions of the organism grow the same (Greek: “same measure”); very few organisms grow isometrically.

  49. What are plesiomorphies?
    Topic: Evolution, Extinction & Zoogeography

    In phylogenetics, a plesiomorphy (symplesiomorphy) is an ancestral character (trait state) shared by two or more taxa, but also shared with other taxa linked earlier in the clade — that is, taxa having an earlier last common ancestor with them than theirs.

  50. What is cladistics?
    Topic: Evolution, Extinction & Zoogeography

    Cladistics is an approach to biological classification in which organisms are categorized in groups based on the most recent common ancestor. It is a method of reconstructing phylogeny based on the identification of shared derived characters (homologies).

  51. What is biogeography?
    Topic: Evolution, Extinction & Zoogeography

    Biogeography is the branch of biology that deals with the geographical distribution of plants and animals.

  52. What is island biogeography?
    Topic: Evolution, Extinction & Zoogeography • 2 marks marks

    Island biogeography is the study of the species composition and species richness on islands; it is a field within biogeography that examines the factors affecting the species richness of isolated natural communities, a theory originally developed to explain the species richness of actual (principally oceanic) islands. Islands are areas of land not connected to a continent and surrounded by water; small islands are sometimes called cays, keys or islets, a group of islands is an archipelago, and there are two main types — continental islands and oceanic islands.

  53. What is the Isthmus of Panama? Discuss its significance.
    Topic: Evolution, Extinction & Zoogeography

    A barrier for some organisms may provide a corridor for others. The emergence of the Isthmus of Panama (about 3 Mya) connected North and South America and separated the Atlantic and Pacific oceans, allowing land animals to move between the continents while dividing marine populations.

  54. What is the Gaia hypothesis?
    Topic: Evolution, Extinction & Zoogeography

    The Gaia hypothesis views the Earth as a living organism in equilibrium with its biosphere — the Earth's physical and biological systems together maintain conditions suitable for life.

  55. What is a biocenosis (coenosis)?
    Topic: Evolution, Extinction & Zoogeography

    An ecosystem, originally defined by Tansley (1935), is a biotic community (or biocenosis) along with its physical environment (or biotope). In ecological studies, biocenosis is the emphasis on relationships between species in an area.

  56. What is the first fossil evidence of bipedalism?
    Topic: Evolution, Extinction & Zoogeography

    The oldest clear evidence for bipedalism is human tracks in volcanic ash from Tanzania, 3.75 Ma (the Laetoli footprints). The oldest substantial skeletons are those of Praeanthropus afarensis, 3.2 Ma.
