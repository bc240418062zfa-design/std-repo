  1. Differentiate between solids and liquids.
    Topic: Fluids and Flow Classification

    A solid can resist an applied shear (tangential) stress by deforming only slightly: in a solid the stress is proportional to the strain, and once the stress is removed the solid tends to recover its original shape. A solid therefore maintains its own definite shape and volume. A liquid, being a fluid, cannot resist a shear stress at all: no matter how small the applied shear stress, a liquid deforms continuously (it flows) as long as the stress acts. In a liquid the stress is proportional to the rate of strain (velocity gradient), not to the strain itself. A liquid has a definite volume but no definite shape of its own; it takes the shape of its container and forms a free surface under gravity. Also, a solid can resist normal as well as shear stresses while at rest, whereas a fluid at rest can sustain only normal stress (pressure) and is in a state of zero shear stress.

  2. Why do liquids have a free surface while gases do not?
    Topic: Fluids and Flow Classification

    A liquid has a free surface because its molecules are held together by strong cohesive forces: the molecules are close enough that the liquid maintains a definite volume, and the molecules at the top surface experience a net inward pull (surface tension) which keeps the surface intact, so the liquid does not expand to fill the container. The molecules of a gas, in contrast, are very far apart and the cohesive forces between them are negligible. Gas molecules move freely and randomly in all directions, so a gas has neither a definite volume nor a definite shape; it expands until it completely fills the whole container. Since there is no well-defined boundary of a fixed volume of gas, a gas cannot form a free surface — a free surface can exist only at the interface of a liquid with another fluid, normally its own vapor or air.

  3. What is the no-slip condition? State its important consequences.
    Topic: Fluids and Flow Classification

    When a fluid flows over a solid surface or through a pipe, the fluid layer in direct contact with the surface does not slip over it; because of viscous effects it sticks to the surface and acquires the velocity of the surface itself. For a stationary surface the fluid velocity at the surface is therefore zero. This is known as the no-slip condition. Its important consequences are: (i) all velocity profiles must have zero value (relative to the surface) at the points of contact between the fluid and the solid surface; (ii) the sticking layer slows down the adjacent fluid layers through viscous friction, which develops the velocity profile and the boundary layer near the wall; and (iii) it gives rise to surface drag, the force a fluid exerts on a surface in the flow direction.

  4. Differentiate between steady and unsteady flow, and between laminar and turbulent flow.
    Topic: Fluids and Flow Classification

    Steady flow: A flow is steady if the properties (velocity, pressure, temperature, density, etc.) at any fixed point do not change with time, i.e. ∂/∂t = 0 for all properties. In unsteady flow the properties at a point change with time; transient flow usually describes a starting or developing flow, while periodic flow oscillates about a steady mean. Laminar flow: The highly ordered fluid motion characterized by smooth layers (laminae) of fluid sliding over one another, typical of high-viscosity fluids at low velocities. Turbulent flow: The highly disordered fluid motion that typically occurs at high velocities and is characterized by velocity fluctuations and rapid mixing, typical of low-viscosity fluids such as air at high velocities. A flow that alternates between laminar and turbulent is called transitional. The Reynolds number is the key parameter that determines the flow regime.

  5. Differentiate between a closed system and a control volume.
    Topic: Fluids and Flow Classification

    A system is a quantity of matter or a region in space chosen for study; everything outside it is the surroundings, and the surface separating them is the boundary. A closed system (control mass) consists of a fixed amount of mass: no mass can cross its boundary, although energy in the form of heat or work can cross, and its volume need not be fixed. If even energy cannot cross the boundary, it is an isolated system. An open system, or control volume, is a properly selected region in space (for example the interior of a nozzle, turbine or compressor) through whose boundary — the control surface — both mass and energy can cross. Most engineering devices involving mass flow are analyzed as control volumes.

  6. Define density, specific volume, specific weight and specific gravity. For mercury (ρ = 13,600 kg/m³), calculate the specific gravity, specific volume and specific weight.
    Topic: Fluid Properties

    Density ρ is the mass per unit volume, ρ = m/V (kg/m³). Specific volume v is the volume per unit mass, v = V/m = 1/ρ (m³/kg). Specific weight γ is the weight per unit volume, γ = ρg (N/m³). Specific gravity (relative density) SG is the ratio of the density of a substance to the density of water at 4°C (ρ = 1000 kg/m³): SG = ρ/1000. Example: For mercury, ρ = 13,600 kg/m³. Hence SG = 13,600/1000 = 13.6; specific volume v = 1/13,600 = 7.35 × 10⁻⁵ m³/kg; specific weight γ = 13,600 × 9.81 = 133,416 N/m³ ≈ 133.4 kN/m³.

  7. State Newton's law of viscosity. How does viscosity vary with temperature for liquids and for gases, and why?
    Topic: Fluid Properties

    When a fluid layer moves over a stationary surface, experiment shows that the shear stress τ at the wall is directly proportional to the velocity gradient du/dy. For Newtonian fluids this gives Newton's law of viscosity: τ = μ (du/dy) where μ is the dynamic (absolute) viscosity of the fluid, a measure of its internal resistance to flow or deformation. The ratio ν = μ/ρ is called the kinematic viscosity. Temperature effect: The viscosity of liquids decreases with temperature, because at higher temperatures the liquid molecules have more energy and can more easily overcome the strong cohesive intermolecular forces. The viscosity of gases increases with temperature, because viscosity in gases is due to molecular collisions, and at higher temperatures the molecules move faster and collide more frequently, offering greater resistance to flow.

  8. What is vapor pressure? Explain the phenomenon of cavitation and its harmful effects.
    Topic: Fluid Properties

    The vapor pressure Pv of a pure substance is the pressure exerted by its vapor in phase equilibrium with its liquid at a given temperature; it is identical to the saturation pressure Psat. When the local pressure in a flowing liquid falls to (or below) the vapor pressure, vapor bubbles — cavitation bubbles — form in the liquid. When these bubbles are carried into regions of higher pressure they collapse violently, generating extremely high-pressure waves. This phenomenon is called cavitation. Its harmful effects are: drop in performance of hydraulic machines, erosion and pitting of surfaces (especially pump and turbine impeller blades), fatigue failure and eventual destruction of components, and annoying noise and vibrations. Cavitation must therefore be avoided or minimized in the design of pumps and hydraulic turbines.

  9. What is surface tension? Explain the capillary effect and write the formula for the height of capillary rise. Calculate the rise of water (σs = 0.073 N/m, φ = 0°) in a glass tube of diameter 2 mm.
    Topic: Fluid Properties

    Surface tension σs is the magnitude of the pulling force, acting parallel to the surface due to the attractive forces between liquid molecules, per unit length of the surface (N/m). It makes the liquid surface behave like a stretched elastic membrane and causes the liquid to minimize its surface area, which is why small droplets are spherical. The capillary effect is the rise (or fall) of a liquid in a narrow tube (capillary) caused by surface tension together with the adhesion between the liquid and the tube wall. The curved free surface in the tube is called the meniscus. If the contact angle φ < 90° the liquid wets the surface and rises; if φ > 90° it does not wet the surface and is depressed (e.g. mercury in glass, φ = 130°). The height of rise is h = 4σs cosφ / (ρgd). For water (σs = 0.073 N/m, φ ≈ 0°, ρ = 1000 kg/m³) in a glass tube of diameter d = 2 mm: h = 4(0.073)(1)/(1000 × 9.81 × 0.002) = 0.0149 m ≈ 1.49 cm.

  10. Derive the relation P = Patm + ρgh for the variation of pressure with depth in a fluid at rest. Also calculate the gauge and absolute pressure at a depth of 8 m in water.
    Topic: Pressure and Fluid Statics

    Consider a fluid at rest and a vertical column of the fluid of cross-sectional area A extending from the free surface (where the pressure is Patm) down to a depth h. The forces acting on this column in the vertical direction are: the atmospheric force Patm·A downward at the top, the pressure force P·A upward at the bottom, and the weight of the column W = ρgAh downward. Since the column is in equilibrium, ΣF = 0 gives: P·A = Patm·A + ρgAh ⟹ P = Patm + ρgh Thus the pressure in a fluid at rest increases linearly with depth, and the gauge pressure at depth h is simply Pgage = ρgh. The pressure difference between two points at depths differing by Δz is ΔP = ρgΔz. Example: At h = 8 m in water (ρ = 1000 kg/m³), Pgage = 1000 × 9.81 × 8 = 78,480 Pa ≈ 78.5 kPa, and the absolute pressure is P = 101.325 + 78.5 ≈ 179.8 kPa.

  11. State Pascal's law and explain its application in a hydraulic lift with a numerical example.
    Topic: Pressure and Fluid Statics

    Pascal's law states that the pressure applied to a confined fluid increases the pressure throughout the fluid by the same amount, i.e. pressure is transmitted undiminished in all directions. Pascal also noted that the force exerted by a fluid is proportional to the surface area. Application — hydraulic lift: Two cylinders of areas A1 (small) and A2 (large) are connected and filled with oil. A force F1 on the small piston creates pressure P = F1/A1, which acts equally on the large piston, giving F2 = P·A2 = F1 (A2/A1). Since A2 > A1, a small effort lifts a much larger load; this is the working principle of hydraulic lifts, brakes and presses. Example: F1 = 100 N, A1 = 0.005 m², A2 = 0.25 m²: P = 100/0.005 = 20,000 Pa; F2 = 20,000 × 0.25 = 5000 N.

  12. What is a manometer? How does a barometer measure atmospheric pressure?
    Topic: Pressure and Fluid Statics

    A manometer is a device, based on the principle that a fluid column can be used to measure pressure differences (ΔP = ρgΔz), that is commonly used to measure small and moderate pressure differences. It consists of a glass or plastic U-tube containing one or more fluids such as mercury, water, alcohol or oil; heavy fluids like mercury are used when large pressure differences are expected. Starting from one end and adding ρgh when going down and subtracting ρgh when going up through the fluid columns, the unknown pressure is obtained by equating pressures at the same horizontal level in a continuous fluid. A barometer is the device used to measure atmospheric pressure. In a mercury barometer, a tube filled with mercury is inverted into a mercury reservoir; the space above the mercury in the tube contains only mercury vapor at negligible pressure, so a vertical force balance gives Patm = ρgh, where h is the height of the mercury column. At standard conditions h = 760 mm, which defines 1 standard atmosphere (101.325 kPa). The height of the column does not depend on the length or cross-sectional area of the tube.

  13. State Archimedes' principle. What is the condition of floating, and what do you understand by the stability of immersed and floating bodies?
    Topic: Pressure and Fluid Statics

    Archimedes' principle states that the buoyant force acting on a body immersed in a fluid is equal to the weight of the fluid displaced by the body, and it acts upward through the centroid of the displaced volume: FB = ρfluid · g · Vdisplaced. The buoyant force is caused by the increase of pressure with depth and is independent of the depth of the body and of the density of the solid body. For a floating body, equilibrium requires that the weight of the whole body equals the buoyant force, i.e. the weight of the fluid whose volume equals the volume of the submerged portion of the body. Stability: A floating or immersed body is stable if, when slightly disturbed, it returns to its original position. For a completely immersed body, stability requires the center of gravity to be below the center of buoyancy. For floating bodies such as ships, stability is governed by the position of the metacentre relative to the center of gravity: the body is stable if the metacentre lies above the center of gravity. Stability assessment is of great importance in the design of ships and submarines.

  14. Differentiate between the Lagrangian and Eulerian descriptions of fluid flow.
    Topic: Fluid Kinematics

    Lagrangian description: The motion is described by following an individual fluid particle as it moves through the flow field, recording its position (xparticle(t), yparticle(t), zparticle(t)) and properties as functions of time. The equations of motion such as Newton's second law are directly applicable to the particle. Eulerian description: The motion is described at fixed points in space: the velocity and other properties are expressed as field variables, e.g. V = V(x, y, z, t), without following any particular particle. The material derivative provides the transformation between the two descriptions: applying it to a property gives the rate of change experienced by a moving fluid particle in terms of the Eulerian field variables.

  15. What is the material derivative? Write its expression and explain its local and advective parts.
    Topic: Fluid Kinematics

    The material derivative is the total time derivative following a fluid particle as it moves through the flow field. It is denoted by D/Dt and defined as: D/Dt = ∂/∂t + (V·∇) = ∂/∂t + u(∂/∂x) + v(∂/∂y) + w(∂/∂z) For example, the acceleration of a fluid particle is the material derivative of velocity: a = DV/Dt = ∂V/∂t + (V·∇)V. The first term, ∂/∂t, is the local part: it represents the change at a fixed point in space and is nonzero only in unsteady flow. The second term, (V·∇), is the advective (convective) part: it represents the change because the particle moves to a new location where the field variable has a different value, and it is nonzero in both steady and unsteady flows whenever spatial gradients exist. The material derivative is also called the total, particle, Lagrangian or substantial derivative.

  16. Write the components of the acceleration field in Cartesian coordinates and distinguish between local and advective acceleration.
    Topic: Fluid Kinematics

    For a velocity field V(x, y, z, t), the acceleration of a fluid particle is a = DV/Dt = ∂V/∂t + (V·∇)V, whose Cartesian components are: ax = ∂u/∂t + u(∂u/∂x) + v(∂u/∂y) + w(∂u/∂z) ay = ∂v/∂t + u(∂v/∂x) + v(∂v/∂y) + w(∂v/∂z) az = ∂w/∂t + u(∂w/∂x) + v(∂w/∂y) + w(∂w/∂z) The term ∂V/∂t is the local acceleration: it arises from the unsteadiness of the flow at a fixed point and vanishes in steady flow. The remaining terms form the advective (convective) acceleration: it arises because the particle moves into a region of different velocity, and it can be nonzero even in perfectly steady flow — for example, water particles accelerate while passing through the converging nozzle of a garden hose although the flow is steady. Advective acceleration is nonlinear and is the primary challenge in solving fluid flow problems.

  17. Calculate the y-component of advective acceleration for the velocity field V = (0.5 + 1.8x) i + (1.5 − 1.8y) j.
    Topic: Fluid Kinematics

    Given u = 0.5 + 1.8x and v = 1.5 − 1.8y (steady flow, w = 0). The y-component of acceleration is ay = ∂v/∂t + u(∂v/∂x) + v(∂v/∂y). Since the flow is steady, the local part ∂v/∂t = 0. Now ∂v/∂x = 0 and ∂v/∂y = −1.8. Advective part = u(∂v/∂x) + v(∂v/∂y) = (0.5 + 1.8x)(0) + (1.5 − 1.8y)(−1.8) = −1.8(1.5 − 1.8y) = −2.7 + 3.24y m/s². Hence the y-component of acceleration (which is entirely advective here) is ay = 3.24y − 2.7 m/s². (Similarly, ax = u(∂u/∂x) = (0.5 + 1.8x)(1.8) = 0.9 + 3.24x m/s².)

  18. Using the streamline formula, find the equation of the streamlines for the velocity field V = y² i + 3x² j.
    Topic: Fluid Kinematics

    A streamline is a curve that is everywhere tangent to the instantaneous local velocity vector. Along a streamline the arc-length element dr is parallel to V, which gives the streamline equation: dx/u = dy/v = dz/w Here the flow is two-dimensional with u = y² and v = 3x², so: dy/dx = v/u = 3x²/y² y² dy = 3x² dx Integrating: y³/3 = x³ + C, i.e. y³ − 3x³ = C1 (constant). This is the equation of the streamlines; each value of the constant C1 gives a different streamline.

  19. For the steady two-dimensional velocity field V = (0.5 + 0.8x) i + (1.5 − 0.8y) j, locate the stagnation point and find the acceleration components.
    Topic: Fluid Kinematics

    Given u = 0.5 + 0.8x, v = 1.5 − 0.8y (steady flow). Stagnation point: Set u = 0 ⟹ 0.5 + 0.8x = 0 ⟹ x = −0.625. Set v = 0 ⟹ 1.5 − 0.8y = 0 ⟹ y = 1.875. The stagnation point is (−0.625, 1.875). Acceleration: The flow is steady, so the local acceleration is zero. ax = u(∂u/∂x) + v(∂u/∂y) = (0.5 + 0.8x)(0.8) + (1.5 − 0.8y)(0) = 0.4 + 0.64x m/s². ay = u(∂v/∂x) + v(∂v/∂y) = (0.5 + 0.8x)(0) + (1.5 − 0.8y)(−0.8) = −1.2 + 0.64y m/s². Check at the stagnation point: ax = 0.4 + 0.64(−0.625) = 0 and ay = −1.2 + 0.64(1.875) = 0, as expected at a stagnation point.

  20. Define streamline, pathline and streakline. When do they coincide?
    Topic: Fluid Kinematics

    Streamline: A curve that is everywhere tangent to the instantaneous local velocity vector at a given instant of time. It is an instantaneous picture of the flow direction and is obtained from dx/u = dy/v = dz/w. Pathline: The actual path traveled by an individual fluid particle over a period of time, i.e. the trajectory of one marked particle, found by integrating the velocity following that particle. Streakline: The locus of all fluid particles that have passed sequentially through a prescribed point in the flow, e.g. the line formed by dye continuously injected at one point. In steady flow, streamlines, pathlines and streaklines all coincide; in unsteady flow they are generally different from one another.

  21. Describe the four fundamental types of motion and deformation of a fluid element, and write the expressions for the linear, volumetric and shear strain rates.
    Topic: Fluid Kinematics

    A fluid element may undergo four fundamental types of motion/deformation, best described as rates: (a) translation — rate given by the velocity V; (b) rotation — rate given by the angular velocity (rate of rotation) vector; (c) linear (extensional) strain — rate of increase of length per unit length, e.g. εxx = ∂u/∂x, εyy = ∂v/∂y, εzz = ∂w/∂z; and (d) shear strain — rate of decrease of the angle between two initially perpendicular lines, e.g. εxy = ½(∂u/∂y + ∂v/∂x). The volumetric strain rate is the sum of the linear strain rates: ∂u/∂x + ∂v/∂y + ∂w/∂z = ∇·V, and it is zero for incompressible flow. The linear and shear strain rates combine into the symmetric strain-rate tensor εij, which is used to relate fluid stress to strain rate.

  22. Define vorticity. Distinguish between rotational and irrotational flow, giving one example of each.
    Topic: Fluid Kinematics

    The vorticity vector ξ is defined as the curl of the velocity vector: ξ = ∇ × V. It is equal to twice the angular velocity (rate of rotation) of a fluid particle, i.e. ω = ξ/2. In Cartesian coordinates: ξ = (∂w/∂y − ∂v/∂z) i + (∂u/∂z − ∂w/∂x) j + (∂v/∂x − ∂u/∂y) k. If the vorticity at a point is nonzero, the fluid particles there are rotating and the flow in that region is called rotational. If the vorticity is zero (or negligibly small) throughout a region, the particles do not rotate and the flow is called irrotational in that region. Example — two circular flows: For solid-body rotation, uθ = ωr, the vorticity is ξ = 2ω k ≠ 0, so the flow is rotational (like a merry-go-round). For a line vortex, uθ = K/r, the vorticity is zero everywhere except at the origin, so the flow is irrotational (like a Ferris wheel) even though the streamlines are circular.

  23. State the Reynolds transport theorem and explain its importance in fluid mechanics.
    Topic: Reynolds Transport Theorem

    The Reynolds transport theorem (RTT) is the relationship between the time rate of change of an extensive property B of a system (a fixed mass, analyzed by following the fluid — the Lagrangian/system approach) and the rate of change of the same property within a control volume (a fixed region in space — the Eulerian/control-volume approach). It states that the time rate of change of B for the system equals the rate of change of B inside the control volume plus the net rate at which B is carried out of the control volume across the control surface: dBsys/dt = (∂/∂t)∫CV ρb dV + ∫CS ρb (V·n) dA, where b = B/m is the property per unit mass. It is the fundamental tool used to convert the basic laws written for systems (conservation of mass, Newton's second law, conservation of energy) into the control-volume form used in fluid mechanics. For B = mass (b = 1) it yields the conservation of mass (continuity) equation. Its derivation makes elegant use of the Leibniz theorem for differentiating an integral with moving limits.
