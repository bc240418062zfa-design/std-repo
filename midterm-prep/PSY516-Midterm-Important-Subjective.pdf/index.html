  1. Differentiate between descriptive and inferential statistics.
    Topic: Foundations of Statistics in Psychology

    Descriptive statistics are procedures used to summarize, organize and simplify data — for example arranging scores in a frequency table or graph, or computing an average — so that the researcher can see what happened in the study and communicate the results. Inferential statistics consist of techniques that allow us to study samples and then make generalizations about the populations from which they were selected; most research uses models such as Student's t-tests, ANOVA and regression analysis for this purpose. In short, descriptive statistics describe the data in hand, whereas inferential statistics use sample statistics to draw conclusions about population parameters, always allowing for sampling error — the naturally occurring discrepancy between a sample statistic and the corresponding population parameter.

  2. Define population, sample, parameter and statistic, with examples.
    Topic: Foundations of Statistics in Psychology

    A population is the set of all the individuals of interest in a particular study — for example, all preteen children whose self-esteem after parental divorce is being studied. A sample is a set of individuals selected from the population, usually intended to represent it, because populations are too large to measure completely. A parameter is a value, usually numerical, that describes a population and is derived from measurements of all its individuals — for example, the mean score of the whole population (μ). A statistic is a value that describes a sample, derived from the individuals in the sample — for example, the sample mean (M). Every population parameter has a corresponding sample statistic, and research uses statistics as the basis for answering questions about parameters.

  3. Describe the four scales of measurement with examples.
    Topic: Foundations of Statistics in Psychology

    (1) Nominal scale: a set of categories with different names; measurements only label and categorize observations (e.g. male/female, blood group). We can tell whether two individuals differ, but not the direction or size of the difference. A two-category nominal scale is called dichotomous. (2) Ordinal scale: categories organized in an ordered sequence, so observations are ranked by size or magnitude (e.g. finishing positions in a race, small/medium/large). Direction of difference is known, but not its size. (3) Interval scale: ordered categories that are all intervals of exactly the same size, so equal number differences reflect equal magnitude differences, but zero is arbitrary and does not mean absence of the variable (e.g. temperature in °F, where 0° does not mean no temperature). Ratios cannot be computed. (4) Ratio scale: an interval scale with an absolute zero point representing complete absence of the variable (e.g. weight, reaction time), so ratios of numbers reflect ratios of magnitude and all arithmetic operations are meaningful.

  4. Differentiate between discrete and continuous variables. What are real limits?
    Topic: Foundations of Statistics in Psychology

    A discrete variable consists of separate, indivisible categories; no values can exist between two neighbouring categories. Such variables are commonly restricted to whole, countable numbers — e.g. the number of children in a family can be 18 or 19 students in class, never 18.5. A continuous variable can take an infinite number of possible values between any two observed values and is divisible into an infinite number of fractional parts — e.g. time, height and weight. Because a continuous measurement is actually an interval, each score is defined by boundaries called real limits, positioned exactly halfway between adjacent scores: a weight of X = 150 pounds represents the interval from the lower real limit 149.5 to the upper real limit 150.5. Two consequences follow: identical measurements for two individuals should be very rare, and each category must be defined by its real limits.

  5. Compare the correlational and experimental methods. Why can correlation not prove causation?
    Topic: Research Methods and SPSS

    In the correlational method, two variables are observed as they exist naturally for a set of individuals, and the relationship between them is measured, most commonly with the Pearson Product Moment Correlation (r), which ranges from -1.00 to +1.00. Correlational research can establish reliability and validity, describe relationships and make predictions. In the experimental method, one variable is manipulated while another is observed and measured, and all other variables are controlled, in order to demonstrate a cause-and-effect relationship. A correlational study cannot demonstrate cause and effect because it provides no explanation for the relationship: a third, uncontrolled variable may be responsible (for example, late waking and low grades may both stem from another factor). Causal inference requires covariation between IV and DV, a time-order relationship (cause before effect), and elimination of confounding variables — conditions only the experimental method with manipulation and control (random assignment, matching, holding variables constant) can satisfy.

  6. What are quasi-experiments and other non-experimental methods? State their advantages and disadvantages.
    Topic: Research Methods and SPSS

    Non-experimental designs include correlational designs, quasi-experiments, case studies and observational designs. A quasi-experiment manipulates an independent variable (the quasi-independent variable) but has no random assignment of participants to conditions; its two subtypes are nonequivalent-groups studies, which compare pre-existing groups (e.g. boys vs girls, 8-year-olds vs 10-year-olds), and pre-post studies, which measure the same variable before and after a treatment without controlling the passage of time. A single case study is an intensive, detailed description and analysis of one case using interviews, tests and observations. Observational designs collect data by direct observation (participant, structured, or field experiments) or indirect observation (archival records, physical traces). Advantages: they provide new ideas and hypotheses, are the best method for rare phenomena, allow trying new therapeutic interventions, and can challenge or generate theories. Disadvantages: cause-and-effect assumptions are difficult, findings are often subjective and biased, generalization is problematic, and selection biases occur.

  7. Describe the main windows and views of SPSS.
    Topic: Research Methods and SPSS

    SPSS (Statistical Package for the Social Sciences) organizes data in a two-dimensional table in which rows represent cases (individuals) and columns represent variables (measurements such as age, sex, income). It has three working windows: (1) The Data Editor Window (.sav), which has two views — Data View, where rows are cases and columns are variables containing the recorded answers, and Variable View, where rows define each variable's characteristics: Name, Type, Width, Decimals, Label, Values, Missing, Columns, Align and Measure (Scale for continuous variables, Ordinal for ranked categories, Nominal for named categories). (2) The Output Viewer Window (.spv), which displays the results of analyses, with an outline on the left and the actual output on the right. (3) The Syntax Editor Window (.sps), which shows and runs command scripts, either pasted from dialog boxes or typed by the user. Analyses are run from menus such as Analyze (all statistical analyses and descriptive statistics), Data (sorting, selecting, merging cases), Transform (recoding and computing new variables) and Graphs (box plots, histograms, bar charts).

  8. What is a frequency distribution? Explain grouped frequency distributions and the rules for constructing them.
    Topic: Frequency Distributions, Central Tendency and Variability

    A frequency distribution is an organized tabulation of the number of individuals located in each category on the scale of measurement. It presents two elements: the set of categories that make up the original measurement scale, and a record of the frequency (f), or number of individuals, in each category. Proportions (p = f/N, also called relative frequencies) and percentages (p × 100) can be added as further columns. When scores cover a wide range, listing every score would need too many rows, so scores are grouped into class intervals, producing a grouped frequency distribution table. The rules of thumb are: use about 5 to 10 intervals (roughly 10) that cover the whole range; make all intervals the same width; choose a simple width (e.g. 5 or 10); and start the lowest interval at a multiple of the width so that the lowest score is included. For interval or ratio data the distribution is graphed as a histogram or polygon; for nominal or ordinal data a bar graph with spaces between bars is used.

  9. Define percentile and percentile rank. How are they obtained from a frequency distribution?
    Topic: Frequency Distributions, Central Tendency and Variability

    The percentile rank of a particular score is the percentage of individuals in the distribution with scores equal to or less than that value. When a score is identified by its percentile rank, the score itself is called a percentile. For example, if exactly 60% of a class scored 43 or lower on an exam, then X = 43 has a percentile rank of 60% and is called the 60th percentile. Percentile rank refers to a percentage; percentile refers to a score. To obtain them, one first counts the number of individuals at or below each point in the distribution, giving cumulative frequencies, and then converts these into cumulative percentages, which show the percentage of individuals accumulated up to each score as one moves up the scale.

  10. Compare the mean, median and mode. When should each be used?
    Topic: Frequency Distributions, Central Tendency and Variability

    The mean (μ = ΣX/N for a population; M = Σx/n for a sample) is the arithmetic average and uses every score; it is the preferred measure for inferential work because it is related to the standard deviation, but it is displaced by extreme scores. The median is the midpoint of the distribution — the point below which 50% of scores lie; it is preferred when there are extreme scores or a skewed distribution (e.g. incomes), for undetermined or incomplete data, for open-ended distributions, and for ordinal data, because extreme values do not affect it. The mode is the score or category with the greatest frequency; it is the only measure available for nominal data, gives sensible whole-number values for discrete variables (the typical family has 2 children, not 2.4), and describes the shape (peaks) of a distribution. In the handouts' example, a sample of scores mostly between 10 and 13 with one score of 100 has a mean of 20.3, which represents nobody, while the median of 11.5 is representative and would stay 11.5 even if the extreme score were 1000. In a perfectly symmetrical distribution the three measures coincide; in a positively skewed distribution they fall in the order mode, median, mean.

  11. For the scores 6, 8, 8, 10, 12, 8, 14, compute the mean, median and mode. Show your working.
    Topic: Frequency Distributions, Central Tendency and Variability

    Step 1 — Mean: Σx = 6 + 8 + 8 + 10 + 12 + 8 + 14 = 66; n = 7; M = Σx/n = 66/7 ≈ 9.43. Step 2 — Median: arrange the scores in order: 6, 8, 8, 8, 10, 12, 14. With n = 7 (odd), the median is the middle (4th) score = 8. Step 3 — Mode: the most frequent score is 8, which occurs three times, so the mode = 8. The mean (9.43) is pulled above the median by the high score of 14, while the median and mode (both 8) better represent the typical score.

  12. Define variance and standard deviation.
    Topic: Frequency Distributions, Central Tendency and Variability

    For the scores 4, 6, 8, 10, 12, compute SS, the population variance and standard deviation, and the sample variance and standard deviation. Variance is the mean of the squared deviation scores — the average squared distance from the mean; the standard deviation is the square root of the variance and measures the standard (average) distance of scores from the mean. Working: the mean of 4, 6, 8, 10, 12 is μ = 40/5 = 8. Deviations (X - μ): -4, -2, 0, +2, +4 (note they sum to zero). Squared deviations: 16, 4, 0, 4, 16. SS = Σ(X - μ)² = 40. Population variance σ² = SS/N = 40/5 = 8; population standard deviation σ = √8 ≈ 2.83. Sample variance s² = SS/(n - 1) = 40/4 = 10; sample standard deviation s = √10 ≈ 3.16. Squaring the deviations is necessary because the raw deviations always sum to zero, which would make their mean useless as a measure of variability.

  13. Why does the formula for sample variance divide by n - 1 rather than n? Discuss biased and unbiased estimators.
    Topic: Frequency Distributions, Central Tendency and Variability

    Samples consistently tend to be less variable than their populations, because the extreme scores that make population variability large are unlikely to be selected in a sample. Sample variability computed with n in the denominator therefore gives a biased estimate that systematically underestimates the population variance. The correction is to divide SS by n - 1 (the degrees of freedom, df = n - 1 — the number of scores that are independent and free to vary). Dividing by the smaller number increases the value just enough to remove the bias. A statistic is unbiased if the average of all possible sample values of that statistic equals the population parameter; it is biased if that average systematically underestimates or overestimates the parameter. With the n - 1 adjustment, sample variance is an unbiased estimator of population variance: individual samples may over- or underestimate, but on average they are accurate.

  14. What is a z-score? State its formula and the properties of a z-score distribution. Convert X = 130 from a distribution with μ = 100 and σ = 15 into a z-score.
    Topic: Z-Scores, Probability and Sampling Distributions

    A z-score specifies the precise location of each X value within a distribution: its sign tells whether the score is above (+) or below (-) the mean, and its numerical value tells the distance from the mean counted in standard deviations. The formula is z = (X - μ)/σ for a population (and z = (X - M)/s for a sample); conversely X = μ + zσ. If every score is transformed, the z-score distribution has three properties: (1) Shape — exactly the same shape as the original distribution, because transformation only relabels scores without moving them; (2) Mean — always 0, since the original mean becomes z = 0; (3) Standard deviation — always 1, so the z value itself equals the number of standard deviations from the mean. Worked example: z = (130 - 100)/15 = 30/15 = +2.00, meaning the score lies 2 standard deviations above the mean.

  15. State and explain the basic laws of probability: additive law, multiplicative law, joint and conditional probability.
    Topic: Z-Scores, Probability and Sampling Distributions

    Every probability lies between 0 and 1; the probabilities of all possible outcomes sum to 1; an impossible event has probability 0. Additive law: for a set of mutually exclusive (disjoint) events — events that cannot occur together, such as having blood type A and blood type B — the probability that one or another occurs equals the sum of their separate probabilities, P(A or B) = P(A) + P(B). Multiplicative law: the probability of the joint occurrence of two or more independent events equals the product of their individual probabilities — e.g. the probability that two dice both show 5 is (1/6)(1/6) = 1/36. A joint probability is the probability of the co-occurrence of two or more events; for independent events it is found by the multiplicative law. A conditional probability is the probability that one event will occur given that another event has already occurred — e.g. the probability of contracting AIDS given that a person is an intravenous drug user.

  16. IQ scores form a normal distribution with μ = 100 and σ = 15. Find the probability of randomly selecting an individual with an IQ score less than 120. Show the two-step procedure.
    Topic: Z-Scores, Probability and Sampling Distributions

    Step 1 — Transform the X value into a z-score: z = (X - μ)/σ = (120 - 100)/15 = 20/15 = +1.33. So IQ scores less than 120 correspond to z-scores less than +1.33. Step 2 — Use the unit normal table: because we want the proportion of the distribution in the body to the left of this point, we read column B (proportion in the body) for z = 1.33, which gives 0.9082. Therefore p(X < 120) = p(z < 1.33) = 0.9082, i.e. there is about a 90.82% probability that a randomly selected individual has an IQ below 120. (Sketching the normal curve and shading the required area first is recommended to avoid errors.)

  17. What is the binomial distribution? When and how is it approximated by the normal distribution?
    Topic: Z-Scores, Probability and Sampling Distributions

    Binomial data arise when a variable is measured on a scale of exactly two categories ('two names') — heads/tails, male/female, high/low aggression. Using the notation p = p(A), q = p(B) = 1 - p, n = number of observations and X = number of times category A occurs (0 to n), the binomial distribution gives the probability of each value of X. The binomial distribution tends toward a normal shape as n grows; it is nearly perfectly normal when pn and qn are both equal to or greater than 10. Under those conditions it is approximated by a normal distribution with mean μ = np and standard deviation σ = √(npq), and probabilities are computed from z-scores and the unit normal table — remembering that binomial X values are discrete (each value corresponds to a histogram bar bounded by real limits, e.g. X = 6 spans 5.5 to 6.5) while the normal curve is continuous. If p < 0.5 the binomial distribution is skewed to the right.

  18. What is a sampling distribution? Describe the central limit theorem and the standard error of the mean, with an example.
    Topic: Z-Scores, Probability and Sampling Distributions

    A sampling distribution is a distribution of statistics obtained by selecting all possible samples of a specific size from a population; the distribution of sample means (the sampling distribution of M) is the collection of means of all possible random samples of size n. The central limit theorem describes it precisely: for any population with mean μ and standard deviation σ, the distribution of sample means for sample size n will (1) have a mean equal to μ — called the expected value of M; (2) have a standard deviation of σ/√n; and (3) approach a normal distribution as n increases (it is almost perfectly normal if the population is normal or n is around 30 or more). The standard deviation of this distribution, σM = σ/√n, is called the standard error of M: it describes how much difference is expected between sample means and how well a single sample mean represents the population. Example: with σ = 20 and n = 25, σM = 20/√25 = 20/5 = 4. By the law of large numbers, larger samples give smaller standard errors and sample means closer to μ.

  19. What is a confidence interval? Construct the 95% confidence interval for the mean age of graduate students when a sample of n = 30 has M = 33 years and SD = 4.3 years.
    Topic: Confidence Intervals and Hypothesis Testing

    A confidence interval is an interval, or range of values, centred around a sample statistic (the point estimate), within which the true population parameter is expected to lie; the most common confidence level is 95%, and a 100% interval is impossible unless the whole population is measured. Wider confidence (99%) gives a wider interval than 95% from the same sample. Construction: the standard error of the mean is SEM = s/√n = 4.3/√30 ≈ 4.3/5.48 ≈ 0.79. For a 95% interval we add and subtract about 2 standard errors (≈1.96 exactly): CI = M ± 2(SEM) = 33 ± 2(0.79) = 33 ± 1.57, giving approximately 31.43 to 34.57 years. Interpretation: we are 95% confident that the mean age of the entire population of graduate students lies between about 31.4 and 34.6 years; 95% of intervals constructed this way contain the true mean, and 5% do not.

  20. Describe the four steps of hypothesis testing.
    Topic: Confidence Intervals and Hypothesis Testing

    Step 1 — State the hypotheses: two opposing statements about the population parameter. The null hypothesis (H0) states there is no change, no effect and no difference (e.g. H0: μ = 80 even with the treatment); the alternative hypothesis (H1) states there is a change, difference or relationship (H1: μ ≠ 80 for a non-directional test, or a specified direction such as μ > 80 for a directional, one-tailed test). Step 2 — Set the criteria for a decision: choose the alpha level (level of significance, commonly .05, .01 or .001), which defines the critical region — the extreme sample means in the tails of the distribution of sample means that are very unlikely (e.g. the most unlikely 5%) if H0 is true. Step 3 — Collect data and compute the sample statistics: obtain the sample, compute the sample mean, and convert it to a z-score, z = (M - μ)/σM, locating the sample mean in the distribution predicted by H0. Step 4 — Make a decision: if the z-score falls in the critical region, reject H0 and conclude the treatment had an effect; if it falls outside, fail to reject H0 and conclude the treatment does not appear to have an effect. In the handouts' example (μ = 80, σM = 4), M = 92 gives z = +3.00, inside the critical region beyond 1.96, so H0 is rejected, whereas M = 84 gives z = +1.00, so H0 is not rejected.

  21. Differentiate between Type I and Type II errors. What is the power of a test, and what factors affect it?
    Topic: Confidence Intervals and Hypothesis Testing

    A Type I error occurs when a researcher rejects a null hypothesis that is actually true — concluding a treatment has an effect when it has none (a 'false positive'), often because an extreme, non-representative sample was obtained by chance. The alpha level is the probability of a Type I error, which is why many researchers use .01 or .001 to reduce the risk. A Type II error occurs when a researcher fails to reject a null hypothesis that is really false — the test fails to detect a real treatment effect (a 'false negative'); its probability is denoted β. The power of a test is the probability that it will correctly reject a false null hypothesis, i.e. identify a treatment effect if one really exists; power = 1 - β. Factors affecting power: sample size (larger samples give greater power; reducing n can drop power below 50%); alpha level (lowering α from .05 to .01 reduces power); and the form of the test (a one-tailed test has more power than a two-tailed test). The size of the z-score, and hence the decision, is also influenced by the mean difference, the variability of scores and the sample size through the standard error.

  22. Compare one-tailed and two-tailed hypothesis tests.
    Topic: Confidence Intervals and Hypothesis Testing

    In a directional (one-tailed) test, the hypotheses specify either an increase or a decrease in the population mean — for example H1: μ > 80 (the supplement increases scores) with H0 stating scores are not increased. The whole critical region lies in one tail of the distribution. In a non-directional (two-tailed) test, H1 states only that there will be some effect in either direction (H1: μ ≠ 80), and the alpha level and critical region are split between both tails. The major distinction is the criterion for rejecting H0: a one-tailed test can reject H0 with a relatively small difference, provided it is in the specified direction, so it has greater power in that direction; a two-tailed test requires a relatively large difference, independent of direction. One-tailed tests are appropriate only when a specific direction is predicted in advance.
