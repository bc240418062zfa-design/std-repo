  1. Trace the origin and development of the newspaper.
    Topic: Core Subjective Topics

    By DeFleur's definition, a newspaper is produced by a mechanical printing process, is available for a price to people of all walks of life, prints news of general interest, is readable by people of ordinary literacy, is timely and is stable over time. By this definition the first newspaper was the Oxford Gazette, later called the London Gazette, first published in 1665 under the authority of King Charles II. The first daily newspaper in English, the Daily Courant, began publishing in London on 11 March 1702. In France, The Gazette was founded in 1631. In America, Publick Occurrences appeared in Boston in 1690, while the first American paper fitting DeFleur's definition was The Boston News-Letter (1704). In 1721 James Franklin started the New England Courant, which was not published by authority and appealed to a well-educated, prosperous elite.

  2. What was the significance of the Zenger trial for the freedom of the press?
    Topic: Core Subjective Topics

    John Peter Zenger started The New York Weekly Journal in 1733 in opposition to the officially authorized New York Gazette of Governor William Cosby. He was jailed on a charge of seditious libel for criticizing the governor. Zenger ultimately won the case. The significance of the trial was that it set an important legal precedent: the press should be allowed to criticize the government. It became a landmark in the unfolding struggle to establish a free press in the colonies.

  3. Discuss the Penny Press and its role in the development of mass media.
    Topic: Core Subjective Topics

    On 3 September 1833 Benjamin Day started The New York Sun in New York. It was designed to appeal to less sophisticated people, it employed the first salaried reporter, and it was sold on the streets by newsboys for only a penny. The paper was an instant success, soon selling more than 8,000 copies a day. The Sun spurred a revolution in newspaper publishing: within a few months it had competitors, and together these competing cheap newspapers were known as the penny press. The New York Herald, founded in 1835 by James Gordon Bennett, added features such as a financial page, editorial comment and more serious local, foreign and national news. With the penny press, the mass media became a reality.

  4. What is Yellow Journalism? How did it get its name?
    Topic: Core Subjective Topics

    Yellow journalism is the sensational style of journalism that developed in the last decade of the 19th century out of the competition for readers. Joseph Pulitzer built the circulation of the New York Sunday World to over 300,000 by combining good reporting with crusades, an emphasis on disasters and melodramatics, sensational photographs and comic strips. He pioneered the use of colour printing of comics in newspapers. One famous cartoon in the paper featured a bald-headed, toothless, grinning kid clad in a yellow sack-like garment, called the Yellow Kid, which depicted life in the slums of New York and became very popular. Historians believe the label 'yellow journalism' was derived from this cartoon character, symbolizing the newspapers' mindless intellectual level.

  5. Write a note on the beginning of broadcasting in the subcontinent.
    Topic: Core Subjective Topics

    In March 1926 the Indian Broadcasting Company, a private company, was formed, and on 23 July 1927 the IBC started a station at Bombay - the beginning of broadcasting in the subcontinent. A small transmitting station was set up at Lahore in 1928. In April 1930 broadcasting came under the direct control of the government under the title Indian State Broadcasting Service. The Peshawar Radio Station was set up in January 1935, the Delhi station opened in January 1936 with A. S. Bukhari as Station Director, and in June 1936 the Indian State Broadcasting Service was changed into All India Radio. The Lahore station went on air in December 1937 and the Dhaka station in 1939. After independence, the first news bulletin went on air from Radio Pakistan on 14 August 1947.

  6. Write a note on the development of television in Pakistan.
    Topic: Core Subjective Topics

    Television came late to Pakistan mainly because no trained technical staff was available. The country's first TV station was set up in Lahore on 26 November 1964. PTV began as a black-and-white service, and its most prominent early feature was live transmission, since it had no recording facilities; news, talks, plays and music were all broadcast live. In 1973 all TV stations in the country were linked by a microwave network, enabling simultaneous live telecasts across the country, a facility fully exploited during the Lahore Islamic Summit of February 1974. Colour transmission started on an experimental basis in 1976, and regular colour transmission began on 18 February 1982. Private TV channels began functioning after 2002, when the first private channel received its licence from PEMRA.

  7. Compare the NTSC, PAL and SECAM television transmission systems.
    Topic: Core Subjective Topics

    There are three main television transmission standards used throughout the world. NTSC (National Television Standards Committee) is the oldest existing standard, developed in the USA and first used in 1954; it consists of 525 horizontal lines of display and 60 vertical lines. SECAM (Systeme Electronique pour Couleur avec Memoire) was developed in France and first used in 1967; it is a 625-line vertical, 50-line horizontal display. PAL (Phase Alternating Line) was developed by the German engineer Walter Bruch, who patented his invention in 1963; its first commercial application was in August 1967. PAL is also a 625/50-line display and is an alternative to NTSC.

  8. Who were the Big Five studios? How did they control the American film industry?
    Topic: Core Subjective Topics

    By 1929 the film-making firms that ruled and monopolized Hollywood were the giants known as the Big Five: Warner Bros. Pictures, Paramount Pictures, RKO Pictures, Metro-Goldwyn-Mayer and Fox Film Corporation. They produced more than 90 percent of the fiction films in America and distributed their films nationally and internationally. Their control rested on vertical integration: production, distribution and exhibition were integrated under one direction. They owned their own film-exhibiting theatres - about 50 percent of the seating capacity in the US, mostly first-run houses in major cities - and required blind or block booking, whereby theatre owners had to rent a block of films, including cheaply made B-pictures, to obtain the one prestige A-level picture they wanted. This monopolistic studio control lasted about twenty years, until the late 1940s, when a federal decree in U.S. vs. Paramount ordered the studios to divest their theatres.

  9. Write a note on the beginning of cinema in the subcontinent.
    Topic: Core Subjective Topics

    The Lumiere Brothers of France exhibited their short films in December 1895 at the Grand Cafe, Paris, and the following year brought the show to India, holding its premiere at the Watson Hotel in Bombay on 7 July 1896. Raja Harishchandra (1913), made by Dadasaheb Phalke, was the first silent feature film made in the subcontinent; Phalke built a studio on Dadar Main Road, wrote the scenario, erected the set and completed the film, which was released at the Coronation Cinema on 21 April 1913. Madan Theatres of Calcutta produced Shirin Farhad and Laila Majnu (1931), well-composed and recorded musicals that established the hold of songs on local films. The first Indian sound film, Ardeshir Irani's Alam Ara, was released on 14 March 1931 and was a super hit. Ardeshir Irani's Kisan Kanya (1937) was the first colour film.

  10. Define new media. How does it differ from old media?
    Topic: Core Subjective Topics

    New media refers to forms of human and media communication that have been transformed by the creative use of technology to fulfil the basic social need to interact and transact; it is the marriage of technology, communication and design. New media can be defined as graphics, moving images, sounds, shapes, spaces and texts that have become computable - that is, they comprise simply another set of computer data. Its forms include websites, blogs and wikis, email, interactive television, mobile devices, podcasting, video games and virtual worlds. The distinction between new media and old media is not sharp: from 1995 to 2004 old media expanded into producing new media, blurring the boundaries, and much old media content was re-purposed in digital format. The term 'new media' gained popular currency in the mid-1990s with the proliferation of interactive CD-ROMs and the emergence of the mass consumer internet. A key advantage of new media is two-way, individualized communication: a sender can now communicate a unique message to each recipient, and can individualize simultaneous messages sent to a mass of recipients.

  11. What is the Audit Bureau of Circulations? Discuss its functions.
    Topic: Core Subjective Topics

    The Audit Bureau of Circulations (ABC) is one of several organizations of the same name operating in different parts of the world; the majority of these bureaus are members of the International Federation of Audit Bureau of Circulations. It audits circulation, readership and audience information for the magazines, newspapers and other publications produced by its members. ABC of North America is the world's first and largest not-for-profit circulation-auditing organization and a forum of the world's leading magazine and newspaper publishers, advertisers and advertising agencies. Funded by dues and service fees, it provides credible, verified information critical to the media buying and selling process by conducting independent, third-party audits of print circulation, readership and website activity, and it maintains the world's foremost electronic database of audited circulation and readership media. Membership is open to all publishers, advertisers and advertising agencies.

  12. Write a note on Yomiuri Shimbun as the world's biggest newspaper.
    Topic: Core Subjective Topics

    Yomiuri Shimbun of Japan is the biggest newspaper in the world. Its combined morning and evening circulation of 13.6 million - including a 30,000-copy daily edition in English - is bigger than that of the 17 largest U.S. dailies put together. It operates 436 bureaus in Japan and 28 in the rest of the world, and its editorial staff of 3,059, quadruple that of The New York Times, produces a daily paper of 24 to 32 pages with numerous updated and regional editions. The paper reaches 38 percent of Japan's 34 million households, almost all by home delivery, and more than 60 percent of subscribers buy both the morning and evening editions. It also operates Japan's foremost professional baseball team, the Yomiuri Giants (founded in 1934 as a circulation gimmick), the Yomiuriland amusement park and a symphony orchestra. Unlike big U.S. papers, which earn up to 70 percent of revenue from advertising, only about 40 percent of Yomiuri's revenues come from advertising, and most of its top officers are former reporters or editors.

  13. What is globalization? Discuss its impact on mass media.
    Topic: Core Subjective Topics

    Globalization is the increasing interconnectedness of people and places as a result of advances in transport and communication technologies. In economics, it is the convergence of prices, products, wages, rates of interest and profits towards developed-country norms. For mass media, globalization means that a small number of giant transnational corporations dominate the global media system: news agencies, broadcasting houses, TV channels and films of a few developed countries rule the world of mass media and can build or change any country's image according to their own wishes. Global flows of media content are largely one-way, from the developed West to the developing world, which produces cultural homogenization and threatens indigenous cultures and identities. At the same time, new communication technologies allow audiences and independent media to organize across borders, as when anti-globalization protesters coordinated the 1999 Seattle WTO protests through the internet and the Independent Media Center.

  14. Discuss the first tier of global media giants.
    Topic: Core Subjective Topics

    The global media system is now dominated by a first tier of nine giant firms. The five largest are Time Warner (1997 sales: $24 billion), Disney ($22 billion), Bertelsmann ($15 billion), Viacom ($13 billion) and News Corporation. The first tier is rounded out by TCI, the largest U.S. cable company, which also has U.S. and global media holdings in scores of ventures; the other first-tier firms include General Electric (owner of NBC), Sony (owner of Columbia and TriStar Pictures and major recording interests) and Seagram (owner of Universal film and music interests). Bertelsmann is the one European firm in the first tier. Below the first tier are around 50 or so 'second tier' companies doing media-related business at the national or regional level, each doing more than one billion dollars' worth of business. Compared with the 1980s, this is a very limited market in terms of diversity of ownership.

  15. What is the New World Information and Communication Order (NWICO)? Discuss the role of the MacBride Commission.
    Topic: Core Subjective Topics

    NWICO was the demand of the developing countries for a more balanced global flow of information, ending the monopoly of a few developed countries over mass media. In 1976 UNESCO's Director General Amadou-Mahtar M'Bow was authorized to appoint an International Commission for the Study of Communication Problems. The commission, led by Sean MacBride, former foreign minister of Ireland and recipient of both the Nobel and Lenin Peace Prizes, produced the report 'Many Voices, One World' (1980), which supported free reporting of news but also encouraged state regulation of the media. In October 1980 the 21st General Conference of UNESCO at Belgrade received the report, and the Group of 77, a bloc of more than 100 developing countries, put forward a detailed description of a 'New World Information Order'. The assembly accepted for the first time that a new information order could be defined, and agreed on guidelines including the elimination of imbalances and inequalities in information flows, removal of obstacles to a free and better balanced dissemination of information, plurality of sources and channels, freedom of the press, and respect for each people's cultural identity. Western media reacted with fury, and the attitude of the media giants towards NWICO and the MacBride report caused the idea to fail in practice.

  16. Why did the United States and the United Kingdom withdraw from UNESCO in the 1980s?
    Topic: Core Subjective Topics

    Western governments and media organizations opposed UNESCO's involvement in the new information order debate, which they saw as an attempt to regulate news content and restrict the free flow of information. In May 1981, some 100 representatives of print and broadcast organizations from the U.S. and 20 other nations adopted the Declaration of Talloires, calling on UNESCO to abandon attempts to regulate news content and formulate rules for the press. U.S. officials charged that UNESCO had lent itself to 'a massive assault on the free flow of information'. In 1984 the United States withheld its contributions and withdrew from UNESCO in protest, followed by the United Kingdom in 1985 and Singapore in 1986. The UK rejoined in 1997 after a change of government, and the United States rejoined in 2003.

  17. What is concentration of media ownership? Discuss its dangers for democracy.
    Topic: Core Subjective Topics

    Concentration of media ownership, also known as media consolidation, describes a situation in which the majority of media outlets are owned by a small number of conglomerates and corporations; it may refer to oligopoly or monopoly within a media industry. The concern is that concentration increases the economic and political influence of a few owners, which can itself be unaccountable. If media moguls control both media content and media distribution, they have a lock on the extent and range of diverse views and information available to citizens, which is potentially dangerous for any democracy. Concentrated ownership risks less aggressive news coverage and a more muted marketplace of ideas: outlets are unlikely to criticize the policies or interests of their parent companies or advertisers. Since most citizens form their views of the world from mainstream media, reduced diversity of issues and perspectives directly weakens democratic life, even though corporate media can at times check governments and break corrupt state-controlled systems.

  18. Define vertical integration and interlocking directorates in the context of media conglomerates.
    Topic: Core Subjective Topics

    Vertical integration means that a company owns operations and businesses across the whole chain of an industry - for a media company, production, distribution and exhibition, and even related verticals such as toys and clothing manufacture and retailing. In film, the Big Five studios were vertically integrated, owning production facilities, distribution networks and theatre chains. Vertical integration enhances market power and allows cross-promotion and cross-selling of media properties across sectors, but corporations that control a total process have few motives for genuine innovation and can squeeze out competitors, distorting the economy with monopolistic control over prices. Interlocking directorates is a related practice in which a director of one company sits on the board of another company. As the U.S. media watchdog FAIR has pointed out, media corporations share board members with banks, investment companies, oil companies, health care and pharmaceutical companies and technology companies, tying media power to wider corporate interests.

  19. How do power blocks use and exploit the mass media? Explain with reference to war coverage.
    Topic: Core Subjective Topics

    Powerful governments and elites use the media to manufacture consent for their policies, through censorship by omission, misuse of language, and the construction of media spectacles. During the 2003 war against Iraq, which began on 19 March 2003 with a dramatic attempt to 'decapitate' the Iraqi regime, the media spectacle of war was carefully managed. The dramatic story of 'Saving Private Lynch' was one of the most spectacular human-interest stories of the war: it revealed the constructed and spectacle nature of the event and the ways the Pentagon constructed mythologies that were replicated by the TV networks. Since the Persian Gulf War of 1991, conflicts with Iraq were framed within the so-called 'war on terror'. As Goering observed, people can be brought to the bidding of their leaders simply by telling them they are being attacked and denouncing peacemakers for lack of patriotism - a formula that works in any country. A free and impartial media is a key pillar of democracy, yet journalists in many countries face censorship, threats and violence for reporting against the interests of power holders.

  20. Differentiate between human trafficking and people smuggling.
    Topic: Core Subjective Topics

    Human trafficking differs from people's smuggling in the element of exploitation. Smuggling is the illegal transportation of people across borders, usually with the consent of the person being smuggled, and the relationship between smuggler and migrant generally ends on arrival at the destination. Trafficking, by contrast, involves the recruitment, transportation or harbouring of persons by means of force, deception or coercion for the purpose of exploitation, such as forced labour or sexual exploitation, and the exploitation continues after arrival. United States State Department data estimated that 600,000 to 820,000 men, women and children are trafficked across international borders each year, and approximately 80 percent of them are women and girls. High levels of human smuggling and trafficking are driven by factors such as unemployment, insufficient income and a non-supportive family environment.

  21. Discuss the feminization of poverty.
    Topic: Core Subjective Topics

    The 'feminization of poverty' is the phenomenon, unfortunately on the increase, whereby women make up a growing and disproportionate share of the world's poor - sociologists note that two out of every three poor adults are women. Its causes include women's lower wages and concentration in low-paid and informal work, their greater responsibility for unpaid care and household work, limited access to education, land, credit and property, and the rise of female-headed households. Women's poverty is deepened by discrimination in labour markets and by development policies that ignore gender. Because women are usually responsible for children's nutrition, health and schooling, their poverty is transmitted to the next generation, making the feminization of poverty a central issue in development and in media coverage of social issues.

  22. How do media sell images and values, particularly regarding women's body image?
    Topic: Core Subjective Topics

    Mass media present images and values that audiences copy, so the media indirectly reshape patterns of life, traditions and culture. Images of female bodies are everywhere: women - and their body parts - sell everything from food to cars, while popular film and television actresses are becoming younger, taller and thinner. Analysts argue the roots of these beauty standards are economic: by presenting an ideal that is difficult to achieve and maintain, the cosmetic and diet industries are assured of growth and profits - the diet industry alone is worth an estimated $100 billion (U.S.) a year. Women who are insecure about their bodies are more likely to buy beauty products, clothes and diet aids. On the other hand, research indicates that exposure to images of thin, young, air-brushed female bodies is linked to depression, loss of self-esteem and the development of unhealthy eating habits in women and girls; one American research group reports that one out of every four college-aged women uses unhealthy methods of weight control.

  23. Define terrorism. Differentiate between extremism and fundamentalism.
    Topic: Core Subjective Topics

    Terrorism refers to the purposeful use of violence or threats of violence by groups or individuals in order to serve political or personal agendas. Chemical terrorism includes the purposeful contamination of water and food supplies or the aerosolization of toxicants in enclosed public spaces, while biological terrorism includes contamination with infectious materials or the release of infected vectors. Extremism and fundamentalism are often treated as identical by Western scholars, but most Muslim scholars view them as different - former Malaysian Prime Minister Dr. Mahathir Muhammad, for example, said he was a fundamentalist but not an extremist. Fundamentalism means adherence to fundamentals: the interpretation of every word of the sacred texts as literal truth. Extremism means radicalism and fanaticism, and is usually used for ideologies that believe in the use of violence; the terms 'extremist' and 'radical' are often used as labels for those who advocate or use violence against the will of the larger social body.

  24. What is an information society? Discuss its main features.
    Topic: Core Subjective Topics

    An information society is a society in which the creation, distribution, diffusion, use and manipulation of information is a significant economic, political and cultural activity; its economic counterpart is the knowledge economy, in which wealth is created through the economic exploitation of understanding. Information technology occupies a central position in production, the economy and society at large, and the information society is seen as the successor to industrial society. Related concepts include Daniel Bell's post-industrial society and Manuel Castells' network society. Fritz Machlup introduced the concept of the knowledge industry, distinguishing five sectors - education, research and development, mass media, information technologies and information services - and calculated that in 1959, 29 percent of the GNP of the USA was produced in knowledge industries. Peter Drucker argued there is a transition from an economy based on material goods to one based on knowledge, and Marc Porat distinguished primary and secondary information sectors. On such indicators, an information society is one where more than half of the GNP is produced, and more than half of the employees are active, in the information economy.

  25. Define propaganda and discuss its types.
    Topic: Core Subjective Topics

    Propaganda is the planned dissemination of news, information, special arguments and appeals designed to influence the beliefs, thoughts and actions of a specific group; it is a message conveyed to support and spread a particular opinion or point of view, engaging the emotions of the audience. Harold Lasswell, a pioneer of propaganda studies, defined it as 'the management of collective attitudes by the manipulation of significant symbols'. The term comes from the Congregatio de Propaganda Fide, a missionary organization established by the Pope in 1622. One common classification divides propaganda into three types. White propaganda is correctly attributed to the sponsor and the source is truthfully identified - for example, the Voice of America broadcasts white propaganda. Grey propaganda is unattributed and conceals the real source of the propaganda, advancing viewpoints in the interest of the sponsor. Black propaganda also masks the sponsor's participation, but unlike grey propaganda it is falsely attributed; it is subversive and provocative and is usually prepared by secret agents or an intelligence service.

  26. Discuss the role of ICTs and national e-strategies in development.
    Topic: Core Subjective Topics

    Information and communication technologies can accelerate development by improving education, health, governance, trade and access to information, but these benefits require deliberate national planning. A national e-strategy guides and focuses government priorities in ICT development: it explains how institutions interact, how they share resources and responsibilities, and it involves leaders from government, the private sector, academia and civil society. E-strategies have been on the international development agenda in recent years, with the G-8 and the United Nations, among others, pressing developing countries to establish ICT programmes. In 2000 the Digital Opportunity Task Force (DOT Force) was launched under the auspices of the G-8; in 2001 the UN Secretary General created the UN ICT Task Force; and in 2003 world leaders at the first phase of the World Summit on the Information Society (WSIS) adopted a Plan of Action encouraging national e-strategies. Countries that launched e-strategies early are reaping benefits today - Singapore began its ICT programme in 1991, the United States in 1993, followed shortly by Canada, Japan and most European nations.

  27. What is the digital divide? Discuss its dimensions.
    Topic: Core Subjective Topics

    The digital divide is the gap between those who have access to information and communication technologies - the information rich - and those who do not - the information poor. It operates between countries: ICT infrastructure and internet use are concentrated in the developed world, and surveys cited in the course show the share of 15- to 24-year-olds who had ever used the internet ranging from less than 1 percent in Ethiopia to 12 percent in Indonesia, 15 percent in Egypt, 29 percent in Armenia and 53 percent in China. It also operates within countries, between urban and rural areas, rich and poor, men and women, and the educated and uneducated. Nicholas Negroponte, chairman of the MIT Media Lab, described the digital divide as less economically driven than generational and educational. Bridging the divide requires infrastructure, affordable access, relevant local content and digital literacy, which is why national e-strategies and international initiatives such as the G-8 DOT Force and the WSIS Plan of Action were created.

  28. Discuss the relationship between popular culture and consumerism in contemporary media.
    Topic: Core Subjective Topics

    Popular culture is the culture of the masses, produced and spread largely through the mass media, and in contemporary societies it is closely tied to consumerism. Media conglomerates cross-promote and cross-sell their properties - a film is not only exhibited but sold through magazines, books, television shows, toys and clothing owned by the same company - so cultural products become vehicles for consumption. Media present lifestyles, beauty standards and branded goods as images and values for audiences to copy, turning viewers into consumers and making consumption a marker of identity and success. Critics argue that this commercially driven popular culture displaces local traditions and values, promotes materialism, and reduces meaningful diversity of expression, since the global commercial media system respects no tradition or custom that stands in the way of profits. At the same time, popular culture gives ordinary people shared references and pleasures, and new media allow audiences to produce and remix culture themselves rather than only consuming what conglomerates supply.
