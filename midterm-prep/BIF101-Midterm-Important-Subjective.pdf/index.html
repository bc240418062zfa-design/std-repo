  1. What is the role of bioinformatics in present-day research?
    Topic: Bioinformatics: Concepts, Databases & Issues

    Bioinformatics is now widely used in routine research and analysis, with its most significant applications including Genomics, Transcriptomics, Proteomics, Metabolomics, Structural Proteomics, Drug Design, Systems Biology, and the personalization of medicine for improved treatment strategies. In addition to these applications, bioinformatics has introduced advanced techniques that allow the generation and analysis of vast biological datasets. Over time, the scope of bioinformatics applications has expanded from genomic-level analysis to a more holistic, systems-level approach, enhancing our understanding of complex biological processes and facilitating innovations in various biomedical fields.

  2. What issues are dealt in bioinformatics?
    Topic: Bioinformatics: Concepts, Databases & Issues

    Bioinformatics deals with the following key issues: - Data storage and management of large-scale biological data. - Sequence alignment to compare DNA, RNA, or proteins. - Gene prediction and annotation to identify gene locations and functions. - Protein structure prediction based on amino acid sequences. - Integration of diverse biological data (genomics, proteomics, etc.). - Drug discovery using computational models. - Biological network analysis to study gene and protein interactions. - Development of algorithms to handle big biological data efficiently.

The GenBank database is designed to provide and encourage access within the scientific community to the most up-to-date and comprehensive DNA sequence information.

  8. What is meant by exact and inexact matching?
    Topic: Bioinformatics: Concepts, Databases & Issues

    Exact Matching: Exact matches include complete matching in terms of residues and their order. Inexact Matching: Inexact matching includes partial matching in terms of residues and order, with room for variations in both.

  9. What is sequence alignment and what are its types?
    Topic: Sequence Alignment & Similarity Searching

    The process of inexact matching while keeping in view the conserved residues is called sequence "Alignment". There are two types of sequence alignment: - Pairwise Sequence Alignment - Multiple Sequence Alignment

  10. What is pairwise sequence alignment?
    Topic: Sequence Alignment & Similarity Searching

    Pairwise Sequence Alignment is used to identify regions of similarity that may indicate functional, structural and/or evolutionary relationships between two biological sequences. Pairwise sequence alignment helps compare two sequences. - It takes into consideration inexact matching. - Matches are colored and missing nucleotides are denoted by dot '.'

  11. Describe the types of pairwise alignment (global and local alignment).
    Topic: Sequence Alignment & Similarity Searching

    Global and local pairwise alignments are two types of alignment and they can lead to different results. Local Alignment: Local alignment finds the regions between two sequences that have the strongest similarity, excluding less similar regions. It is used to find similar domains, motifs, and detecting distant homology. Global Alignment: Global alignment maximizes sequence matches over the entire length of two sequences, by introducing gaps. It is used to determine overall similarity and conservation.

  12. What is identity sequence and similarity sequence?
    Topic: Sequence Alignment & Similarity Searching

    Identity: Identity is the number of nucleotides or amino acids which match exactly between two biological sequences. Identity measurement is made on the shorter of the two sequences. Formula: Identity = Number of matches / Smaller Length × 100% Similarity: Sequence similarity is a measure of an empirical relationship between sequences. Similarity is the comparison between sequences calculated by using the alignment approach.

  13. What is multiple sequence alignment (MSA)?
    Topic: Sequence Alignment & Similarity Searching

    Multiple Sequence Alignment (MSA) is generally the alignment of three or more biological sequences (protein or nucleic acid) of similar length. For multiple sequence alignment, we use progressive alignment as dynamic programming becomes expensive.

  14. What is progressive alignment?
    Topic: Sequence Alignment & Similarity Searching

    A progressive alignment method is described that utilizes the Needleman and Wunsch pairwise alignment algorithm iteratively to achieve the multiple alignment of a set of protein sequences and to construct an evolutionary tree depicting their relationship.

  16. What is the BLAST tool?

  22. Describe the structure and function of the nucleus.
    Topic: Cell Biology Basics

    The nucleus contains most of the cell's genetic material (DNA). The duplication of the genetic material and the first steps in decoding genetic information take place in the nucleus. The single nucleus is usually the largest organelle in a cell. The nucleus of most animal cells is approximately 5 μm in diameter - substantially larger than most entire prokaryotic cells. Structure: The nucleus is surrounded by two membranes, which together form the nuclear envelope. The two membranes of the nuclear envelope are separated by 10-20 nm and are perforated by nuclear pores approximately 9 nm in diameter, which connect the interior of the nucleus with the cytoplasm. At these pores, the outer membrane of the nuclear envelope is continuous with the inner membrane. Each pore is surrounded by a pore complex made up of eight large protein granules arranged in an octagon where the inner and outer membranes merge. RNA and proteins pass through these pores to enter or leave the nucleus. Function of nucleus: The nucleus is the site of DNA duplication. The nucleus is the site of genetic control of the cell's activities. A region within the nucleus, the nucleolus, begins the assembly of ribosomes from specific proteins and RNA.

  23. How is the cell a structural and functional unit of life?
    Topic: Cell Biology Basics

    The cell is known as the structural and functional unit of life because it forms the basic building block of all living organisms and carries out essential life processes. Structural Unit of Life: - All living organisms, from unicellular to multicellular, are made up of cells. - Cells provide structure and support; for example, plant cell walls maintain rigidity, while animal cells form tissues, organs, and systems. - The arrangement of cells determines an organism's size, shape, and complexity. - Example: Bone cells provide strength; plant cells maintain rigidity. Functional Unit of Life: - The cell is the site of essential life processes, crucial for survival: Metabolism (biochemical reactions like respiration and protein synthesis); Energy Production (mitochondria produce ATP for activities); Growth and Reproduction (cells divide via mitosis or meiosis); Response to Stimuli (cells adapt to environmental changes). - Example: Red blood cells carry oxygen, pancreatic cells produce insulin, white blood cells fight infections. Thus a cell is both the structural foundation and the functional powerhouse of life. It is the smallest unit capable of independent existence and serves as the cornerstone of all biological systems.

  24. What is the difference between unicellular and multicellular organisms?
    Topic: Cell Biology Basics

    Unicellular Organisms: These organisms consist of only one cell that carries out all necessary life processes such as nutrition, respiration, excretion, and reproduction. They are usually microscopic and have a simple structure. Examples include Amoeba, Paramecium, and Bacteria. Multicellular Organisms: These organisms are made up of many cells, which are specialized to perform different functions. The cells work in coordination, forming tissues, organs, and systems. They are usually larger and more complex. Examples include humans, animals, and plants.

  25. What are the components of chromosomes?
    Topic: Cell Biology Basics

    Chromosomes are made up of the following key components: 1. DNA (Deoxyribonucleic Acid): The genetic material that carries hereditary information in the form of genes. 2. Histone Proteins: Positively charged proteins around which DNA winds to form nucleosomes. They help in DNA packaging and gene regulation. 3. Non-Histone Proteins: Various proteins involved in chromosome structure, gene expression, and enzymatic activities. 4. Chromatin: A complex of DNA and proteins (mostly histones) forming the structural basis of chromosomes. 5. Centromere: A constricted region that holds sister chromatids together and attaches to spindle fibers during cell division. 6. Telomeres: Repetitive nucleotide sequences at the ends of chromosomes that protect them from deterioration or fusion with other chromosomes. 7. Nucleosomes: Structural units of chromatin consisting of DNA wrapped around histone octamers.

  26. What is a nucleic acid?
    Topic: Nucleic Acids: DNA & RNA

    Nucleic acids are an important group of biomolecules which are responsible for storage & transmission of hereditary information. Like proteins and polysaccharides, nucleic acids are also polymeric compounds. The repeating units in the nucleic acids are nucleotides.

  27. Describe DNA and its structure. What are the components of DNA?
    Topic: Nucleic Acids: DNA & RNA

    DNA is a polymer of deoxyribonucleotides and is composed of three components: deoxyribose, nitrogenous base, and phosphoric acid. It carries the genetic instructions used in the growth, development, functioning, and reproduction of all known living organisms. DNA has three parts: - A phosphate group - A sugar group (deoxyribose) - One of four types of nitrogenous bases DNA is also composed of chemical building blocks called nucleotides. In a DNA strand, the nucleotides are linked into a chain. There are four bases in DNA: Adenine, Guanine, Cytosine, Thymine. These bases form pairs: Adenine (A) pairs with Thymine (T); Guanine (G) pairs with Cytosine (C). The chemical structure of DNA was first discovered in 1869, but its role in genetic inheritance was not demonstrated until 1943. In 1953, James Watson and Francis Crick determined that the structure of DNA is a double-helix polymer, a spiral consisting of two DNA strands wound around each other. Each strand is composed of a long chain of monomer nucleotides. The nucleotide of DNA consists of a deoxyribose sugar molecule, which is attached to a phosphate group and one of four nitrogenous bases: two purines (adenine and guanine) and two pyrimidines (cytosine and thymine). The nucleotides are joined together by covalent bonds between the phosphate of one nucleotide and the sugar of the next, forming a phosphate-sugar backbone, from which the nitrogenous bases protrude. One strand is held to another by hydrogen bonds between the bases, with the bonding sequence being specific - i.e., adenine bonds only with thymine, and cytosine only with guanine.

  28. What are the types of deoxyribonucleotides?
    Topic: Nucleic Acids: DNA & RNA

    There are four types of deoxyribonucleotides: - Deoxyadenosine - Deoxythymidine - Deoxyguanosine - Deoxycytidine These four deoxyribonucleotides make the structural units of DNA.

  29. What are nucleotides?
    Topic: Nucleic Acids: DNA & RNA

    A nucleotide is the basic building block of nucleic acids. Both RNA and DNA are polymers made up of long chains of nucleotides. Each nucleotide consists of three components: 1. A sugar molecule (which is ribose in RNA and deoxyribose in DNA) 2. A phosphate group 3. A nitrogen-containing base These three components together form the fundamental unit that makes up nucleic acids.

  30. Differentiate between nucleotide and nucleoside.
    Topic: Nucleic Acids: DNA & RNA

    Nucleotide: A molecule containing all three components is called a nucleotide. A nucleotide consists of a sugar molecule (either ribose in RNA or deoxyribose in DNA) attached to a phosphate group and a nitrogen-containing base. The bases used in DNA are adenine (A), cytosine (C), guanine (G), and thymine (T). Nucleoside: A molecule without the phosphate group is called a nucleoside. A nucleoside is composed of a nucleobase - a pyrimidine (cytosine, thymine or uracil) or a purine (adenine or guanine) - and a five-carbon sugar, which is either ribose or deoxyribose.

  31. Describe the types of RNA.
    Topic: Nucleic Acids: DNA & RNA

    There are mainly three types of ribonucleic acids (RNAs) present in the cells of living organisms. - Messenger RNA (mRNA): It is the type of RNA that carries genetic information from DNA to the protein biosynthetic machinery of the ribosome. It provides the templates that specify amino acid sequences in polypeptide chains. - Transfer RNA (tRNA): Transfer RNAs serve as adapter molecules in the process of protein synthesis. They are covalently linked to an amino acid at one end. - Ribosomal RNA (rRNA): Ribosomal RNAs are components of ribosomes. rRNA is a predominant material in the ribosomes constituting about 60% of its weight. It has a number of functions to perform in the ribosomes.

  32. Describe the structure of RNA.
    Topic: Nucleic Acids: DNA & RNA

    RNA consists of ribose nucleotides (nitrogenous bases appended to a ribose sugar) attached by phosphodiester bonds, forming strands of varying lengths. The nitrogenous bases in RNA are adenine, guanine, cytosine, and uracil, which replace thymine in DNA.

  33. Define genetics. How does genetics differ from molecular biology?
    Topic: Genetics & Genomics

    Genetics is a branch of biology concerned with the study of genes, genetic variation, and heredity in organisms. Genes are made up of DNA. Some genes act as instructions to make molecules called proteins. However, many genes do not code for proteins. Every person has two copies of each gene, one inherited from each parent. Genetics vs Molecular Biology: Genetics is the study of genes, heredity, and variation in organisms; it focuses on the principles of heredity and how traits are passed from one generation to the next, and deals with understanding the function and structure of genes, chromosomes, and the transmission of genetic information. Key figure: Mendel, who established the basic laws of inheritance. Molecular Biology is the study of biological molecules, especially those related to genes and their products (like proteins and RNA). It focuses on the molecular mechanisms that regulate gene expression, replication, and repair. Molecular Biology often overlaps with genetics, but it delves deeper into the biochemical processes within cells. This field has significantly impacted health sciences and agricultural sciences, particularly through innovations like genetic engineering and biotechnology. In short: Genetics is primarily about inheritance patterns and the function of genes; Molecular Biology is more focused on the molecular mechanisms behind gene function and expression.

  34. What are the sub-disciplines of genetics?
    Topic: Genetics & Genomics

    There are four sub-disciplines of genetics: 1. Transmission (Classical) Genetics - Deals with movement of genes and genetic traits from parents to offspring; deals with genetic recombination. 2. Population Genetics - Studies heredity in groups for traits determined by one or a few genes. 3. Quantitative Genetics - Studies group heredity for traits determined by many genes simultaneously. Examples: skin color, height and eye color. 4. Molecular Genetics - Deals with the molecular structure and function of genes. Historically, transmission genetics developed first, followed by population genetics, quantitative genetics, and finally molecular genetics.

  35. What are traits, characters and true breed?
    Topic: Genetics & Genomics

    A heritable feature (like skin color, height etc.) that is passed from one generation to another is called a character. These are observable attributes in an organism that are determined by specific genes. Examples of characters include flower color in plants, eye color in humans, or plant height in peas. Each character can have multiple forms, known as traits. A trait is a variant form of a character (i.e. brown, black, white etc.). It represents the specific expression of a character in an individual. For example, if the character is eye color, then blue, brown, or green are its traits. Traits result from different alleles (gene variants) and contribute to the diversity seen within a population. True-breed means that all offspring are of the same variety when an organism is self-crossed or crossed within the same line. This occurs because the organism is homozygous for the specific trait, and therefore it produces offspring with identical traits every time. True-breeding lines are important in genetics to study inheritance patterns.

  36. Differentiate between genotype and phenotype.
    Topic: Genetics & Genomics

    Genotype: The genetic make-up of an organism is called genotype. For an individual's gene makeup there is a tall variety (T) and there is a short variety (s); T and s are called the alleles. Freckles or no freckles is another example. The information that is passed from parent to child is carried in the cell of the genotype. Phenotype: The physical appearance of an organism is called phenotype. Examples of phenotypes include height, wing length, and hair color. Phenotypes also include observable characteristics that can be measured in the laboratory, such as levels of hormones or blood cells.

  37. Differentiate between dominant and recessive trait.
    Topic: Genetics & Genomics

    Dominant Trait: When one characteristic expresses itself over the other, i.e. round over wrinkled. For example, having a widow's peak (a V-shaped hairline near the forehead) is dominant. Recessive Trait: The trait that does not show through in the first generation, i.e. wrinkled. For example, having a straight hairline is recessive.

  38. What is meant by genomics?
    Topic: Genetics & Genomics

    Genomics is the study of all of a person's genes (the genome), including interactions of those genes with each other and with the person's environment.

The first prokaryotic genome sequenced was that of Haemophilus influenzae. It paved the way for sequencing of many other organisms.

  40. Describe the eukaryotic genome.
    Topic: Genetics & Genomics

    Eukaryotes have larger genomes. Eukaryotic genomes are composed of one or more linear DNA chromosomes. The number of chromosomes varies widely, from the jack jumper ant and an asexual nematode, which each have only one pair, to a fern species that has 720 pairs. It is surprising the amount of DNA that eukaryotic genomes contain compared to other genomes.

  41. What is the central dogma?
    Topic: Central Dogma, Transcription & Translation

    The central dogma states that information in nucleic acid can be perpetuated or transferred, but the transfer of information into protein is irreversible. DNA -> RNA -> Protein Synthesis - Transcription: Process of DNA serving as a template for RNA synthesis. - Translation: Process of RNA serving as a template for protein synthesis. The central dogma outlines the flow of genetic information during growth and division of the cells. Genetic information flows from DNA to RNA to protein during cell growth. In addition, all living cells must replicate their DNA when they divide; during cell division each daughter cell receives a copy of the genome of the parent cell. Replication is the process by which two identical copies of DNA are made from an original molecule of DNA, so replication occurs in the cells prior to cell division. An important point is that information does not flow from protein to RNA or DNA. However, flow of information from RNA "backwards" to DNA is possible in certain special circumstances due to the operation of reverse transcriptase. By the end of 1953, the working hypothesis was adopted that chromosomal DNA functions as the template for the synthesis of RNA molecules. These RNA molecules subsequently move to the cytoplasm, where they determine the arrangement of amino acids within proteins. In 1956, Francis Crick referred to this pathway for the flow of genetic information as the Central Dogma.

  42. Differentiate between transcription and translation.
    Topic: Central Dogma, Transcription & Translation

    Transcription: Transcription is the process by which the information in a strand of DNA is copied into a new molecule of messenger RNA (mRNA). The newly formed mRNA copies of the gene then serve as blueprints for protein synthesis during the process of translation. Translation: Translation is the process by which the genetic code contained within a messenger RNA (mRNA) molecule is decoded to produce a specific sequence of amino acids in a polypeptide chain. The key components required for translation are mRNA, ribosomes, and transfer RNA (tRNA).

  43. Describe the process of translation in protein synthesis.
    Topic: Central Dogma, Transcription & Translation

    Translation is the second part of the central dogma of molecular biology: RNA -> Protein. It is the process in which the genetic code in mRNA is read, one codon at a time, to make a protein. After mRNA leaves the nucleus, it moves to a ribosome, which consists of rRNA and proteins. The ribosome reads the sequence of codons in mRNA. Molecules of tRNA bring amino acids to the ribosome in the correct sequence. Translation of the codons in mRNA to a chain of amino acids occurs at a ribosome. To understand the role of tRNA, you need to know more about its structure. Each tRNA molecule has an anticodon for the amino acid it carries. An anticodon is a sequence of 3 bases and is complementary to the codon for an amino acid. For example, the amino acid lysine has the codon AAG, so the anticodon is UUC. Therefore, lysine would be carried by a tRNA molecule with the anticodon UUC. Wherever the codon AAG appears in mRNA, a UUC anticodon on a tRNA temporarily binds to the codon. While bound to the mRNA, the tRNA gives up its amino acid. Bonds form between adjacent amino acids as they are brought one by one to the ribosome, forming a polypeptide chain. The chain of amino acids keeps growing until a stop codon is reached. The tRNA structure is a very important aspect in its role. Though the molecule folds into a 3-leaf clover structure, notice the anticodon arm in the lower segment of the molecule, with the amino acid attached at the opposite end of the molecule (acceptor stem). It is the anticodon that determines which codon in the mRNA the tRNA will bind to. After a polypeptide chain is synthesized, it may undergo additional processes. For example, it may assume a folded shape due to interactions among its amino acids. It may also bind with other polypeptides or with different types of molecules, such as lipids or carbohydrates. Many proteins travel to the Golgi apparatus to be modified for the specific job they will do.

  44. What are the steps of protein synthesis?
    Topic: Central Dogma, Transcription & Translation

    The steps of protein synthesis are: 1. Transcription: Occurs in the nucleus. DNA is used as a template to produce mRNA (messenger RNA). mRNA carries the genetic code from the DNA to the ribosome. 2. Translation: Occurs in the cytoplasm at the ribosome. mRNA is read in sets of three nucleotides (codons), each specifying an amino acid. tRNA (transfer RNA) brings the appropriate amino acids to the ribosome based on the mRNA sequence. Amino acids are linked together to form a polypeptide chain. 3. Protein Folding: The newly formed polypeptide chain folds into its specific 3D shape to become a functional protein. This process allows cells to produce the proteins needed for various functions in the body.

  45. What is the role of ribosomal RNA (rRNA) in the translation of protein?
    Topic: Central Dogma, Transcription & Translation

    Ribosomal RNA (rRNA) is a type of stable RNA that is a major constituent of ribosomes. It ensures the proper alignment of the mRNA and the ribosomes during protein synthesis and catalyzes the formation of the peptide bonds between two aligned amino acids during protein synthesis. Within the ribosome, the rRNA molecules direct the catalytic steps of protein synthesis - the stitching together of amino acids to make a protein molecule. In fact, rRNA is sometimes called a ribozyme or catalytic RNA to reflect this function. During translation of mRNA, rRNA functions to bind both mRNA and tRNA to facilitate the process of translating mRNA's codon sequence into amino acids. rRNA initiates the catalysis of protein synthesis when tRNA is sandwiched between the SSU and LSU. In the SSU, the mRNA interacts with the anticodons of the tRNA.

  46. What is translocation in translation?
    Topic: Central Dogma, Transcription & Translation

    Once the peptidyl transferase reaction has occurred, the tRNA in the P-site is deacylated (no longer attached to an amino acid), and the growing polypeptide chain is linked to the tRNA in the A-site. For a new round of peptide chain elongation to occur, the P-site tRNA must move to the E site and the A-site tRNA must move to the P-site. At the same time, the mRNA must move by three nucleotides to expose the next codon. These movements are coordinated within the ribosome and are collectively referred to as translocation. The initial steps of translocation are coupled to the peptidyl transferase reaction. Once the growing peptide chain has been transferred to the A-site tRNA, the A- and P-site tRNAs have a preference to occupy new positions in the large subunit. The 3' end of the A-site tRNA is bound to the growing polypeptide chain and prefers to bind in the P-site of the large subunit. The now deacylated P-site tRNA is no longer attached to the growing polypeptide chain and prefers to bind in the E-site of the large subunit. In contrast, at this time, the anticodons of these tRNAs remain in their initial location in the small subunit bound to the mRNA. Translocation is initiated in the large subunit before the small subunit, and the tRNAs are said to be in "hybrid states." The completion of translocation requires the action of a second elongation factor called EF-G. After the peptidyl transferase reaction, EF-G-GTP binds to and stabilizes the ribosome in the rotated, hybrid state.

  47. What are ribosomes? What is the role/function of ribosomes in a cell?
    Topic: Ribosome & Translation Machinery

    The ribosome is the macromolecular machine that directs the synthesis of proteins. The ribosome is larger and more complex than the minimal machinery required for DNA or RNA synthesis. The machinery for polymerizing amino acids is composed of at least three RNA molecules and more than 50 different proteins, with an overall molecular mass of >2.5 MDa. Compared with the speed of DNA replication i.e., 200-1000 nucleotides per second, translation takes place at a rate of only two to 20 amino acids per second. In prokaryotes, the transcription machinery and the translation machinery are located in the same compartment. Thus, the ribosome can commence translation of the mRNA as it emerges from the RNA polymerase. This situation allows the ribosome to proceed in tandem with the RNA polymerase as it elongates the transcript. Recall that the 5' end of an RNA is synthesized first, and thus the ribosome, which begins translation at the 5' end of the mRNA, can start translating a nascent transcript as soon as it emerges from the RNA polymerase.

  48. What are the binding sites on ribosomes? What is the role of binding sites in translation/protein synthesis?
    Topic: Ribosome & Translation Machinery

    Ribosomes are composed of two subunits, one small and one large. Four binding sites are located on the ribosome, one for mRNA and three for tRNA. The three tRNA sites are labeled P, A, and E. - The A-site (aminoacyl/acceptor site) is the binding site for the aminoacylated-tRNA, which carries the new amino acid to be added to the polypeptide chain. - The P-site (peptidyl site) is the binding site for the peptidyl-tRNA holding the growing polypeptide chain of amino acids. - The E-site (exit site) is the binding site for the tRNA that is released after the growing polypeptide chain has been transferred to the aminoacyl-tRNA; it serves as the final transitory step before a tRNA, now bereft of its amino acid, is released by the ribosome. Each tRNA binding site is formed at the interface between the large and the small subunits of the ribosome. A ribosome binding site (RBS) is a sequence of nucleotides upstream of the start codon of an mRNA transcript, responsible for the recruitment of the ribosome during the initiation of translation.

  49. What is meant by initiation factor?
    Topic: Ribosome & Translation Machinery

    In prokaryotes, translation initiation is controlled by three initiation factors: IF1, IF2, and IF3. Both IF1 and IF2 are involved in positioning the initiator tRNA in the partial P site of the 30S subunit, while the GTPase activity of IF2 signals the beginning of translation elongation.

  50. What are the classes of release factors? What is the difference between prokaryotic and eukaryotic release factors?
    Topic: Ribosome & Translation Machinery

    Stop codons are recognized by proteins called release factors (RFs) that activate the hydrolysis of the polypeptide from the peptidyl-tRNA. There are two classes of release factors: - Class I release factors recognize the stop codons and trigger hydrolysis of the peptide chain from the tRNA in the P-site. - Class II release factors stimulate the dissociation of the class I factors from the ribosome after release of the polypeptide chain. Prokaryotes have two class I release factors called RF1 and RF2. RF1 recognizes the stop codon UAG and RF2 recognizes the stop codon UGA. The third stop codon, UAA, is recognized by both RF1 and RF2. In eukaryotic cells, there is a single class I release factor called eRF1 that recognizes all three stop codons. Prokaryotes and eukaryotes have only one class II factor called RF3 and eRF3, respectively. Like EF-G, IF2, and EF-Tu, class II release factors are regulated by GTP binding and hydrolysis. Release factors are composed entirely of protein, so protein-RNA interaction must mediate stop codon recognition.

  51. What is the genetic code? Mention its features and examples.
    Topic: Genetic Code

    The genetic code is the set of instructions in a gene that tell the cell how to make a specific protein. A, T, G, and C are the "letters" of the DNA code. They stand for the chemicals adenine, thymine, guanine, and cytosine, respectively, that make up the nucleotide bases of DNA. The letters are combined in groups of three to form code "words," called codons. Each codon stands for (encodes) one amino acid, unless it codes for a start or stop signal. There are 20 common amino acids in proteins. There are 64 possible codons, more than enough to code for the 20 amino acids. Example: The sequence AUG is a codon that specifies the amino acid methionine. There are 64 possible codons, three of which do not code for amino acids but indicate the end of a protein. The remaining 61 codons specify the 20 amino acids that make up proteins. Features: 1. The codon is a triplet. 2. There are 64 codons of which only 61 are coding and 3 act as stop codons and do not code for any amino acid. 3. One codon codes for only 1 amino acid. Thus, it is unambiguous and specific. 4. A single amino acid may be coded by more than one codon. Thus, it is degenerate. 5. A codon is universal i.e., from bacteria to human beings, UUU codes for phenylalanine. However, exceptions have been found in some mitochondria and protozoans. 6. A codon in mRNA is read in a contiguous fashion i.e., without any punctuation. 7. The codon AUG has dual functions. It codes for Methionine (Met) as well as acts as the initiator codon.

  52. Define codon and anticodon.
    Topic: Genetic Code

    Codon: The codon is unambiguous and specific, meaning one codon can code for only one amino acid. Codons are tri-nucleotide units that are present in mRNA and code for a particular amino acid in protein synthesis. Anticodon: An anticodon is a tri-nucleotide unit present in tRNA. It is complementary to the codons in mRNA.

  53. What are codons? Explain with example.
    Topic: Genetic Code

    The genetic code specifies which amino acids will be used to build a protein. The genetic information in an mRNA molecule is a series of sequential, non-overlapping three-letter "words." Each sequence of three nucleotide bases (the three "letters") along the chain specifies a particular amino acid. Each three-letter "word" is called a codon. Each codon is complementary to the corresponding triplet in the DNA molecule from which it was transcribed. Thus, the genetic code is the means of relating codons to their specific amino acids. Combinations of the four available "letters" (the bases) give 64 (4³) different three-letter codons, yet these codons determine only 20 amino acids. AUG, which codes for methionine, is also the start codon, the initiation signal for translation. Three of the codons (UAA, UAG, UGA) are stop codons, or termination signals for translation; when the translation machinery reaches one of these codons, translation stops, and the polypeptide is released from the translation complex.

  54. What is hydrolysis? Explain with examples.
    Topic: Proteins & Amino Acids

    Hydrolysis is the opposite of condensation. A large molecule is split into smaller sections by breaking a bond, adding -H to one section and -OH to the other. The products are simpler substances. Since it involves the addition of water, this explains why it is called hydrolysis, meaning splitting by water. A-B + H2O -> A-H + B-OH Example of Hydrolysis: Sucrose + Water -> Glucose + Fructose. This is a hydrolysis reaction where the disaccharide sucrose breaks down into two monosaccharides (glucose and fructose) with the addition of water and the help of the enzyme sucrase.

  55. What are amino acids and describe their structure? Does every amino acid have a central carbon atom?
    Topic: Proteins & Amino Acids

    Amino acids are organic compounds that serve as the building blocks of proteins. They are essential for various biological functions, including enzyme activity, cell structure, and metabolic processes. In nature, there are many amino acids, but only 20 standard amino acids are commonly found in proteins. These amino acids are encoded by the genetic code and play a crucial role in forming the structure and function of proteins in all living organisms. Structure of Amino Acids: Each amino acid has a basic structure consisting of a central alpha (alpha) carbon atom bonded to four different groups: an amino group (-NH2), a carboxyl group (-COOH), a hydrogen atom (-H), and a variable side chain called the R group. The R group differs in each amino acid and determines its unique chemical properties, such as being polar, nonpolar, acidic, or basic. The general formula of an amino acid is R-CH(NH2)-COOH. Amino acids join together through peptide bonds to form polypeptide chains, which then fold into functional proteins according to their sequence. Yes, every amino acid has a central carbon atom known as the alpha-carbon. Amino acids are also referred to as alpha-amino carboxylic acids, where "alpha" refers to the position of the carbon atom (the central carbon) in relation to the amino and carboxyl groups. The structure of the carboxyl group is -COOH (where C is double-bonded to O and also bonded to -OH).

  56. How are amino acids linked? How are peptide bonds formed?
    Topic: Proteins & Amino Acids

    Amino acids are linked by peptide bonds through a process called dehydration synthesis: 1. The carboxyl group (-COOH) of one amino acid reacts with the amino group (-NH2) of another amino acid. 2. During this reaction, a water molecule (H2O) is released. 3. This forms a covalent bond between the carbon of the carboxyl group and the nitrogen of the amino group, creating a peptide bond. 4. As a result, amino acids are linked together to form a polypeptide chain. Peptide bonds are formed by a biochemical reaction that extracts a water molecule as it joins the amino group of one amino acid to the carboxyl group of a neighboring amino acid. The linear sequence of amino acids within a protein is considered the primary structure of the protein. This process continues during protein synthesis to form proteins, with the chain eventually folding into a functional 3D structure.

  57. Define proteins and describe their functions.
    Topic: Proteins & Amino Acids

    Proteins are large biomolecules and macromolecules that comprise one or more long chains of amino acid residues. Functions: Protein has many roles in your body. It helps repair and build your body's tissues, allows metabolic reactions to take place and coordinates bodily functions. In addition to providing your body with a structural framework, proteins also maintain proper pH and fluid balance.

  58. Write a short note on the structure of proteins.
    Topic: Proteins & Amino Acids

    Within a protein, multiple amino acids are linked together by peptide bonds, thereby forming a long chain. - Primary Structure: Sequence of amino acids bonded by peptide linkages. - Secondary Structure: alpha helices (hydrogen bonds present between amino acid residues), beta pleated sheets (hydrogen bonds present between amino acid residues). - Tertiary Structure: Generated by bending and folding of the polypeptide chain. - Quaternary Structure: Arrangement of multiple folded protein molecules in a multi-subunit complex.

  59. What is the chemical composition of proteins?
    Topic: Proteins & Amino Acids

    All the proteins are made up of twenty different types of amino acids. So these amino acids are called standard amino acids. In a protein molecule, each amino acid residue is joined to its neighbor by a specific type of covalent bond which is called a peptide bond. Amino acids can successively join to form dipeptides, tripeptides, tetrapeptides, oligopeptides and polypeptides.

  60. What are the different forms of symmetry in the quaternary structure of protein?
    Topic: Proteins & Amino Acids

    Some proteins contain two or more separate polypeptide chains or subunits, which may be identical or different. The spatial arrangement of these subunits is known as a protein's quaternary structure. A multi-subunit protein is also referred to as a multimer. A multimer with just a few subunits is called an oligomer, and a single subunit or a group of subunits is called a protomer. Identical subunits of multimeric proteins are generally arranged in symmetric patterns. Oligomers can have either rotational symmetry or helical symmetry. There are several forms of rotational symmetry: - The simplest is cyclic symmetry, involving rotation about a single axis. - A somewhat more complicated rotational symmetry is dihedral symmetry, in which a twofold rotational axis is present. - More complex rotational symmetries include icosahedral symmetry. An icosahedron is a regular 12-cornered polyhedron having 20 triangular faces. The other major type of symmetry found in oligomers is helical symmetry.

  61. What type of interactions are found in proteins?
    Topic: Proteins & Amino Acids

    Proteins have several key types of interactions: 1. Hydrogen Bonds: Stabilize secondary structures like alpha-helices and beta-sheets. 2. Ionic Bonds: Form between charged side chains, stabilizing the tertiary structure. 3. Van der Waals Forces: Weak interactions that help maintain protein stability. 4. Hydrophobic Interactions: Nonpolar side chains cluster inside the protein, aiding folding. 5. Disulfide Bonds: Strong covalent bonds between cysteine residues, maintaining tertiary and quaternary structures. 6. Peptide Bonds: Link amino acids together, forming the primary structure of the protein. These interactions work together to give proteins their structure and function.

    Since the 1970s, scientists have learned to isolate genes, then place them in new organisms and reproduce them by a set of techniques collectively known as gene cloning. Technique of gene cloning - Genetically Modified Organisms (GMOs): The technique of gene cloning led to the creation of a large number of genetically modified organisms with desirable characters.

  63. What is the Human Genome Project?
    Topic: Molecular Techniques & Genome Projects

    The Human Genome Project (HGP) was launched in 1990 and completed in 2003. The Human Genome Project (HGP) was an international scientific research project with the goal of determining the base pairs that make up human DNA, and of identifying, mapping and sequencing all of the genes of the human genome from both a physical and a functional standpoint.

  64. Compare DNA sequencing, RNA sequencing and protein sequencing.
    Topic: Molecular Techniques & Genome Projects

    DNA Sequencing: DNA sequencing is a laboratory technique used to determine the exact sequence of bases (A, C, G, and T) in a DNA molecule. The DNA base sequence carries the information a cell needs to assemble protein and RNA molecules. DNA sequence information is important to scientists investigating the functions of genes. RNA Sequencing: RNA-seq (RNA-sequencing) is a technique that can examine the quantity and sequences of RNA in a sample using next-generation sequencing (NGS). It analyzes the transcriptome, indicating which of the genes encoded in our DNA are turned on or off and to what extent. Protein Sequencing: Protein sequencing is the practical process of determining the amino acid sequence of all or part of a protein or peptide. This may serve to identify the protein or characterize its post-translational modifications.

  65. Define bioinformatics. What are its main goals?
    Topic: Created • 3 marks marks

    Bioinformatics is an interdisciplinary field that combines biology, computer science, mathematics and statistics to store, retrieve, organize and analyze large amounts of biological data, especially nucleotide and protein sequences. Its main goals are: - To store biological data in databases in an organized and accessible form. - To develop tools and algorithms for comparing sequences and finding similarities and relationships among them. - To predict gene locations, gene functions and protein structures from sequence data. - To understand evolutionary relationships and to support research in genomics, proteomics, drug design and personalized medicine.

  67. Differentiate between primary and secondary (derived) databases. Give examples.
    Topic: Created • 3 marks marks

    Primary (archival) databases contain raw experimental sequence data submitted directly by researchers, with minimal processing. Examples: GenBank (USA), EMBL (European Molecular Biology Laboratory nucleotide database, Europe) and DDBJ (DNA Data Bank of Japan, established in 1984). These three exchange data with each other daily. Secondary (derived) databases are built by processing and annotating the data of primary databases; they add value such as curated protein sequences, motifs and patterns. Examples: SWISS-PROT (curated protein sequences), TrEMBL and PROSITE (protein families and patterns).

  68. What are the problems of flat-file databases? Why is tabular (relational) storage better?
    Topic: Created • 3 marks marks

    In a flat-file database, all data is stored in a single text file format. Its main trouble is that it works only for small data sets: as the data grows, searching becomes slow, the same data is repeated at many places (redundancy), updating is difficult and data integrity and security cannot be maintained properly. Tabular (relational) storage is better because data is organized in related tables of rows and columns, which removes duplication, makes searching and updating fast and accurate, and maintains data integrity, security and accessibility.

  69. What are scoring matrices? What are they used for?
    Topic: Created • 2 marks marks

    Scoring matrices are tables used during sequence alignment to assign a score to every possible pair of aligned residues: a positive score for a match or a conservative substitution, and a penalty for a mismatch or a gap. Common examples are the PAM and BLOSUM matrices for protein sequences. They are used to score and rank alignments, to measure similarity between sequences, and to predict evolutionary relationships between them - the alignment with the highest total score is taken as the best alignment.

  70. What is a guide tree in multiple sequence alignment? How is it constructed?
    Topic: Created • 3 marks marks

    A guide tree is a tree diagram that represents the approximate evolutionary relationships (similarities) among all the sequences in a multiple sequence alignment. In progressive alignment, the guide tree decides the order in which sequences are added to the growing alignment: the most similar sequences are aligned first, and less similar ones are added later. It is constructed by first performing all pairwise alignments, calculating a distance matrix from their scores, and then clustering the sequences on the basis of that distance matrix (as done by CLUSTAL).

  71. What is the role of the promoter region in the regulation of gene expression?
    Topic: Created • 2 marks marks

    The promoter is a specific region of DNA located just before (upstream of) a gene. It is the binding site where RNA polymerase and transcription factors attach to the DNA to start transcription of the gene. By controlling the binding of transcription factors - activators that increase and repressors (negative regulators) that decrease binding - the promoter region regulates when, where and how strongly a gene is expressed.
