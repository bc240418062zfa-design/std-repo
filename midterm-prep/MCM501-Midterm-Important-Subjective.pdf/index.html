  1. Define advertising and discuss its main characteristics.
    Topic: Core Subjective Topics

    Advertising is a paid form of non-personal communication about an organization, product, service or idea by an identified sponsor, using mass media to persuade or influence an audience. According to William H. Bolen, it is any controlled form of non-personal presentation and promotion of ideas, goods and services by an identified sponsor, used to inform and persuade the selected market. Its main characteristics are: (1) Paid form — the space or time for an advertising message must be bought, except in the case of public service announcements. (2) Non-personal — the message is conveyed through mass media such as TV, radio, magazines and newspapers to large groups of people, with generally no opportunity for immediate feedback. (3) Controlled — the content, time and direction of the message are controlled by the advertiser, which distinguishes advertising from personal selling and publicity. (4) Identifiable sponsor — the sponsor is openly identified, which distinguishes advertising from propaganda. (5) It deals with ideas, goods and services, and its purpose is information and persuasion.

  2. What are the objectives of advertising? Discuss.
    Topic: Core Subjective Topics

    The objectives of advertising include: (1) Building awareness of products and brands — supporting new products by generating awareness so that people think of the brand first when they are ready to purchase. (2) Providing product and brand information — telling consumers what is available, its special features, colour and size, and where to buy it. (3) Communicating product information — informing the public whenever changes are made in prices, channels of distribution, quality, size, weight, brand or packing. (4) Telling and showing uses to the consumers — informative advertising introduces a new company, product or service and explains how the product works and how it helps the customers. (5) Urging product use — inducing consumers to try new products and suggesting reuse and new uses through constant reminders and reinforcement. (6) Expanding product distribution — consumer demand created by advertising pushes retailers and distributors to stock the product everywhere. (7) Increasing brand preference and brand loyalty — satisfied customers develop a long-term preference for the brand, which advertising encourages. (8) Reinforcing past purchases and brand experiences to maintain the current customer base. In short, advertising aims to inform, remind and persuade consumers.

  3. Trace the historical development of advertising.
    Topic: Core Subjective Topics

    The idea of advertising emerged when people started to trade. Its development passed through several stages: (1) Signs — traders like the Phoenicians painted commercial messages on rocks along the trade lanes; excavations at Pompeii reveal inscriptions on shop walls, and gladiator contest announcements on papyrus or walls are regarded as the first examples of advertising. (2) Town criers — in the Middle Ages, when the general populace could not read, tradesmen used picture signs, and town criers were paid to circulate through the streets to inform people about products and services; the Greeks used them to announce the arrival of ships with cargoes. (3) Print — the invention of the press is the base of modern advertising; the first printed advertisement was a handbill written by William Caxton in 1477, the first continuously published newspaper in America was the Boston News Letter (1704), and the first advertising agency was founded by Volney B. Palmer in 1840. Thomas J. Barratt of Pears Soap fame is called the father of modern advertising. (4) Radio — the invention of radio played a great role; the first radio station was founded in 1920 in the United States and WEAF of New York aired the first paid radio commercial in 1922. (5) Television — the first audio-visual medium; the first public TV demonstration in the USA occurred in New York in 1939 and the first TV advertisement was broadcast in 1941. (6) Online advertising — the Internet brought a new dimension; HotWired, the pioneer of online advertising, sold the first web banner to AT&T on October 27, 1994.

  4. Write a note on the development of advertising in Pakistan.
    Topic: Core Subjective Topics

    Advertising in Pakistan has generally followed the trends and innovations adopted globally and can be categorized into distinct eras. In 1947, soon after independence, there were very few mediums of advertising, mostly newspapers, magazines and digests; most agencies had their head offices in India, and after independence a few independent agencies began experimenting with the available media. The oldest newspapers were Daily Jang (1939), Nawa-i-Waqt (1940) and Dawn (1941), and advertisements were mainly black and white, based on product features and a tag line. The All Pakistan Newspapers Society (APNS) was founded in 1953 to protect the rights of newspapers. Radio Pakistan, under the Pakistan Broadcasting Corporation, became a popular medium of mass advertising, and cinema advertising began in the early 1960s, the golden era of the Pakistan cinema industry. The arrival of television changed the trends: PTV started broadcasting in November 1964, celebrity endorsements became common, and the Pakistan Advertising Association (PAA) was established in 1973. With the introduction of colour TV in 1978–1988, competition for time slots grew, and international agencies entered the market through joint ventures and partnerships. Under the influence of multinationals, foreign agencies established offices in Pakistan and joined hands with local institutions, making the industry more professional and creative.

  5. Differentiate between advertising and publicity.
    Topic: Core Subjective Topics

    Advertising and publicity differ in the following ways: (1) Payment — advertising is a paid form of presenting ideas, goods and services through media such as radio, television, newspapers, magazines and the Internet, while publicity is usually an unpaid promotion, although a minimal cost is incurred for the materials used. (2) Source — advertising comes from an identified sponsor, while publicity comes from a neutral and impartial source. (3) Control — advertising is controllable by the organization, which can specify the size, scope and content of the advertisement; publicity is not controllable, as the individual or company has no control over how the material is presented, if it is released at all. (4) Credibility — advertising is less credible, while publicity is more credible because it comes from an impartial source. (5) Message — advertising is what you say and promote about your organization, while publicity is what others say for you or your organization. (6) Repetition — in advertising the same content is repeated by the sponsor, while in publicity this is not generally possible. (7) Nature — advertising always carries a positive message because it is paid content, but publicity can be positive or negative. (8) Social responsibility — most of the time in advertising social responsibility is ignored, while in publicity special focus is given on social responsibility.

  6. How does advertising differ from public relations? Discuss.
    Topic: Core Subjective Topics

    Public relations is the practice of managing the flow of information between an individual or an organization and the public, gaining exposure through topics of public interest and news items that do not require direct payment. The difference between advertising and PR is summed up in the saying: 'Advertising you pay for, PR you pray for.' The main differences are: (1) Paid space or free coverage — in advertising the company pays for ad space and knows exactly when the ad will air or be published; in PR the job is to get free publicity through news conferences and press releases. (2) Creative control vs. no control — since you pay for the space, you have creative control over what goes into an ad; in PR you have no control over how the media presents your information, if they decide to use it at all. (3) Shelf life — an ad can be run over and over as long as the budget allows, so it has a longer shelf life; PR exposure from a press release is circulated only once. (4) Wise consumers — consumers know an advertisement is trying to sell them something and view the message guardedly, whereas third-party coverage creates credibility because it is not paid for. (5) Creativity or a nose for news — advertising exercises creativity in creating campaigns; PR requires a nose for news and the ability to generate buzz. (6) Target audience or hooked editor — advertising is directed at the target audience, while PR must hook editors to use the information.

  7. Differentiate between advertising and sales promotion.
    Topic: Core Subjective Topics

    Advertising positions a product or service against that of competitors to convey a brand message to consumers and to enhance its value in the consumer's eyes; for example, a television commercial for a new automobile emphasizing the car's new features and styling. Sales promotions, on the other hand, include a variety of strategies designed to offer purchasers an extra incentive to buy, usually in the short term — examples are cents-off coupons, two-for-the-price-of-one sales and double coupons at the grocery store, all for a limited period of time. The key difference between advertising and sales promotion is the nature of the appeal to the consumer. Advertising is emotional in nature and its objective is to create an enduring brand image — perfumes, makeup and jewellery need imaginative advertising to create the allure needed to sell these products. Sales promotion, by contrast, relies on a short-term extra incentive that pushes the purchaser to buy now rather than building a long-term image of the brand.

  8. Discuss the major controversies and criticisms about advertising.
    Topic: Core Subjective Topics

    Advertising faces several controversies and criticisms: (1) Deception in advertising — the most common short-term argument is that advertising is frequently deceptive; advertising is by its very nature not complete information and is biased in favour of the advertiser, and when advertisers cross the line between giving their point of view and creating false expectations, people begin to object. (2) Effect on our value system — critics argue that advertising degrades people's value systems by promoting a hedonistic, materialistic way of life, encouraging us to buy more things we do not need. (3) Proliferation of advertising — there is just too much of it; in the United States alone, the average person may be exposed to 500 to 1,000 commercial messages a day, and the resulting ad clutter makes paid advertising less effective. (4) Use of stereotypes — advertising has long been criticized for insensitivity to minorities, women, immigrants, the disabled and the elderly. (5) Offensiveness — many people object to advertisements they deem immoral or offensive, especially where children are exposed to them. Advertising is also criticized as a less forceful, impersonal medium compared with personal selling, and for its lack of feedback, since the efficiency of the message cannot easily be studied.

  9. Discuss the benefits of advertising to manufacturers, consumers and the community.
    Topic: Core Subjective Topics

    Advertising benefits all sections of society. Benefits to manufacturers: it helps in the creation of demand for new products, stabilizes and increases demand for existing products, helps in the introduction of new products, supports retail price maintenance, increases profits, creates a brand image in the minds of consumers, reduces the cost of production, establishes direct contact between manufacturers and consumers, and helps in getting efficient and effective middlemen. Benefits to consumers: it provides knowledge of new products and their alternative uses, makes purchasing convenient by telling where and when to buy, provides opportunities to compare the merits and demerits of substitute products, saves time and labour, reduces the possibility of cheating by sellers, is highly informative, raises the standard of living, and eliminates middlemen. Benefits to the community: it creates more employment opportunities, increases the standard of living, encourages healthy competition, encourages research and development, helps industrial progress and prosperity, encourages art and designing, and is educative. Advertising also benefits middlemen by increasing the rate of turnover of stock and ensuring economical selling, and benefits salesmen by making the introduction of new products easy and reducing sales efforts.

  10. Classify advertising by geographical area and by target audience.
    Topic: Core Subjective Topics

    By geographical area, advertising is classified as: (1) International advertising — advertising a product or service in a country other than where it originates. (2) National advertising — its range extends to the territorial limits of the country, using national media and preferably the national language. (3) Regional advertising — confined to one region of the country, used when a product is available and consumed at regional level. (4) Local advertising — placed by a company, organization or individual operating in a limited geographical area such as a city or a state. (5) Retail advertising — conveys the message of local merchants or service organizations directly to consumers to induce them to visit and buy at the store. By target audience, advertising is classified as: (1) Consumer advertising — directed to individuals and families for daily use and consumption. (2) Business/industrial advertising — addressed to purchasing agents, plant managers, engineers and others responsible for buying goods needed to make products and render services. (3) Professional advertising — directed at professional people. (4) Agriculture advertising — generic advertising by producers of agricultural commodities to expand total demand. (5) Public service advertising — aimed at social welfare rather than sales. (6) Non-product advertising — used by political parties and social groups to safeguard ideologies and promote social betterment.

  11. What is an infomercial? Discuss its objective and types.
    Topic: Core Subjective Topics

    An infomercial is a long-format television commercial, typically five minutes or longer. The word 'infomercial' is a combination of the words 'information' and 'commercial'. The main objective of an infomercial is to create an impulse purchase, so that the consumer sees the presentation and then immediately buys the product through the advertised toll-free telephone number or website. Infomercials describe, display and often demonstrate products and their features, and commonly include testimonials from consumers and industry professionals. There are two types of infomercials: long form and short form. Long form infomercials have a time length of 30 minutes, while short form infomercials are 30 seconds to 2 minutes long. Infomercials are also known as direct response television (DRTV) commercials or direct response marketing.

  12. Differentiate between display advertising and classified advertising in newspapers.
    Topic: Core Subjective Topics

    Newspaper advertising comes in two major divisions: display and classified. Display ads are larger advertisements that span multiple columns horizontally and often include graphics and borders; they are higher profile ads that can take up an eighth, a quarter, half or a full page, and in rare cases two facing pages. Display ads may be in colour or black and white, with the colour option costing more, and they can be found throughout the paper. Classified advertising, on the other hand, is in-column advertising that follows the natural flow of the column down the page vertically. Classified advertising is usually the cheapest option for most people and appeals to those who are after a certain market — for example, people seeking to rent an apartment, find a job or buy a pet typically use classified ads. Classified ads are usually found in a specific section of the newspaper, although many publications now offer small graphics and borders with them as well.

  13. What is effective communication? Discuss the barriers to effective communication.
    Topic: Core Subjective Topics

    A communication in which the communicator is successful in achieving his desired goals and objectives is called effective communication. Effective communication should generate the desired effect and maintain the effect, with the potential to increase the effect of the message; it serves the purpose for which it was planned or designed, such as eliciting change, generating action, creating understanding, informing, or communicating a certain idea or point of view. When the desired effect is not achieved, barriers to communication are explored. Barriers to effective communication can retard or distort the message and the intention being conveyed, which may result in failure of the communication process or an undesirable effect. These barriers include filtering, selective perception, information overload, emotions, language, communication apprehension and gender differences. They also include a lack of expression, which occurs when a person uses ambiguous or complex legal words, or descriptions of a situation or environment that are not understood by the recipient.

  14. Explain the 4Cs model of effective communication.
    Topic: Core Subjective Topics

    The 4Cs model is a useful tool for objectively evaluating the effectiveness of many forms of communication. The four Cs are: (1) Comprehension — does the audience get the message, the main idea, the point? If the audience can play the message back, the first C is working. (2) Connection — making a connection means the audience not only gets the message but that it resonates with them; it has meaning and significance for them and usually triggers an emotional response such as frustration, excitement, anger, passion, joy or sadness, and when connection is there it sparks new behaviours and actions. (3) Credibility — the audience needs to believe who is saying it (the brand or messenger's voice), what is being said, and how it is being said; credibility is the critical C, because the audience may completely understand a message and even connect with it emotionally, yet reject it because of its source. (4) Contagiousness — in communications, contagiousness is a good thing: you want your audience to 'catch the message', run with it and spread it around; to be contagious, a message has to be energetic, new, different and memorable, evoke a vivid emotional response, have 'talk' potential, motivate the target to do something, and elicit a demonstrable reaction.

  15. Describe the process of advertising in detail.
    Topic: Core Subjective Topics

    The process of advertising generally involves the following steps: (1) Briefing — the advertiser briefs the agency about the product or service to be advertised, with an analysis of the company and the product. (2) Knowing the objective — first knowing the objective or purpose of advertising, i.e., what message is to be delivered to the audience. (3) Research — finding out the market behavior, knowing the competitors and the type of advertising they are using, the response of consumers, and the availability of resources. (4) Target audience — the target audience should be appropriately identified without any confusion. (5) Media selection — selecting an appropriate media so that the target audience is successfully reached. (6) Setting the budget — the advertising budget has to be planned so that there is no shortage of funds or losses to the company. (7) Designing and creating the ad — first the design, the outline of the ad on paper, is made by the copywriters, then the actual creation is done with the help of art directors and creative personnel. (8) Perfection — refining the ad to the required standard. (9) Place and time of ad — deciding where and when the ad will appear. (10) Execution — running the advertisement as planned. (11) Performance — evaluating the results of the advertising.

  16. Explain the AIDA model of advertising.
    Topic: Core Subjective Topics

    The AIDA model suggests that for an advertisement to be effective it has to be one that: commands Awareness, leads to Interest in the product, then to Desire to own or use the product, and finally leads to Action. For the advertisement to contribute to success, it has to be designed so that the customer passes through all these four phases, with all being equally important. The model implies that advertising should inject memorable and believable messages that will make customers triggered to act in a certain way. It is an attention-getter model, and its first stage emphasizes drawing the attention of the target consumer. However, the model has been criticized: critics claim that there is no evidence that customers behave in this rational, linear way; the model ignores the role of context, environment and mediation in influencing the effectiveness of the advertisement; it represents only high-involvement purchases, whereas many purchases are spontaneous rather than the result of a rational process.

  17. Explain the DAGMAR model of advertising.
    Topic: Core Subjective Topics

    DAGMAR stands for Defining Advertising Goals for Measured Advertising Results. In 1961, Colley wrote a book under the sponsorship of the Association of National Advertisers called 'Defining Advertising Goals for Measured Advertising Results', which became known as the DAGMAR approach. DAGMAR is much more than a model for designing an advert; it is used to monitor and evaluate promotion campaigns. It has three parts: (1) Defining advertising goals so that results, and therefore campaign effectiveness, can be measured — a good advertising goal is a written, measurable task involving a starting point, a defined audience and a time limit; to measure effectiveness, a business must know the current state before advertising starts and the new state after the campaign has finished. (2) The four stages customers pass through before they buy a product or service: awareness (customers must know the product exists), comprehension (customers understand what the product will do for them — its benefits and features), conviction (building a positive attitude and persuading the customer to buy this particular product), and action (the customer buys, or takes any step towards buying). (3) Measuring advertising results against the goals that were set.

  18. Compare print, TV, radio and online advertising.
    Topic: Core Subjective Topics

    Print, television, radio and online advertising differ in cost, audience, measurement and flexibility. Cost: television advertising can be expensive — producing TV advertisements is notoriously expensive, with costs for writers and actors, and television air time is pricey; Internet advertising can reach a larger, targeted audience for less, and producing radio commercials is far simpler and less costly than producing TV commercials. Audience: television is generally considered broad in targeting, while an Internet ad can be more specifically targeted and can reach beyond borders to an international audience; Internet media also attracts a younger age audience compared with mass media. Effectiveness measurement: the Internet offers marketers an unprecedented ability to measure audience size and analyze micro details such as which pages were visited and how long the stays lasted; television advertising, by contrast, is difficult, if not impossible, to track. Message flexibility: a key difference is the ability to quickly change the message on the Internet if it is not working; changing a print message after printing is impossible, and changing a broadcast message can be very costly. TV is generally considered the most effective mass-marketing advertising format, while newspaper advertising is often cheaper than broadcast advertising and does not depend on the audience having a television or radio on at a certain time.

  19. What is an advertising agency? Discuss its functions.
    Topic: Core Subjective Topics

    An advertising agency is a firm that creates new promotional ideas, designs print, radio, television and internet advertisements, books advertisement space and time, plans and conducts advertising campaigns, commissions research and surveys, and provides other services that help a client in entering and succeeding in a chosen market. According to the American Association of Advertising, it is an independent organization of creative people and business people who specialize in developing and preparing market plans, advertisements and other promotional tools. The functions of an advertising agency include: (1) Clients and accounting — maintaining proper accounts in co-operation with the client, accounting for fees received and payment of taxes, bills and other charges. (2) Creative and production — processing the information collected from the client and through research, planning creative strategies, copy or script writing, visualization, designing, layout, shooting of films, editing and music. (3) Distribution and media planning — preparing a media plan on the basis of the media habits of the target audience, deciding which media to use, when to place the ads and for how long. (4) Research — agencies use research as a tool to test consumer reactions to products and services. (5) Monitoring feedback — by monitoring consumer feedback, a decision can be made whether to revise the message, the medium, the target audience or all of them. (6) Allied services — merchandising, public relations, publicity material, event management and direct marketing.

  20. Discuss the types of advertising agencies.
    Topic: Core Subjective Topics

    The main types of advertising agencies are: (1) Full service agencies — these provide services related to advertising such as copywriting, artwork, production of ads and media planning, and also services in respect of pricing, distribution, packaging and product design; a client can purchase all of its marketing communication requirements from a single source, which leads to synergy gains and a single point of contact. (2) Creative boutiques — these focus only on the delivery of creative solutions to clients' communication problems; their work is often short-term and project based, and clients can often get more attention and better access to creative talent than at a larger agency, though boutiques cannot compete in providing integrated communication strategies. (3) Media buying services / media independents — these provide specialist media services in the planning, buying and monitoring of clients' media schedules, particularly in television and radio, employing specialists with detailed knowledge of the media industry and skill in analysis, planning and negotiating. (4) E-commerce agencies — these handle activities related to electronic commerce, from creating websites, web page design, online promotions and email campaigns to developing banner advertising. (5) In-house agencies — these operate within an organization's own departmental structure and take responsibility for planning, developing and implementing the organization's own marketing communication activities, giving the client better access and more control over agency personnel.

  21. How are advertising agencies compensated? Discuss the methods of agency compensation.
    Topic: Core Subjective Topics

    Advertising agencies are paid by different methods, but usually three methods are in practice: (1) Commission system — this is the most common and oldest system of remuneration; the agency is paid a fixed commission by the media on the advertising bill for the ad space bought by the agency. The fixed rate of commission is 15 percent in the USA as well as in Pakistan, and though rates vary from country to country, the rate of 15 percent is almost universal. (2) Fee system — this system is used in TV advertising, where once the commercial is created it may be used over a long time; a flat fee is paid to the agency for the specialized services performed by it. (3) Service charges — these are added to the cost of materials and services bought by the agency for the client, such as artwork, photography, typography and plates; normally, it is cost plus 15 percent.

  22. Discuss the stages in the client-agency relationship.
    Topic: Core Subjective Topics

    In the advertising business, the life cycle of the client-agency relationship has four distinct stages: (1) Pre-relationship stage — this occurs before an agency and client officially do business; they may know each other by reputation, by previous ads or through social contact, and through the presentation process the agency tries to give the best impression it can, because it is selling and the client is buying. (2) Development stage — once the agency is appointed, the development stage begins; agency and client are at the peak of their optimism, the rules of the relationship are established, the respective roles are set, and the agency creates the first work, which is eagerly awaited and judged very thoroughly. (3) Maintenance stage — the year-in, year-out, day-to-day working relationship; when successful it may go on for many years — for example, the Unilever/J. Walter Thompson relationship lasted 92 years, although the average client-agency relationship is much shorter, about 7 or 8 years. (4) Termination stage — at some point an irreconcilable difference may occur and the relationship must be ended, perhaps because the agency has acquired a new competing account or its creative work does not seem to be working; the way the termination is handled affects both sides for a long time. The factors that influence the relationship can be grouped into four Cs: Chemistry, Communication, Conduct and Changes, of which personal chemistry between the client's and the agency's staff is the most critical factor.

  23. What is a creative brief? Discuss its key elements.
    Topic: Core Subjective Topics

    A creative brief is a one or two page document written by the account manager, with the support of the planner, and signed off by the client. It sets out what the advertising is to achieve and how it will be judged, and it is intended to inspire advertising creative people by focusing their thinking and clarifying the dos and don'ts. A creative brief may contain: Background — what is the background of the project and why is it being done? Target audience — to whom are we trying to sell it, including as detailed a profile of the target audience as possible, what they already think about the subject, and anything that should be avoided. Objectives — what is to be accomplished, and how it will be measured and success understood. Single message — what is the one thing to tell the audience, the single thing they should remember about the offering, and why they should believe it. Evidence and differentiators — why is this product the best, what supports that claim, and what makes it different from competitors. Mandatory elements — what the client has indicated must be included, such as the client's logo, address and phone number. Deliverables — what is to be used to give the audience the message and the best way or place to reach them. Timeline — how soon it is needed and how many rounds of revisions the project will undergo. Budget — how much can be spent. Approvals — who needs to give the 'okay'. A brief recap of available market research is also included.

  24. Discuss the structure and working of the creative department of an advertising agency.
    Topic: Core Subjective Topics

    The creative department of an advertising agency exists to develop advertising campaigns aimed at a specific audience, designed to achieve specific objectives, and to do so within certain budget constraints. The creative director is in charge of all creative output and is responsible for delivering the right creative environment through inspiration and team leadership; the greatest quality needed in a creative director is the ability to set high standards, and he is often called the 'last line of defense' in the creative department. The creative team consists of two types of creative people — art directors and copywriters, often simply known as the creatives; this pairing was devised by Bill Bernbach. The copywriter/art director team is given a brief and works on it together: the most important requirement of a copywriter is the ability to write and to create original, fresh ideas, while the art director is responsible for designing ads, websites, outdoor media and brochures, creating and maintaining the visual look of all the work on an account and making sure the selling message is clear. Designers assist the art directors and copywriters with campaign materials and pure design work, while production artists take campaigns and prepare them for print — setting up files for the printing press, creating versions of one ad for multiple publications, and updating existing campaigns. The creative idea itself is developed through four stages: the creative brief and key message, the creative proposition, the creative idea (developed through brainstorming and shown to the client as concept boards or scamps), and the execution, in which the accepted idea is developed into the finished advertisement.

  25. What is layout? Discuss the basic elements of a good layout.
    Topic: Core Subjective Topics

    Layout is the arrangement of the various elements of advertising copy — headlines, illustrations, slogans, text, closing idea and the identification mark — in regard to one another. An advertisement layout is a blueprint: its main functions are to assemble and arrange the different elements of an advertisement into a unified presentation of the sales message, to offer an opportunity of modification before final approval, and to provide specifications for estimating costs and a guide for engravers, typographers and other craft workers. The basic elements of a good layout are: (1) Balance — related to the optical center of an advertisement, the point which the eye designates as the center of an area. (2) Proportion — concerned with the division of space and the emphasis to be accorded to each element, including the tone of the ad. (3) Movement — the eyes follow a particular movement; readers of western script habitually look from left to right and then from top to bottom. (4) Unity — all the elements in the ad must be united into a composite whole, achieved by grouping the elements, encasing the ad in a border, aligning or overlapping elements. (5) Mood — sizes, textures, colours, illustrations and type contribute to creating a mood for the ad. (6) Colour psychology — colour adds realism, beauty and distinctiveness, and various colours depict various moods. (7) Typography — the art of selecting typefaces; good typography leads to legibility and attractiveness. The two basic devices for illustrating an ad are photography, which creates a feeling of immediacy and live action, and drawing, including line drawings and wash drawings.

  26. Discuss the stages of the ad design process.
    Topic: Core Subjective Topics

    The design process serves as both a creative and an approval process. In the creative phase the designer uses thumbnails, roughs, dummies and comprehensives — non-final art — to establish the ad's look and feel, ending with the final artwork with the actual type in place along with all the visuals the printer will need. The stages are: (1) Thumbnail sketches — a thumbnail is a small, rough, rapidly produced drawing the artist uses to visualize layout approaches without wasting time on details; blocks of straight or squiggly lines indicate text placement and boxes show placement of visuals, and the best sketches are developed further. (2) Rough layout — in a rough, the artist draws to the actual size of the ad. (3) Comprehensive — a polished, near-final version of the ad in which all visuals should be final. (4) Dummy — a dummy presents the handheld look and feel of brochures, multipage materials or point-of-purchase displays, assembled by hand page by page to look exactly like the finished product. (5) Mechanical (pasteup) — the type and visuals are placed into their exact position for reproduction by the printer; printers refer to the mechanical or pasteup as camera ready art because they photograph it before starting the reproduction process. Changes can be made at any time until the printing press lays ink on paper, but the expense may grow tenfold with each step from roughs to mechanicals to printing. Approval takes place throughout the process: a new ad concept is first approved by the agency's creative director, then reviewed by the account management team, then by the client's product managers and marketing staff, the legal departments, and finally the company's top executives.

  27. What is advertising copy? Discuss the various types of copy.
    Topic: Core Subjective Topics

    Copy refers to written material which is to be set in type for the print media or spoken by announcers for broadcast commercials. Although earlier ads contained only the advertising message in words, gradually pictures, photographs, visuals, symbols, sketches and illustrations gained a larger space, so the term 'advertising message' now covers what is communicated by the entire ad rather than only the words of the headlines, sub-heads and body copy. The general objectives of ad copy include action (direct action, where the copy seeks an immediate response such as buying or ordering, and indirect action, where the reader retains a favourable impression, as in institutional and public service copy), psychological objectives (every ad should attract attention, develop interest, stimulate desire, carry conviction and encourage action), marketing objectives and institutional objectives. The main types of copy are: scientific copy, in which the technical specifications of a product are explained in scientific terms (for example, Saffola, a low cholesterol edible oil); descriptive copy, in which product attributes are described in a non-technical manner in short, pithy sentences; topical copy, integrated with a recent happening or event; endorsement copy; questioning copy; prestige copy; 'reason why' copy; human interest copy; colloquial copy; personality copy; educational copy; visionary copy; service copy; sequence or strip copy; picture and caption copy; musical copy; and horizontal co-operative copy.

  28. What are the qualities of a good advertising copy? Discuss.
    Topic: Core Subjective Topics

    A good advertisement copy should possess the following qualities: (1) Attention value — the copy must attract the attention of potential consumers, using display type for brief and meaningful headings, pictures (as the proverb says, 'one seeing is worth a thousand telling'), attractive borders and reply coupons. (2) Suggestive value — after attracting attention, the copy should offer a suggestion about the use and utility of the product that remains inscribed on the mind of the reader. (3) Memorising value — the copy should be drafted and laid out so that the product sticks to the mind of the reader; repetition of the advertisement with a slogan is an effective method of creating memorising value. (4) Conviction value — the suggestion contained in the copy must be backed by convincing arguments and truthful statements about the product. (5) Sentimental value — sentiments reflect the personal feelings and attitudes of individuals and play a very important role, particularly in the case of food articles. (6) Educational value — a good copy must educate the general public about the uses of the new product or the new uses of the same product, in order to create future demand. (7) Instructive appeal value — the copy should appeal to the basic instincts that guide human thoughts and actions, such as self-preservation, parental instinct and self-display, using themes like pride, beauty, health, economy, comfort, fear, parental affection, patriotism, achievement and emulation. The psychological aims of an advertisement are that the public must be made to look, like, learn and buy; as James Hunter puts it, the aim of advertising copy is that it shall be seen, read, the message conveyed and then acted upon. Prices should generally not be quoted in the copy except when they are very low, as in a clearance sale or special offers.

  29. What qualifications should a copywriter possess? Discuss.
    Topic: Core Subjective Topics

    Copywriting is basically a kind of message construction, written to produce a certain response and to reach a specific objective. To survive in a fast-paced environment, a copywriter should have the following qualifications: (1) Sense of inquiry — a creative and effective copywriter is interested in trying novel approaches, with an inquiring mind that thinks 'why not try this' rather than staying with the tried and true; the 'why not' approach helps develop new idea combinations. (2) Discipline — broadcasting does not allow the luxury of waiting for the idea; the copywriter must write copy and prepare it for broadcast on time. (3) Knowledge of language — simplicity is the key to broadcast writing; complex sentences and overblown vocabularies should be avoided, commercials should be written in acceptable language accessible to the average person, and accepted rules of grammar should be used. (4) Knowledge of the media — a broadcast copywriter must understand the capabilities of the media to be used: what radio or television can best accomplish in selling a product, and what level of production capability exists at the station, since many commercial ideas are only producible in a sophisticated studio with adequate time and a large budget. (5) Ability to deal with matters of taste — a copywriter may be asked to write for advertisers selling products he or she does not like, or to take approaches he or she is not comfortable with; this may cause dissonance, but it is an element of copywriting that must be handled professionally.

  30. Discuss the elements of print ad copy.
    Topic: Core Subjective Topics

    In print advertising, the key format elements are headlines, visuals, subheads, body copy, slogans, seals, logotypes and signatures. (1) Headlines — the words in the leading position in the advertisement, read first and situated to draw the most attention; effective headlines attract attention, engage the audience, explain the visual, lead the audience into the body of the ad and present the selling message; on average, three to five times as many people read the headline as read the body copy. Headline types include label, selective, benefit, news/information, question, command, situation, affirmative, negative and testimonial headlines. (2) Subheads — an additional smaller headline, set smaller than the headline but larger than the body copy, usually in bold face or a different colour; a subhead above the headline is called a kicker or overline; subheads transmit key selling points fast and support the interest step. (3) Body copy — the advertiser tells the complete sales story in the body copy, covering the features, benefits and utility of the product; it comprises the interest, credibility, desire and often the action steps, and is read by only one out of ten readers. Common body copy styles are straight sell, institutional, narrative, dialogue/monologue, picture-caption and device copy; long copy is formulated through a lead-in paragraph, interior paragraphs, a trial close and a close. (4) Slogans — also called theme lines or taglines, they provide continuity to a series of ads in a campaign and reduce an advertising message strategy to a brief, repeatable and memorable positioning statement. (5) Seals — awarded only when a product meets standards established by a particular organization, such as the Good Housekeeping Institute or Underwriters Laboratories, providing an independent, valued endorsement. (6) Logotypes and signatures — special designs of the advertiser's company or product name that appear in all company ads and, like trademarks, give the product individuality and quick recognition at the point of purchase.
