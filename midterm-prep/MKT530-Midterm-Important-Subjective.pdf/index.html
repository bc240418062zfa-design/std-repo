  1. "Classical conditioning theory is a passive learning." Justify this statement.
    Topic: Midterm Paper Questions (Solved) • 5 marks marks

    Classical conditioning is called passive learning because the consumer does not learn through active effort, reasoning or trial and error; learning happens automatically and reflexively when two stimuli are repeatedly paired together. In Pavlov's experiment, the dog learned to salivate at the sound of a bell simply because the bell was repeatedly presented with food. The learner's response is involuntary — it is elicited by the stimulus rather than chosen. In the same way, a consumer exposed repeatedly to an advertisement that pairs a brand with pleasant music or attractive scenes develops a favourable feeling towards the brand without consciously evaluating it. Because the consumer remains a passive recipient of stimulus pairings rather than an active problem-solver, classical conditioning is termed passive (reflexive) learning.

  2. Smell/aroma of a store, coffee shop or hotel has a positive effect on customers. Give arguments in support of this statement.
    Topic: Midterm Paper Questions (Solved) • 5 marks marks

    A pleasant aroma works on the consumer in several ways. First, it creates a pleasant store environment, which attracts customers and puts them in a positive mood, so they form a good perception of the store, coffee shop or hotel. Second, customers feel relaxed and comfortable, so they stay longer in the store — and the longer they stay, the greater the chances of additional and unplanned purchases. Pleasant scents also improve the evaluation of products and service quality, build a distinctive store image that customers remember, and encourage repeat visits. Hence fragrance is a powerful atmospheric tool that positively affects customers' emotions, perceptions and buying behaviour.

  3. One school of thought says that marketers do not create needs, while the second says that marketers do create needs. Which one will you favour? Give at least five arguments.
    Topic: Midterm Paper Questions (Solved) • 5 marks marks

    The favoured view is that marketers do not create needs; they only make consumers aware of existing needs and offer ways to satisfy them. Arguments: (1) Needs are basic human requirements — food, clothing, shelter, safety, esteem — that existed long before marketing. (2) A need is a state of felt deprivation; marketers cannot manufacture hunger or the need for safety. (3) Marketers can only influence wants — the form a need takes — by offering particular products, e.g., they cannot create the need for food but can create a want for a particular brand of burger. (4) Products that do not serve any real need fail in the market, which proves marketers cannot force needs on consumers. (5) Advertising informs and reminds consumers and activates latent needs, but the underlying need must already be present. (6) Consumer sovereignty means buyers ultimately decide; they reject offerings that satisfy no genuine need.

  4. Miss Sarah knows that smart mobile phones are good for professionals. She is also aware that Samsung Galaxy has good features, and she is searching for Samsung Galaxy features and prices on the internet. According to the tri-component attitude model, what is the attitude of Miss Sarah towards Samsung Galaxy?
    Topic: Midterm Paper Questions (Solved) • 3 marks marks

    According to the tri-component attitude model, an attitude has three components: cognitive (knowledge and beliefs), affective (feelings and emotions) and conative (intention to act). Miss Sarah's case belongs to the cognitive component. She knows that smart mobile phones are good for professionals and she is aware that the Samsung Galaxy has good features — these are her beliefs and knowledge about the object, formed through awareness and information. Her favourable belief that the Galaxy is a good phone shows a positive cognitive attitude towards it. Her searching for its features and price on the internet further shows that the cognitive component is active and is moving her towards the conative stage of actually buying it.

  5. Bata shoes' pricing strategy ranges from Rs. 299 to Rs. 1999, but customers perceive it as Rs. 200 to Rs. 1000. Moreover, after shopping, customers did not notice that the cashier had not paid them back the correct change. What does the customers' behaviour show?
    Topic: Midterm Paper Questions (Solved) • 5 marks marks

    This behaviour shows that consumers perceive and notice many things without being consciously aware of them — perception operates below the level of conscious awareness (subliminal perception). Bata's prices (Rs. 299–1999) are perceived by customers only within a broad, approximate range (Rs. 200–1000) because consumers attend to price information selectively and store it inexactly. Similarly, at the cash counter the customer did not notice the short change because the difference fell below his just noticeable difference and outside his conscious attention. Research (e.g., the National Institute of Mental Health study, 1990) shows that stimuli presented so as to be unidentifiable to conscious perception can still affect judgement and behaviour. Thus the case illustrates subconscious/subliminal perception and the limits of conscious attention in everyday buying.

  6. Which group in the VALS framework shows switching behaviour, and why?
    Topic: Midterm Paper Questions (Solved) • 3 marks marks

    Switching behaviour is shown by the Innovators (actualizers) in the VALS framework. Innovators are successful, sophisticated consumers with abundant resources, so their basic motivation (ideals, achievement or self-expression) can be satisfied by many different brands. They are variety-seeking, curious about new products and always ready to try something new. Because they can afford to experiment and use logical thinking in purchasing, they show low brand loyalty — they buy one product, use it, and then move on to another. This is why companies normally prefer to target these customers when launching new products: they are the most willing to switch and try.

  7. Advertising jingles such as "Ek bili moti tazi thi" of Ding Dong Bubble Gum and "Ay Khuda mere abbu salamat rahain" of State Life Insurance are very memorable. Can such jingles benefit marketers? Explain.
    Topic: Midterm Paper Questions (Solved) • 3 marks marks

    Yes, memorable jingles benefit marketers a great deal. A catchy jingle is stored in the consumer's long-term memory and is recalled instantly whenever the brand or product category is mentioned, giving the brand free recall and top-of-mind awareness for years. Jingles build strong brand associations and emotional attachment — generations remember lines like the Ding Dong bubble gum jingle and State Life's "Ay Khuda mere abbu salamat rahain". Because the tune and brand are conditioned together through repetition, marketers can also use the same jingles in repositioning an existing product and in launching new products, since customers already feel attraction and trust towards them. Jingles therefore cut through advertising clutter at almost no extra cost.

  8. A person goes to a large store where more than 30,000 products are available. There are discount banners and product announcements all around, but the person does not notice them and stays busy managing his purchase, and after purchasing he leaves. What does this behaviour of the person show?
    Topic: Midterm Paper Questions (Solved) • 3 marks marks

    This shows selective attention (selective exposure) in perception. A consumer is exposed to thousands of stimuli every day — here more than 30,000 products, discount banners and announcements — but consciously notices only a tiny fraction of them. The shopper screened out the banners and announcements because they were not relevant to his current purchase goal; his attention was focused on managing his own purchase. Consumers tend to notice stimuli that relate to their current needs and block out the rest (perceptual blocking/selectivity). It also shows a positive, task-focused attitude: his perception of the shopping task remained positive and he stayed loyal to his planned purchase instead of being distracted by in-store stimuli.

  9. What is market segmentation? Briefly explain the bases of market segmentation.
    Topic: Midterm Paper Questions (Solved) • 3 marks marks

    Market segmentation is the process of dividing a large, heterogeneous market into smaller, distinct groups (segments) of consumers who have common needs, characteristics or behaviour and who might require separate products or marketing mixes. Its main bases are: (1) Geographic — region, city size, climate; (2) Demographic — age, gender, income, occupation, education, family size and life-cycle stage; (3) Psychographic — social class, lifestyle and personality; and (4) Behavioural — occasions, benefits sought, usage rate, user status and loyalty. Segmentation lets a marketer design a precise marketing mix for each target segment instead of treating the whole market as identical.

  10. Explain the tri-component attitude model with the help of an example.
    Topic: Midterm Paper Questions (Solved) • 3 marks marks

    The tri-component attitude model states that an attitude consists of three interrelated components. (1) Cognitive component — the knowledge and beliefs a consumer has about an object, e.g., "this car gives good mileage". (2) Affective component — the consumer's emotions and feelings about the object, e.g., "I love this car". (3) Conative component — the likelihood or tendency that the consumer will act towards the object, e.g., the intention to buy the car. The three components are consistent with each other: beliefs shape feelings, and feelings lead to buying intentions. Marketers therefore try to change beliefs, feelings or intentions in order to change overall attitudes.

  11. How can repositioning change consumers' attitude towards a product?
    Topic: Midterm Paper Questions (Solved) • 3 marks marks

    Repositioning means changing the way consumers perceive a brand — altering its image or the beliefs held about it — without necessarily changing the product itself. It changes attitudes because attitudes rest on beliefs: when a brand is repositioned, for example from "an old-fashioned drink" to "a modern energy drink", consumers form new beliefs about its benefits and users, their feelings become more favourable, and purchase intention rises. Repositioning is used when sales decline, when competitors crowd the original position, or when the firm wants to attract a new segment. It works through new advertising themes, new usage occasions and new target users, which together reshape the cognitive and affective components of attitude.

  12. Explain the attitude-toward-behaviour model. How is it different from simply measuring attitude towards the product?
    Topic: Midterm Paper Questions (Solved) • 5 marks marks

    The attitude-toward-behaviour model (an extension of the theory of reasoned action) proposes that a consumer's behaviour is best predicted not by attitude towards the product itself, but by the attitude towards the act of purchasing or using it. A consumer may hold a very favourable attitude towards an expensive sports car (he loves it) yet have a negative attitude towards buying it (because of price, family disapproval or maintenance cost), so he does not buy. The model therefore considers the consumer's evaluation of the consequences of the behaviour and social/subjective norms — what important others think of the purchase. For marketers, the lesson is to improve attitudes towards the buying act itself, e.g., through easy instalments, test drives and social approval in advertising.

  13. A tobacco company launches a new product, a menthol cigarette. Suggest a model to diffuse this new innovative product in the market.
    Topic: Midterm Paper Questions (Solved) • 3 marks marks

    The appropriate model is the diffusion of innovations process, through which a new product spreads through a market over time as different adopter categories accept it: innovators (2.5%), early adopters (13.5%), early majority (34%), late majority (34%) and laggards (16%). For the menthol cigarette, the company should first target innovators and early adopters — smokers who like experimenting and are opinion leaders among their peers — through free samples, introductory offers and visibility in trendy places. Their adoption and word of mouth then legitimise the product for the early majority. Communication should stress the innovation's relative advantage (freshness, mild taste), compatibility with existing smoking habits, trialability (small packs) and observability, because these product characteristics speed up diffusion.

  14. How can marketers market a washing machine to men? Suggest practical steps.
    Topic: Midterm Paper Questions (Solved) • 3 marks marks

    To market a washing machine to men, marketers should first recognise changing gender roles: many men now live alone, share housework, or buy appliances for the family. Practical steps: (1) Position the machine on masculine appeals — technology, power, performance and engineering (e.g., motor power, load capacity, smart features) rather than only on family care. (2) Use male endorsers and show men confidently operating the machine in advertisements, so men can identify with the user image. (3) Stress time-saving, convenience and gadget appeal — programmable settings, digital displays — which match men's buying motives. (4) Place and promote it where men shop and watch media (electronics outlets, sports and news channels, online tech platforms). (5) Offer self-installation guides and durable, dark-coloured designs that fit men's self-image. Such targeting works on men's self-concept and lifestyle rather than treating laundry as an exclusively female task.

  15. Define consumer behaviour. Why is it important for marketers to study consumer behaviour?
    Topic: Important Topics — Solved Questions

    Consumer behaviour is the study of the processes involved when individuals or groups select, purchase, use or dispose of products, services, ideas or experiences to satisfy their needs and desires. Marketers study it because successful marketing begins with understanding why consumers buy. It helps them to: identify target segments and their needs; design products and position them correctly; choose the right price, distribution and promotion appeals; predict responses to new products; and build long-term relationships and brand loyalty. Without consumer research, marketing decisions become guesswork and products fail despite heavy spending.

  16. Differentiate between needs, wants and demands with the help of examples.
    Topic: Important Topics — Solved Questions

    A need is a basic human requirement arising from a state of felt deprivation, e.g., hunger. A want is the form a need takes as shaped by culture and personality, e.g., the desire for a burger rather than plain food. A demand is a want backed by buying power, e.g., a customer who wants a burger and has the money to buy it becomes a demand for the restaurant. Marketers do not create needs, but they influence wants by offering attractive satisfiers, and they convert wants into demands through suitable pricing and availability.

  17. Explain Maslow's hierarchy of needs and its relevance to marketing, with examples.
    Topic: Important Topics — Solved Questions

    Maslow arranged human needs in a hierarchy of five levels, from lowest to highest: (1) Physiological needs — food, water, sleep; (2) Safety needs — security, protection, insurance; (3) Social needs — love, belonging, friendship; (4) Esteem needs — status, prestige, self-respect; (5) Self-actualization — fulfilling one's potential. A person tries to satisfy lower needs before higher ones become motivating. Marketers use the hierarchy to position products: food brands appeal to physiological needs, insurance to safety, social media and clothing to belonging, luxury cars to esteem, and education or travel to self-actualization.

  18. Explain the Freudian theory of personality and its application in marketing.
    Topic: Important Topics — Solved Questions

    Freud proposed that personality is formed by three interacting systems: the id, the storehouse of primitive instincts that seeks immediate pleasure; the ego, the rational part that mediates between the id and reality; and the superego, the conscience that represents society's moral codes. Much of consumer motivation is unconscious, driven by the id's hidden desires. Marketers use this theory through motivation research — projective techniques and in-depth interviews — to uncover deep motives, and they appeal to fantasy, pleasure and symbolism in advertising (e.g., perfumes and sports cars) rather than only to functional benefits.

  19. What is the just noticeable difference (JND)? Explain its marketing implications.
    Topic: Important Topics — Solved Questions

    The just noticeable difference (JND), or differential threshold, is the minimal difference between two similar stimuli that a person can detect — governed by Weber's law, which states that the stronger the initial stimulus, the greater the additional intensity needed for a change to be noticed. Marketing implications: (1) Price increases should be kept below the JND so buyers do not notice them, while price decreases and discounts should exceed the JND so they are perceived as meaningful. (2) Reductions in package size or product quantity are often kept below the JND so consumers do not feel cheated. (3) Product improvements and quality upgrades should be made clearly above the JND and highlighted so that consumers actually perceive the added value.

  20. Differentiate between classical conditioning and operant (instrumental) conditioning, giving a marketing example of each.
    Topic: Important Topics — Solved Questions

    Classical conditioning is learning by association: a neutral stimulus is repeatedly paired with an unconditioned stimulus until it alone elicits the response (Pavlov's dog). It is passive and reflexive; marketers use it by pairing brands with pleasant music, celebrities or scenes so the brand itself creates a good feeling. Operant (instrumental) conditioning is learning by consequences: behaviour followed by rewards is repeated, and behaviour followed by punishment is avoided. It is active learning through trial and error; marketers use it through free samples, satisfaction guarantees, loyalty points and after-sale service that reward purchase behaviour. In short, classical conditioning works on stimulus association and feelings, while operant conditioning works on the outcomes of the consumer's own behaviour.

  21. What are reference groups? Differentiate between normative and comparative reference groups.
    Topic: Important Topics — Solved Questions

    A reference group is any group that serves as a point of reference for an individual in forming values, attitudes and behaviour. A normative reference group influences the consumer's general values and basic behaviour — for example, the family teaches a child table manners, dress norms and what is acceptable consumption. A comparative reference group serves as a benchmark for evaluating oneself, one's status and possessions — for example, a young executive compares his car, watch and lifestyle with those of a successful peer group he admires. Marketers appeal to normative groups by showing family-approved behaviour, and to comparative groups by presenting aspirational lifestyles consumers wish to match.

  22. Explain the roles of family members in the buying decision process.
    Topic: Important Topics — Solved Questions

    Family buying decisions involve several roles, which may be played by one person or shared among members: (1) Initiator — the person who first suggests buying the product; (2) Gatekeeper — the member who controls the flow of information about products into the family; (3) Influencer — the person whose opinion carries weight in the evaluation; (4) Decider — the member who makes the final decision; (5) Buyer (purchaser) — the person who actually makes the purchase; and (6) User — the person who consumes or uses the product. A child may initiate the idea of a video game, the father may decide, the mother may buy it, and the child is the user. Marketers design separate messages for each role-holder.

  23. What is cognitive dissonance? How can marketers help consumers reduce post-purchase dissonance?
    Topic: Important Topics — Solved Questions

    Cognitive dissonance is the discomfort a consumer feels after a purchase when he doubts whether he made the right choice — for example, worrying that another brand was better or cheaper. It is strongest for expensive, risky and long-lasting purchases. Marketers can reduce it by: sending reassuring follow-up messages and thank-you notes; providing warranties, guarantees and easy return policies; advertising that reinforces the wisdom of the choice (e.g., showing satisfied owners); offering responsive after-sale service and helplines; and supplying supporting information and reviews that confirm the product's superiority. Reducing dissonance increases satisfaction, repeat purchase and positive word of mouth.

  24. Define social class. How does social class affect consumer behaviour?
    Topic: Important Topics — Solved Questions

    Social class is the division of society into relatively permanent and ordered groups whose members share similar values, interests and behaviour; it is measured by a combination of occupation, income, education and wealth rather than by income alone. It affects consumer behaviour strongly: classes differ in the brands and stores they prefer, the media they use, their language and recreation, and the importance they give to price versus quality and status. Upper classes buy conspicuous, prestige goods; middle classes are value-conscious and future-oriented; lower classes focus on necessities and familiar local brands. Marketers therefore design different products, retail outlets and advertising appeals for different social classes.

  25. What is culture? Explain how cultural values influence consumer behaviour.
    Topic: Important Topics — Solved Questions

    Culture is the sum total of learned beliefs, values and customs that serve to direct the consumer behaviour of members of a particular society — it is the "lens" through which people view products. Cultural values shape what consumers consider right, important and desirable: they influence food habits (halal requirements), dress, family roles, attitudes towards debt and saving, colour and symbol meanings, and the importance of status or modesty. Because culture is learned and shared, marketers must adapt products, packaging and advertising to each culture — e.g., avoiding offensive symbols, using the local language and respecting religious festivals — while core values change only very slowly.
