  1. What is a theory?
    Topic: Introduction to Second Language Acquisition

    A 'theory' is a more or less abstract set of claims about the significant units within the phenomenon under study. It aims at explanation as well as description. Theories are produced collaboratively and evolve through systematic enquiry. They are assessed through hypothesis testing and involve a reflexive process. A good theory is supposed to give clear and explicit statements about its claims, and it must be testable or falsifiable in some way. Linguists view language as a complex communication system which must be analyzed on different levels.

  2. What is Property Theory?
    Topic: Introduction to Second Language Acquisition

    Linguists are more interested in 'property theory'. They are concerned with the structure of the language and how a language changes; how certain words become obsolete; and how certain factors lead to the addition of new words and merge them into a language system. In property theory, the language system itself is important.

  3. What is Transition Theory?
    Topic: Introduction to Second Language Acquisition

    'Transition theory' is more concerned with the developmental processes of language education. It is interested in finding out the different stages of learning in a second language learner. It can relate to first language acquisition, telling about the different learning stages in a child's life and how that learning is different or similar to second language learning.

  4. What are the types of theories used in SLA?
    Topic: Introduction to Second Language Acquisition

    Two broad types of theories are used in SLA. Property theories are concerned with the nature and structure of language knowledge itself — the language system. Transition theories are concerned with the developmental process of learning — how that knowledge is acquired by the learner over time. Universal Grammar is mainly a property theory, while cognitive approaches such as information processing, Processability Theory and connectionism are transition theories.

  5. Why does Noam Chomsky prefer competence over performance?
    Topic: Nature of Language and Language Learning

    Noam Chomsky prefers competence over performance. By competence, he refers to the abstract and hidden representation of language knowledge held inside the mind, with its potential to create and understand original utterances in a given language. However, there are difficulties in studying competence, as language performance data are believed to be imperfect reflections of competence. Competence can only be assessed indirectly, under controlled conditions, through wide-ranging tests.

  6. What is meant by Nature vs. Nurture in language learning?
    Topic: Nature of Language and Language Learning

    The best known controversy involves B. F. Skinner and Noam Chomsky. Skinner advocates behaviourist mechanisms (nurture); whereas Chomsky emphasized the role of innate dispositions (nature) that expect natural languages to be organized in particular ways, and supported the role of Universal Grammar (UG). The phrase 'nature and nurture' relates to the relative importance of an individual's innate qualities as compared to an individual's personal experiences in causing individual differences, especially in behavioural traits. Whatever we possess by birth is nature, and whatever we get through our experiences is nurture.

  7. What is Chomsky's strong criticism on Skinner's behaviourist view?
    Topic: Nature of Language and Language Learning

    Chomsky strongly criticized Skinner's behaviourist account of language learning. He argued that children produce and understand novel utterances they have never heard before, which cannot be explained by imitation, habit formation and reinforcement alone. The input children receive is too poor and 'messy' to account for the rich grammatical knowledge they attain, so language learning must be guided by an innate language faculty (Universal Grammar) rather than by stimulus–response habits.

  8. Write a note on Language Transfer.
    Topic: Creativity, Transfer and Language Change

    Learners' performance in a second language is influenced by the language(s) they already know, which is obvious in their accent as well as in certain characteristic mistakes in their utterances. This phenomenon is called language transfer. Behaviourists view it as an important source of error and interference in second language learning; whereas recent theorists hold widely differing views on the extent and nature of these influences. Performing in a language involves speaking, making sense of the data we hear, interpreting and processing incoming language data, and language input is essential for normal language learning.

  9. What is Fossilization?
    Topic: Second Language Use and Performance

    Fossilization refers to the situation when a learner's second language system seems to freeze, or become stuck, at some more or less deviant stage. At times certain errors seem to reoccur in students of a second language, no matter what amount of feedback you give them — this is called fossilization.

  10. What is Progressive Learning Pedagogy?
    Topic: Individual Learner Differences

    'Progressive' language pedagogy (1950s) drew on a version of structuralism developed by the British linguist Palmer. This approach is summed up as follows: (1) the conviction about a finite set of 'patterns' or 'structures' in language systems; (2) the belief that reception and practice result in accuracy and fluency in foreign language habits; (3) methodology to teach 'the basics' first before encouraging learners to communicate their own thoughts and ideas.

  11. What are the cognitive and affective factors in second language learning?
    Topic: Individual Learner Differences

    Gardner and MacIntyre divide learner traits into two groups. Cognitive factors include intelligence, language aptitude and language learning strategies. Affective (emotional) factors include language attitudes, motivation, language anxiety and willingness to communicate. (In some summaries, motivation is listed with the psychological variables alongside personality and language aptitude.)

  12. What is the Behaviourist Approach to language learning?
    Topic: Behaviourism and First Language Acquisition

    Behaviourists view language learning (like any other kind of learning) as a formation of habits; that is, the creation of stimulus–response pairings which become stronger with reinforcement. In the case of first language learning, the process is relatively simple, as we have to learn a new set of habits. Second language learning involves replacing those habits with a set of new ones, which is complicated. If SL structures are similar to the learners' first language, learning will easily take place, but if they are different, learning will be difficult. From a teacher's point of view, this approach had two-fold implications: (1) learning takes place by imitating and repeating (practice makes perfect); (2) teachers needed to focus their teaching on structures which were believed to be difficult — the areas of difference.

  13. How do behaviourists view errors in second language learning, and how can errors be controlled?
    Topic: Behaviourism and First Language Acquisition

    When we talk about errors in SL utterances, behaviourists view errors as a result of 'bad habits', which can be controlled through rote learning and pattern drilling using target language models. Recent studies contradict this view by claiming that errors and mistakes are patterned: although some of them are caused by first language influence, this is not true for all of them. The learner's language system is unstable and characterized by a high degree of variability, which is a central feature of learner interlanguage.

  14. What are the characteristics of second language learning carried forward from the 1970s? (Three fundamental issues)
    Topic: Krashen's Monitor Model

    From the brief and oversimplified account of 1970s first language acquisition research, the following characteristics emerge: (1) Children go through stages. (2) These stages are very similar across children for a given language, although individual progress rate varies. (3) These stages are similar across languages. (4) Child language is rule-governed and systematic, and their rules do not necessarily correspond to adult ones. (5) Children are resistant to correction. (6) Their processing capacity limits the number of rules they can apply at any one time.

  15. What is Krashen's Monitor Model based on? (Learning–Acquisition Hypothesis)
    Topic: Krashen's Monitor Model

    Krashen's Monitor Model (evolved in the late 1970s and refined in the early 1980s) is based around five basic hypotheses: 1. The Acquisition–Learning hypothesis; 2. The Monitor hypothesis; 3. The Natural Order hypothesis; 4. The Input hypothesis; 5. The Affective Filter hypothesis. According to the Acquisition–Learning hypothesis, language acquisition and learning are separate processes: acquisition is the subconscious process that results from natural interaction with language via meaningful communication, whereas learning is a conscious process that results in 'knowing about' language. The hypothesis has been criticized for its vague definition of what constitutes conscious versus subconscious processes, and for Krashen's claim that learning cannot turn into acquisition.

  16. What is Comprehensible Input?
    Topic: SLA Hypotheses

    Comprehensible input is a critical concept for second language development. It means that students should be able to understand the essence of what is being said or presented to them. This does not mean that teachers must use only words students understand; in fact, instruction can be incomprehensible even when students know all of the words. Students learn a new language best when they receive input that is just a bit more difficult than they can easily understand — input at the level 'i+1', where 'i' is the learner's current level. According to Krashen, humans acquire language in only one way — by understanding messages, or by receiving comprehensible input.

  17. Write the criticism on the Input Hypothesis.
    Topic: SLA Hypotheses

    Major criticism of the Input Hypothesis claims: (1) it is vague and a-theoretical; (2) it is impossible to verify the theory, as no independently testable definitions are given — it does not tell how we determine level 'i' and level 'i+1'; (3) the theory does not specify the internal workings of the 'Language Acquisition Device' where acquisition actually takes place; (4) just comprehensible input is not enough — learners also need to 'let that input in'; (5) the hypothesis focuses only on comprehensible input and neglects any possible importance of output; and the Affective Filter supposedly determines how receptive to the input a learner is going to be.

  18. What is Schumann's Acculturation Model?
    Topic: SLA Hypotheses

    According to Schumann, acquiring a new language is part of a general process of acculturation, related to pidginization. A pidgin is a reduced, simplified language formulated when individuals of two different language backgrounds come into contact and need to communicate; as learners progress, they gradually add complexity to it. Success in SLA is determined by the extent to which learners can orient themselves to the target language culture. Two important factors play a role: (a) Social distance — the learner's interaction with the SL native group, in terms of time, frequency, closeness and openness to each other; (b) Psychological distance — the motivation and attitude of a person towards learning the SL, the beliefs he has for that language and culture, and his language anxiety: the less anxiety, the better the learning will take place.

  19. Write a note on the Universal Grammar (UG) approach.
    Topic: Universal Grammar

    On the basis of messy input, children create a mental representation of language which goes beyond the input they are exposed to and is very similar to that of other native speakers of the same language variety. Language is the most abstract piece of knowledge. Second language learners are cognitively mature and presumably much more resourceful in problem solving and dealing with abstract concepts. From a theoretical point of view, different scenarios are open to consideration: SL grammars are constrained by Universal Grammar (UG); or UG does not constrain SL grammar, or UG is impaired. The UG approach is concerned with knowledge of language, called 'competence'; it is not about performance — how language is used in real life. A complete theory of language also has to define how we access our knowledge base and how it relates to sociolinguistic and psychological variables.

  20. What is the scope and achievement of UG?
    Topic: Universal Grammar

    1) UG theory aims to describe and explain human language. 2) It is nonetheless directly relevant to the study of second language. 3) It is a linguistic theory, not a learning theory. 4) It has been hugely influential in drawing up sophisticated hypotheses regarding SLA. 5) It helps in exploring the interplay between first and second language learners and facilitates understanding of the linguistic knowledge learners bring to the task of SLA. 6) It is primarily concerned with the description and explanation of the formal system underlying language. 7) Its scope does not include a theory of processing or learning — UG is more of a property theory than a transition theory, and must be evaluated as such.

  21. What is the impact of cognitive deficit on language learning?
    Topic: Universal Grammar

    Many children with cognitive deficit develop language normally. As there are numerous examples of people with cognitive disabilities or difficulties who performed well in language(s), evidence of the opposite is also found. There are many cognitively 'normal' children with an impaired language condition known as 'specific language impairment'. A study suggests that some aspects of language at least might be genetically controlled. Damage to the left hemisphere of the brain also results in language deficit. Language and cognition seem to be largely separate, but they do interact.

  22. What is cross-linguistic variation?
    Topic: Universal Grammar

    The term linguistic variation (or simply variation) refers to regional, social, or contextual differences in the ways that a particular language is used. Variation between languages, dialects, and speakers is known as interspeaker variation. Variation within the language of a single speaker is called intraspeaker variation. According to White, there are three potential sources of cross-linguistic variation relating to functional categories: (1) languages can differ in terms of the functional categories realized in the grammar (e.g. Japanese lacks the category Determiner); (2) the features of a particular functional category can vary from language to language (e.g. French has a gender feature while English does not); (3) features vary in strength — a feature can be strong in one language and weak in another, with a range of syntactic consequences.

  23. What is the Emergentist / Constructionist view?
    Topic: Cognitive Approaches to SLA

    Emergentists or Constructionists do not consider the separation between property and transition theories as legitimate. They believe that you can explain both the nature of knowledge and how it is processed through general cognition principles. They consider that the complexity of language emerges from associative learning processes, being exposed to a massive and complex environment. Many of them believe that language develops as learners move from the learning of exemplars that are committed to memory. They claim that children pick up frequent patterns they hear around them and slowly make more abstract generalizations as the database of related utterances grows.

  24. Name the cognitive theorists of SLA.
    Topic: Models of Second Language Learning

    The cognitive theorists of SLA include: (a) McLaughlin — known for his Information Processing model (1987, 1990); (b) Anderson — known for the Active Control of Thought (ACT*) model (1983, 1985); (c) O'Malley and Chamot — known for their classification of learning strategies (1990).

  25. What is McLaughlin's Information Processing model?
    Topic: Models of Second Language Learning

    McLaughlin's model (1987–1990) is an information processing model. In it, human beings are viewed as autonomous and active beings: they voluntarily learn a language, and language is not just assimilated in their minds when they are exposed to it. The information processing approach investigates how different memory stores (short-term memory and long-term memory) deal with SL information, and how this information is automatized and restructured with repeated use. It differentiates between declarative knowledge and procedural knowledge.

  26. Write a note on Anderson's Active Control of Thought (ACT) Model.
    Topic: Learning Strategies and Skill Learning

    Anderson's Active Control of Thought (ACT*) model is more wide-ranging, and practice leading to automatization plays a central role in it. Anderson suggests three kinds of memory: (a) a working memory; (b) a declarative long-term memory; (c) a procedural long-term memory. According to Anderson, the move from declarative to procedural knowledge takes place in three stages: 1. The cognitive stage — a description of the procedure is learnt; 2. The associative stage — a method for performing the skill is worked out; 3. The autonomous stage — the skill becomes more and more rapid and automatic. This model has been criticized for insisting that all knowledge starts out in declarative form, which is clearly problematic in the case of first language learners, as Anderson has accepted in answering the criticism. It is a general cognitive model of skill acquisition which can be applied to those aspects of SLL that require proceduralization and automatization.

  27. There are three stages in children in second language acquisition. Please explain the third stage.
    Topic: Learning Strategies and Skill Learning

    The third stage is the autonomous stage: the skill becomes more and more rapid and automatic. When the learner's actions become increasingly automatic after practicing any rule for some time, they become their second nature. (This is related to Anderson's ACT model of skill acquisition: cognitive stage → associative stage → autonomous stage.)

  28. How do Towell and Hawkins explain grammar knowledge in SLA?
    Topic: Learning Strategies and Skill Learning

    Towell and Hawkins reject the idea that Anderson's model can account for all aspects of SLL. They use models of natural language processing in order to explain how grammatical knowledge transforms into fluent performance in a second language. In order to explain why certain grammatical structures appear before others and why learners go through rigid stages in the acquisition of SL, they resort to the Universal Grammar approach. In order to understand how learners use this grammatical knowledge in increasingly efficient ways, Towell and Hawkins appeal to an information-processing account.

  29. How are learning strategies classified by O'Malley and Chamot?
    Topic: Learning Strategies and Skill Learning

    O'Malley and Chamot (1990) suggest that learning strategies are procedures undertaken by learners to make their language learning as effective as possible. Learning strategies must not be confused with communication strategies; they overlap, but there is a difference in focus: learning strategies facilitate learning, whereas communication strategies help in overcoming a specific communicative problem. Learning strategies can be classified into three categories: 1. Metacognitive strategies: selective attention, planning, monitoring, evaluation. 2. Cognitive strategies: rehearsal, organization, inferencing, summarizing, deducing, imagery, transfer, elaboration. 3. Social or affective strategies: co-operation, questioning for clarification, and self-talk.

  30. What is Processability Theory?
    Topic: Processing Theories and Connectionism

    Processability Theory claims that we need both a theory of grammar and a processing component to understand SLA. It focuses on the acquisition of the procedural skills required for processing the formal properties of second languages. Pienemann believes that language learning is the gradual acquisition of computational mechanisms. Limitations in processing skills in the early stages of learning prevent learners from attending to some aspects of SL. The processing challenge within this framework is that learners must learn to exchange grammatical information across elements of a sentence. It looks at the processing demands made by formal aspects of the second language — which structures will be easy to learn — and has direct implications for the learnability and teachability of second language structures.

  31. What is the Teachability Hypothesis?
    Topic: Processing Theories and Connectionism

    Pienemann's Processability Theory explains that SL learners follow a fairly rigid route while acquiring certain grammatical structures; structures only become 'learnable' when previous steps on this acquisitional path have been acquired. Learners can only operate within their hypothesis space, constrained by available processing resources. This led Pienemann to develop the 'teachability hypothesis', which considers the pedagogical implications of the learnability/processability model. It predicts: (1) stages of acquisition cannot be skipped through formal instruction; (2) instruction will be beneficial if it focuses on structures from 'the next stage'.

  32. What are the two approaches of cognitive theorists of SLA?
    Topic: Processing Theories and Connectionism

    a. Processing Theorists: Those who believe that language knowledge might be 'special' in some way, but they are concerned to develop transition or processing theories to complement property theories. Processing approaches investigate how second language learners process linguistic information, and how this ability develops over time. b. Perceptual Saliency Approach: This approach argues that human beings perceive and organize information in certain ways, and it is the perceptual saliency of linguistic information that drives the learning process forward, rather than an innate language-specific module. We find similarity in linguistic development across children and across languages because human beings are programmed to perceive and organize information in a certain way. Slobin (1979) has devised operating principles which guide children in their processing of the linguistic strings they encounter, based on the claim that certain linguistic forms are more 'accessible' or more 'salient' to children than others, e.g.: pay attention to the end of words; avoid exceptions; underlying semantic relations should be marked overtly and clearly.

  33. What does Connectionism say about language learning?
    Topic: Processing Theories and Connectionism

    Connectionism (parallel distributed processing) claims that learners are sensitive to regularities in the language input and extract probabilistic patterns on the basis of these regularities. Learning occurs as these patterns become strengthened or weakened through repeated activation or non-activation. It likens the brain to a computer consisting of neural networks — complex clusters of links between information nodes. Connectionism strikingly differs from other approaches, as it does not believe that the learning of rules underlies the construction of linguistic knowledge; rather, learning happens through an associative process. It is seen as an alternative to symbolic accounts of language acquisition — a transition theory that explains how associative patterns emerge in learners.

  34. Who were the connectionists? Give the advantages of connectionist models.
    Topic: Processing Theories and Connectionism

    Connectionists are primarily concerned with the individual; they do not see the learner as a social being. They are interested in the mind as a processor of information rather than in the specificity of the linguistic information it contains. The link they build up with neurology and neurobiology is also important. The advantages of these models over traditional symbolic models are: a. They are neurally inspired. b. They incorporate distributed representation and control of information. c. They are data-driven, with prototypical representations. d. They show graceful degradation, as do humans with language disorders. e. They are models of learning and acquisition rather than static description.

  35. What are the general benefits of applying cognitive theory to SLA?
    Topic: Processing Theories and Connectionism

    (1) Learning is an active and dynamic process in which individuals make use of a variety of information and strategic modes of processing. (2) Language is a complex cognitive skill in terms of how information is stored and learnt. (3) Learning a language entails a stage-wise progression from initial awareness, active manipulation of information and learning processes, to full automaticity in language use. (4) Learning strategies parallel theoretically derived cognitive processes and have the potential to influence learning outcomes in a positive manner.

  36. Why do processing theorists not say much about the language itself?
    Topic: Processing Theories and Connectionism

    Processing theorists are concerned with developing transition (processing) theories — with how learners process linguistic information and how this ability develops over time — rather than with describing the language system itself. The description of language knowledge is the concern of property theories (such as UG); processing theorists take that knowledge largely as given and focus on the mechanisms of processing, automatization and restructuring.

  37. Write a note on Functional Research in SLA.
    Topic: Functional Perspectives

    Functionalist research with a social orientation is interested in the relationship between the development of a child's formal language system and aspects of their social world. Some of this work examines children's speech acts and their relationship with lexical or grammatical choices. Other work looks more broadly at the social context in which children interact and the types of speech events they are engaged in, and seeks to link these influences to linguistic development. The functional approaches pay attention respectively to the relations between grammatical development and prototype events, between grammar, pragmatics and text organization, and between grammar and the social world. The functional tradition is well established in SLL theory: it claims that language development is driven by pragmatic communicative needs and that the formal resources of language are elaborated to express more complex patterns of meaning. Functionalist research takes the form of naturalistic case studies, mostly of adults in the early stages of SLL, and has offered rich accounts of both the rate and route of naturalistic SLL. Some studies adopted a 'patch' approach, while others proposed an SL 'basic variety' that represents a proto-grammar stage all learners should pass through.

  38. What is the functionalist view of the transition theory?
    Topic: Functional Perspectives

    A transition theory describes the process of language learning. UG is also a transition theory, as it is interested in describing the language transition process — the learning process that occurs during the learning of the second language. In that sense, it does answer the two main questions that Chomsky said a language theory must answer: it describes the knowledge of the language (property theory) and also moves on to explain how that knowledge is acquired by the learners (transition theory).

  39. What is Child-Directed Speech (CDS)? How can it facilitate language acquisition?
    Topic: Input, Output and Interaction

    Adults commonly use 'special' speech styles when talking with young children; this is called 'baby talk' or child-directed speech (CDS). Interest in CDS was triggered in the 1960s. Interactionists such as Gallaway and Richards (1994) have pointed out the ways in which CDS might facilitate language acquisition: it enables the child to manage attention on specific linguistic items; it facilitates segmentation; it provides feedback and correct models; it reduces the child's processing load; it encourages the child into interaction and to be part of the conversation; and it explicitly teaches social routines. However, CDS has been criticized by UG theorists — Chomsky described the notion that aspects of first language acquisition could be related to the input as 'absurd'.

  40. Why do SLA learners mostly remain noticeably different from native speakers? (Foreigner Talk)
    Topic: Input, Output and Interaction

    Various studies during the 1970s and 1980s demonstrated that the talk addressed to learners was typically grammatically regular but somewhat simplified linguistically. The degree of simplification reported in many descriptive studies was puzzlingly variable. Also, these studies stopped short at the description of the distinctive features of 'Foreigner Talk Discourse'. Because the input addressed to learners is simplified and modified, and because learners' interlanguage systems may fossilize, SLA learners mostly remain noticeably different from native speakers.

  41. Discuss Input, Output and Interaction hypotheses and the phases of research on them.
    Topic: Input, Output and Interaction

    The Input, Output, and Interaction hypotheses have led to very active strands of empirical research. Long proposed his Interaction Hypothesis as an extension of Krashen's original Input Hypothesis, linking features of 'environmental' language with learners' second language development; Swain's Output Hypothesis argues that SL production (output) forces learners to undertake complete grammatical processing. The first phase of research was inclined toward documenting the phenomenon of meaning negotiation. The second phase developed in the following ways: (a) relating environmental factors in language learning to linguistic theory, particularly to Universal Grammar; (b) interest in the significance of negative evidence; (c) increased attention to information processing theory and the complications involved in the conversion of environmental language firstly into input and subsequently into intake. Achievements of this tradition: it has been shown that native speaker and non-native speaker interlocutors can and will work actively to achieve mutual understanding; negotiations involve both linguistic and interactional modifications, which together offer opportunities to 'notice' aspects of target language form, whether from positive or negative evidence.

  42. Which hypotheses took inspiration from Krashen's proposals?
    Topic: Input, Output and Interaction

    Two hypotheses took inspiration from Krashen's proposals: 1. The Interaction Hypothesis (Long) — an extension of Krashen's Input Hypothesis, linking environmental language and negotiation of meaning with SLA. 2. The Output Hypothesis (Swain) — arguing that comprehensible input alone is not sufficient and that producing language (output) plays a central role in acquisition.

  43. Write a note on negative feedback and recast in SL classrooms.
    Topic: Feedback, Negative Evidence and Output

    Observational studies examined the occurrence and apparent effects of negative feedback in SL classrooms. For instance, Lyster and colleagues (1997) noted that recasts were the most common type of feedback. However, recasts were much less likely to lead to immediate self-correction by the students. A further analysis reported that feedback provided by teachers varied according to the type of error made: teachers were more likely to respond to lexical errors with negotiation (e.g. clarification requests), whereas they responded to grammatical and phonological errors with recasts. Recasts were seen as an effective strategy in the case of phonological errors; however, recasting was much less effective for the repair of grammatical mistakes. The study suggested that more interactive feedback modes would be more effective in pushing learners to amend their hypotheses about SL grammar as well as vocabulary.

  44. What is Negative Evidence?
    Topic: Feedback, Negative Evidence and Output

    Negative evidence means any kind of input that informs the learner about unacceptable forms in the target language. It includes formal correction by the teacher and informal paraphrasing by a native-speaking conversational partner. However, corrections often seem ineffective; therefore, recent theorists put more emphasis on the provision of positive evidence. From a psycholinguistic view, interaction is also interesting because of the controversies over the usefulness of negative evidence for second language development.

  45. What are Swain's three functions of learner output?
    Topic: Feedback, Negative Evidence and Output

    Swain (1985, 1995) goes beyond the 'practice' function and proposes three further functions for learner output: (1) the noticing/triggering function (consciousness-raising role) — producing language pushes learners to become aware of gaps and problems in their current SL system; (2) the hypothesis-testing function — learners test their hypotheses about the target language through production; (3) the metalinguistic function (reflective role) — learners reflect on language form. Swain argued that students often succeed in comprehending SL text while only partly processing it, whereas SL production (output) really forces learners to undertake complete grammatical processing.

  46. What is the role of intake? Discuss Input Processing Theory.
    Topic: Input Processing and Autonomous Induction

    In Input Processing Theory: (1) linguistic data is actually processed from the input; (2) it is held in working memory for further processing — the portion of input that is processed and held is the intake; (3) input processing theory does not offer a complete model of successful processing of input; (4) it offers a set of principles; (5) it explains the apparent failures of learners to process the input. Its principles include: Principle No. 1 — learners have a preference for semantic processing over morphological processing (e.g. in 'We travelled by train yesterday', learners attend to meaning-bearing words); Principle No. 2 — learners have a preference for beginning and ending words. The theory is primarily focused on explaining the shortcuts and restricted processing strategies which learners seem to use.

  47. What is the Autonomous Induction Theory?
    Topic: Input Processing and Autonomous Induction

    Autonomous Induction Theory, by Suzanne Carroll (2000), offers a much more complete and ambitious model of input processing. According to Carroll, understanding SLA processes requires an adequate theory of the following phenomena: (1) representation of language in the mind; (2) how the language is processed, both receptively and productively; (3) how our mental representations of language can be changed to process the environmental language we encounter. Carroll rejects parameter (re)setting as a totally inadequate metaphor. Instead, she proposes inductive learning (i-learning) — learning by generalization from examples. She challenges the claim that increased comprehension can lead to identification and acquisition in a sequential manner; according to her, it is logically impossible unless enough formal analysis is done so that elements are identified in the speech stimulus. Carroll's model is complex and has been criticized for its neglect of the detail of language processing which converts language stimuli into interpretable input.

  48. Elaborate the term 'noticing' in attention theory.
    Topic: Input Processing and Autonomous Induction

    Researchers have developed the idea that the amount of attention may influence the extent to which SL input and interaction actually produce SL intake. Schmidt (1990, 1994, 2001) has been most influential in promoting this view. He distinguishes among different types of attention: he uses the term 'noticing' to refer to the process of bringing some stimulus into focal attention and registering its simple occurrence, whether voluntarily or involuntarily. He reserves the terms 'understanding' and 'awareness' for explicit knowledge — awareness of a rule or generalization. Schmidt argued that noticing is the necessary and sufficient condition for the conversion of input to intake for learning; later, he modified this view to the claim that more noticing leads to more learning.

  49. Who were Vygotsky's leading supporters?
    Topic: Interactionist Approach and Sociocultural Theory

    The leading supporters of the Soviet theorist Lev Semeonovich Vygotsky were: 1. Jerome Bruner; 2. James Wertsch; 3. Barbara Rogoff; 4. James Lantolf; 5. Mercer; 6. Wells.

  50. What are the socio-cultural perspectives (Vygotsky) on second language learning?
    Topic: Interactionist Approach and Sociocultural Theory

    The theorists who advocate this perspective view language learning in essentially social terms. They claim that target language interaction cannot be viewed simply as a source of 'input' for autonomous and internal learning mechanisms; rather, it plays a more central role in learning. This theory was originally presented by the Soviet developmental psychologist Vygotsky, who argued that humans do not act directly on the physical world but rely on tools and labour activity, and use symbolic tools or signs to mediate and regulate their relationships with others. The theory proposes that: language learning takes place in a social context; interaction is central to learning; interaction itself constitutes the learning process; and learning is also a mediated process.

  51. What is Interlanguage?
    Topic: Core Concepts

    An interlanguage is an idiolect that has been developed by a learner of a second language (L2) which preserves some features of their first language (L1), and can also overgeneralize some L2 writing and speaking rules. These two characteristics of an interlanguage result in the system's unique linguistic organization. Learner's language system is unstable and characterized by a high degree of variability, which is a central feature of learner interlanguage.

  52. What is the difference between forms and functions?
    Topic: Core Concepts

    Form and function are two different things. The form is related to the structure — the architecture of something — whereas a function is the product of a structure which plays a specific role. For example, a building has a form: bricks, wood, a map and a design; that is the form or structure of the building. The complete, well-furnished building serves the purpose of living — that is the 'function' of the building.

  53. What are the three main points of view of SLL researchers regarding the learner?
    Topic: Core Concepts

    There are three main points of view among SLL researchers as far as the learner is concerned. (1) The linguistic perspective is concerned with modelling language structures and processes within the mind. (2) The social psychological perspective is concerned with modelling individual differences among learners and their implications for eventual learning success. (3) The sociocultural perspective views learners as social beings and members of social groups and networks. The first two are primarily concerned with analyzing and modelling the inner mental mechanisms available to the individual learner; they aim to document and explain the developmental routes but are less concerned with the speed or rate of development, and they minimize or disregard social and contextual differences.
