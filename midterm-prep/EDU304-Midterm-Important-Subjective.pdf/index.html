  1. Define guidance and counselling. How is counselling a part of guidance?
    Topic: Foundations of Guidance and Counselling

    Guidance is a continuous process of advisement that assists individuals to understand and accept themselves, live effectively in society, set realistic goals and carry out adequate plans to achieve those goals. In the educational context, Skinner defines guidance as a process of helping a young person learn to adjust to self, to others and to circumstances, while Ruth Strang defines it as helping an individual, through his/her own efforts, to discover and develop potentialities for personal happiness and social usefulness. Counselling is a more specialized service offered to an individual who is undergoing a problem and needs professional help to overcome it; it involves active listening and empathetic understanding and brings about change through self-knowledge. Guidance and counselling are not synonymous: counselling is a part of guidance. Guidance is the broader process of helping individuals choose from a variety of available options and make wise decisions, while counselling is the specialized, deeper part of that process used when a person faces a serious problem.

  2. Differentiate between guidance and counselling.
    Topic: Foundations of Guidance and Counselling

    Guidance and counselling differ in several ways. (1) Focus: guidance focuses on helping individuals choose what they value most, while counselling focuses on helping them make changes. (2) Timing: guidance is put in place at the time a choice is to be made; counselling helps in considering all sides of a potential choice even before it is made, and also takes place when a choice has been made and there is a need to modify, reinforce or abandon it. (3) Nature of service: guidance is a pre-problem service that aims to improve the adjustment of individuals, while counselling is a post-problem service that addresses an existing problem. (4) Decision making: guidance is an act of making decisions for another person to help them set or achieve their goals, while counselling assists a person in finding the correct answer or behaviour themselves. (5) Raw material: intellectual attitudes are the raw material of guidance, whereas emotional attitudes are the raw material of counselling. Both, however, aim at helping people in personal and professional development and social adjustment.

  3. Discuss the purpose of guidance and counselling in schools.
    Topic: Foundations of Guidance and Counselling

    Guidance and counselling serve the following purposes in schools: (1) They provide required information and assistance to students. (2) They bring the teacher and the student closer together, which is important for the school and especially for the student. (3) They facilitate wise decisions and choices. (4) They develop better self-understanding in students. (5) They facilitate adjustment with one's surroundings. (6) They help avoid bullying in school, so that students do not become victims or bullies. (7) They help in the adaptation process. (8) They develop independence and confidence. (9) They improve the productivity and capabilities of students. (10) They groom an individual to become a successful person who works as an effective member of society and achieves his/her goals.

  4. Explain the significance of guidance and counselling for students.
    Topic: Foundations of Guidance and Counselling

    Guidance and counselling are significant because they cultivate essential life skills along with academic skills, so that students can merge well in society. They empower students to plan and manage their own future through better self-awareness, and they help students progress in academics and in every field of life. By sorting out school problems, they reduce dropouts. They reduce or eliminate anti-social behaviours such as bullying, racism, discrimination, prejudice, conflict and fights, using conflict management strategies. They facilitate personal growth and positive change through self-understanding, which improves students' manners and etiquettes, healthy relationships, personal appearance, hygiene and confidence. They assist students to become psychologically mature and realize their potential. Finally, they enhance career and job prospects by creating awareness about career and planning choices and by cultivating appropriate work attitudes.

  5. Why are guidance and counselling needed in academic settings?
    Topic: Foundations of Guidance and Counselling

    Guidance and counselling are needed in academic settings when students require making choices of courses and when they need assistance in turmoil and confusion. They develop readiness for choices and for facing new challenges, make up the deficiencies of home, and seek solutions for diverse learners. They check migration in order to prevent brain drain and minimize the incidence of indiscipline. Students need an intelligent listener who understands their difficulties and provides good suggestions for their working plan, so teachers need to be well grounded in social and emotional learning in order to listen appropriately. Guidance and counselling also ensure proper utilization of time spent outside the classroom, help students in vocational development, and help them gain insight into the origins and development of emotional difficulties.

  6. What are the benefits of counselling for students?
    Topic: Foundations of Guidance and Counselling

    Counselling benefits students in many ways. Students feel less anxiety and regain emotional balance, because counselling brings stress relief. They develop greater self-confidence and increased assertiveness. They build better relationships with others and develop decision-making skills. Counselling assists in resolving conflicts and relieves the discomfort caused by conflicts. It enables students to set boundaries and helps in trauma resolution. Overall, counselling helps students achieve optimum achievement and adequate adjustment in varied life situations.

  7. Discuss the areas and problems addressed by guidance and counselling.
    Topic: Foundations of Guidance and Counselling

    Guidance and counselling address a wide range of student problems. They address special educational needs, including students who are extremely defiant in subject areas (for whom remedial programmes are developed) and students with physical disabilities, whose disability is overcome through education rather than treated as an educational disability. They serve gifted students who are exceptionally talented and bright. They deal with anxiety, mental health, learning and hearing problems, depression caused by domestic problems, family break-up problems, and students victimized by a major accident or incident. Specific areas include the egocentrism problem, disability and bereavement, violence and abuse, disruptive behaviour, developmental problems, socio-economic problems, problems of vocational choice, and nervousness. The best way to deal with these problems is for the teacher to talk to the students facing them.

  8. Explain the principles of continuity, assistance and support in guidance and counselling.
    Topic: Principles of Guidance and Counselling

    Continuity means that guidance is not a one-off event; rather, it is a continuous process of assisting an individual. It develops a permanent relationship with students in which we not only understand their problems but also solve them. Assistance refers to helping individuals in the developmental procedures: it develops problem-solving skills in students, enhances the self-discovery process, and provides assistance in the developmental process rather than giving direction. Support means that the teacher and counsellor must be ready and available all the time in the school for the guidance and counselling of students, so that students can approach them whenever a problem arises.

  9. Discuss the principles of cooperation and flexibility, and of self-awareness and dignity of individuals.
    Topic: Principles of Guidance and Counselling

    Cooperation and flexibility mean that guidance and counselling 'apply to all': they cater to everyone regardless of age, gender, ethnicity, religion and socio-economic status, so students must never be labelled in schools. Guidance and counselling require cooperation among all persons, who need to be on the same platform while dealing with students, and the programme must be flexible and tailored to individual and community needs. Self-awareness and dignity of individuals mean that students discover themselves: they analyze their strengths, abilities and capabilities and reflect on them, because reflection is very important in the educational process. Teachers and parents should respect the dignity of individuals, since a person's attitude and personal perception are the basis of his acts — thoughts create feelings, feelings create behaviour, and behaviour reinforces thoughts.

  10. Why are goal-directedness and confidentiality essential principles of guidance and counselling?
    Topic: Principles of Guidance and Counselling

    Guidance and counselling are always goal-directed activities: the guide directs the child towards a proper goal, for example by constructing a remedial programme for a student who is weak in a language so that the student reaches the proper goal, and by giving the student a guidance and counselling orientation. Confidentiality is equally essential: a guide and counsellor should not openly explain the domestic problems of students to others. Maintaining the student's privacy is the key aspect of developing trust between the counselee and the counsellor, and without this trust students will not share their real problems.

  11. What is empathy? Differentiate between primary empathy and advanced empathy.
    Topic: Principles of Guidance and Counselling

    Empathy refers to the power of understanding and imaginatively entering into another person's feelings. There are two levels of empathy. Primary empathy means responding in such a way that exhibits that the counsellor has understood the counselee's major theme. Advanced empathy means exploring themes, issues, meanings and emotions that are below the surface of what is being shared by the counselee. Thus, primary empathy works at the surface level of what the student explicitly shares, while advanced empathy goes deeper into the hidden feelings and meanings behind it.

  12. Explain educational guidance and vocational guidance.
    Topic: Types of Guidance

    Educational guidance provides assistance to students in their choices and adjustment to the school's curriculum, adjustment to school life, and pursuing the right type of education in which the right balance is met for accommodating the human resource needs of a nation. It is related to all aspects of the curriculum, methods of instruction, discipline and co-curricular activities: the counsellor gives directions about curriculum content and methods of instruction, guides in discipline matters, and guides students in choosing their co-curricular activities. Vocational guidance is a process of helping students to choose an occupation, preparing for entering into it, and making progress in the vocation of choice. Its primary focus is helping students make right decisions, plan their future and build their careers.

  13. Write notes on avocational guidance, health guidance and moral guidance.
    Topic: Types of Guidance

    Avocational guidance refers to giving proper guidance to a person so that he/she spends leisure time in a positive manner and judiciously utilizes it. It caters to students facing lack of interest in, or over-enthusiasm for, sports, music and art, inability to engage in sports due to health issues, and limited or excessive means; it is especially important where there is lack of interest in reading and handicrafts, and it brings a positive balance between academic and non-academic issues. Health guidance means care of the total health of students; its goal is preventive and curative. Preventive measures include keeping hostels and canteens hygienically neat and clean, while curative guidance provides awareness about nutrition, body fitness, physical exercises and prevention of pollution. It is a cooperative effort of the principal, doctor, counsellor/psychologist, teacher, student and parents. Moral guidance helps in the all-round development of students, making them strong academically, morally and socially, and provides a base platform for their social and cultural development.

  14. Differentiate between directive and non-directive counselling.
    Topic: Approaches of Counselling

    Directive counselling is counsellor-centred: the counsellor directs the student to take steps in order to resolve conflicts, telling him/her what the problem is and what its solution is, and what is right and wrong. It gives more importance to the intellectual aspect than to emotional aspects, it is less time consuming, and it is best for less mature and less intelligent students who cannot analyze their own decisions; however, in it the student's autonomy becomes weak and personal autonomy and integrity are not respected. Non-directive (permissive) counselling is student-focused: it brings a lot of autonomy to the student to discover his/her own problems, their causes, weaknesses and strengths, while the counsellor's main function is to facilitate the student in working out the problem. Emotional elements rather than intellectual elements are stressed, the student's confidence level becomes high, but it is time consuming because the student solves problems through his/her own understanding, which may take days or months.

  15. Describe the steps of directive counselling.
    Topic: Approaches of Counselling

    There are six steps in directive counselling. (a) Analysis: finding out the problem. (b) Synthesis: collective thought about the information gathered. (c) Diagnosis: finding out the solution of the problem. (d) Prognosis: predicting the development of the problem. (e) Counselling: giving directions to the student. (f) Follow up: it is extremely important to follow up the process to see whether the directions are working and the problem is being resolved.

  16. What is eclectic counselling? State its steps.
    Topic: Approaches of Counselling

    Eclectic counselling takes into account both directive and non-directive guidance and counselling; it is a combination of the first two types. The role of the counsellor is very flexible: he/she is neither too active nor too passive, but follows the middle course, and does not play a commanding or passive position. The counsellor may be a teacher or a specialized helper, and the role can change from directive to non-directive as the situation requires, making it a highly flexible and balanced method. Its steps are: (a) establishing rapport, (b) problem diagnosis, (c) analysis, (d) tentative planning, (e) counselling, and (f) follow up.

  17. Write notes on crisis counselling, preventive counselling and facilitative counselling.
    Topic: Approaches of Counselling

    Crisis counselling focuses on a single or recurrent problem that is overwhelming or traumatic. It not only educates students but also guides them and provides the required support to solve their crisis; the crisis counsellor helps the student face and move past distressing and traumatic life events, which may even be a simple emotional problem. Preventive counselling is an attempt to reduce emotional and physical distress through prevention, i.e., by acting before problems become serious. Facilitative counselling helps students to correct their mistakes, solve problems and plan for their future; its primary concern is to help the client deal with the negative feelings that arise during the implementation of plans.

  18. Explain the client-centred (Rogerian) theory of counselling.
    Topic: Theories of Guidance and Counselling

    Client-centred theory, also known as non-directive or Rogerian theory, places much responsibility for the treatment process on the client/student, while the counsellor takes a non-directive, supportive role. Its primary goals are to increase self-esteem, develop greater openness to new experiences, foster a closer agreement between the client's idealized and actual selves, and lower levels of defensiveness, guilt and insecurity. Its techniques are listening, accepting, respecting, understanding and responding. Its three major components are: (1) Congruence — the counsellor's openness and genuineness and his/her willingness to relate to students without hiding behind a professional facade. (2) Unconditional positive regard — the counsellor accepts the student totally without disapproving of particular feelings, actions or characteristics. (3) Empathy — showing emotional understanding of, and sensitivity to, the student's feelings throughout the counselling session. The counsellor's role is to be an instrument for change, and to be present and accessible. This theory helps in self-exploring, self-acceptance and self-change.

  19. Discuss the holistic health (bio-psychosocial) theory.
    Topic: Theories of Guidance and Counselling

    The holistic health or bio-psychosocial theory is an important theory to implement in schools. It holds that there are three factors in human development — biological, social and psychological — and that psychological and social factors play as significant a role as biological factors in human functioning; therefore, health must be understood in the combination of all three. The theory links science with humanism and involves the environmental factors that affect human personality and behaviour. Its principles are that the counsellor should use self-awareness as a diagnostic and therapeutic tool, develop empathy (social and emotional learning develops empathy, and the counsellor is very sensitive towards the feelings of others), cultivate mutual trust between counsellor and counselee, and practise self-calibration to reduce bias by consciously learning and objectively monitoring habits to maintain the client's centrality. The theory is criticized because being eclectic in approach can result in one enacting one's own dogmas, and because it does not guide how to prioritize the biological, psychological and social factors.

  20. Explain the solution focused theory: its core concepts and interventions.
    Topic: Theories of Guidance and Counselling

    Solution focused theory is a client-centred approach that focuses on the individuality of the client or student; the idea that 'one size fits all' does not apply, because every person has individual needs and problems. It employs therapies that tend to be hopeful and solution oriented. Its core concepts are: it is a postmodern approach that focuses on the future rather than examining the past; there are no fixed truths or realities — the only reality is what the client/student presents; the counsellor takes a 'not-knowing' stance, showing that he/she does not know anything about the student, who tells everything in detail; the client/student is basically the expert, not the counsellor; the client/student alone determines the goals of counselling, not the therapist; change takes place throughout and fosters change; and the counsellor should avoid analyzing the problem. Its interventions include not knowing, complementing strength, exception questions and coping questions. It is criticized for lacking empirical research support and for being inadequate for clients with serious problems.

  21. Discuss the existential theory and its basic principles.
    Topic: Theories of Guidance and Counselling

    Existential theory is a humanistic approach that focuses on the issues central to human existence. Its core belief is that people are essentially good in nature and growth oriented, although a person who deviates from this basic nature may move towards destructive acts and face problems. Human growth depends upon environmental, socio-economic and social-emotional factors, and developing trust, respect and care helps a person regain emotional and spiritual equilibrium. Many choices are available, and people can choose what they become — as Jean Paul Sartre said, 'Man is nothing else but he makes of himself.' Its basic principles are: (1) Self-awareness — we are finite with limited time, and we have the potential to make choices and reflect on them. (2) Isolation — we were born alone and will die alone, so we must give a sense of meaning to life. (3) Freedom and responsibilities — we are free to choose among alternatives and are responsible for our lives, actions and their consequences. (4) Identity — trusting ourselves, discovering ourselves and building relationships with others. (5) Objectivity — searching for meaning and purpose in life, since mindlessness leads to hollowness.

  22. Explain the cognitive behavioural theory (ABC model), including its cognitive and behavioural methods.
    Topic: Theories of Guidance and Counselling

    Cognitive behavioural theory is based on the principle that actions are driven by thoughts: we do those things which are in our mind. Personality includes both genetic endowment and social influence; our perception, interpretations and assumptions shape our behaviour and emotions; maladjustment is a result of inaccurate cognition; and increasing the repertoire of adaptive behaviour gives freedom. Its basic structure has two methods. The cognitive method includes explaining the problem, identifying and challenging negative thoughts, and cognitive restructuring. The behavioural method includes relaxation (imagery, breathing exercises, progressive muscle relaxation), distraction through object focusing, distraction through sensory awareness, monitoring activities and scheduling activities. Its strategies include cognitive modeling (manifesting the student's behaviour), thought stopping (stopping negative thoughts through relaxation and exercises) and cognitive restructuring (re-modelling the student's behaviour). The theory is criticized because it requires a lot of training and skill and does not consider the complexity of lives.

  23. What is the strength based theory? State its key rules.
    Topic: Theories of Guidance and Counselling

    Strength based theory, also known as the 'positive thinking' and 'learned optimism' theory, deals with the positive elements of human beings and emphasizes positivity. Here the 'problem' is not the focal point; instead, 'What can I learn from this?' is the central question. A negative situation is not a complete defeat; it is a learning lesson, a temporary setback. Its key rules are: the counsellor should be as concerned with strength as with weakness; he/she is interested in building the best things in life as in repairing the worst; and the theory is concerned with making people's lives fulfilling as much as with healing them, so the counsellor and teacher must give hope to students, because negativity lowers self-esteem. For example, if a student is weak in mathematics but good in language, the teacher should ignore the weakness and encourage the strength, because constructive and hopeful people fare better in almost all aspects of life. The theory builds rapport between counsellor and counselee. It is criticized because self-help does not adequately recognize the structural inequalities in society that can impinge on personal and social development.

  24. What is affective education? Discuss its dimensions and elements.
    Topic: Affective Education and Social-Emotional Learning

    Affective education is the way to deal with feelings and values, in which emotion is a central element; it also deals with appreciation, enthusiasm, motivations and attitudes. It has three main dimensions: feelings, emotions and self-esteem. As Goleman (1996) states, emotions are central to the development of morality and learning. The elements of affective education are: (a) Interpersonal relations — the social associations, connections or affiliations between two or more people; students should communicate and work with other students, for which group and peer teaching should be applied. (b) Humanitarianism — a moral of kindness, benevolence and sympathy extended to all human beings. (c) Curiosity and risk-taking — curiosity is a strong desire to know or learn something, the fuel of learning, development and adaptation throughout the lifespan, and making students risk-takers develops critical thinking in them. (d) Complexity and imagination — understanding complex human behaviour helps students grow into powerful members of society, and affective education also develops leadership skills. (e) Character — character building is extremely important, because schools are mini societies that give students the opportunity to learn behaviour.

  25. Why is affective education neglected in schools? Give reasons.
    Topic: Affective Education and Social-Emotional Learning

    Affective education is neglected in schools for several reasons. First, there is a traditional lack of concern in education for the affective domain — the concept is 'just not there', as schools consider their responsibility to be only academic education so that students pass their classes. Second, there is a misconception that emotions are to be dealt with at home rather than in school. Third, there is a fear of indoctrination — of imposing things on students. Fourth, there is a misperception that if the school meets the child's cognitive needs, affective development will automatically follow. In addition, there is a lack of reliable and valid tools for assessing affective functioning, a lack of clarity as to the optimal level of affective functioning to be attained, and a belief that healthy emotional development among students is automatic. The result is that when the curriculum focuses only on cognitive development, the other domains develop unevenly and students suffer emotionally, showing anxiety, depression, poor self-image and anti-social behaviour.

  26. Discuss empathy and pro-social behaviour as vital components of affective education.
    Topic: Affective Education and Social-Emotional Learning

    Empathy is a vital component of affective education. Empathy may be expressed as putting oneself in the shoes of another person. It differs from sympathy: in sympathy you feel sorry for someone, whereas in empathy you take a step ahead and seriously work towards resolving the conflict and problems. Empathy must be modeled for students, and daily interactions with them in school may be more critical than the specifics of curricula designed to teach it, because empathy is a part of school culture. It plays a crucial role in developing kindness and peaceful conflict resolution and in decreasing disruptive classroom behaviour. Pro-social behaviours are attributes and voluntary actions to help others; they involve feelings of cooperation and help in peer interactions. Pro-social skills are the abilities to interact with peers and adults, accommodate and cooperate with others, praise and help others, actively listen, and display socially acceptable behaviours — cooperating with others, participating in classroom discussions and activities, following classroom rules, sharing with others and treating others with kindness. Both empathy and pro-social behaviour can be safely developed by integrating these skills in the classroom.

  27. What is Social and Emotional Learning (SEL)? Discuss its need and benefits.
    Topic: Affective Education and Social-Emotional Learning

    Social and Emotional Learning (SEL) is a process of integrating thinking, feeling and behaviour through which a person becomes aware of the self and others. It helps in personality development, makes us make responsible decisions, and regulates human behaviour. SEL skills are referred to as 'soft' skills and enable students to reach their full potential. SEL is needed to help students make responsible decisions and regulate their behaviour, to foster caring relationships between teachers and students, to ensure cooperation among all stakeholders, to reduce conflict among students, and to create a greater sense of school safety. Its benefits include increased academic success, improved quality of relationships between teachers and students, decreased problem behaviour, and an improved learning environment. SEL needs to be taken care of at every level of schooling and systematically integrated into the curriculum, lessons and school procedures.

  28. Explain the five components of SEL: self-awareness, self-management, social awareness, relationship management and responsible decision making.
    Topic: Affective Education and Social-Emotional Learning

    (1) Self-awareness: SEL builds self-awareness by encouraging students to accurately assess their feelings, interests and values/strengths, and to maintain a well-grounded sense of self-confidence. (2) Self-management: it regulates one's emotions to handle stress (classroom, examination and group-work stresses) and to control impulses, and it helps in overcoming obstacles and in setting and monitoring progress towards goals. (3) Social awareness: it helps in taking the perspective of and empathizing with others, recognizing and appreciating individual and group similarities and differences, and recognizing and using family, school and community resources. (4) Relationship management: SEL helps in establishing and maintaining healthy and rewarding cooperative relationships, resisting inappropriate social pressure, preventing and resolving interpersonal conflicts, managing situations, and seeking help when needed. (5) Responsible decision making: SEL helps in making decisions based on consideration of ethical standards, realizing the consequences of various actions, applying decision-making skills to academic and social situations, and contributing to the well-being of one's school and community.

  29. Define character and moral development. Discuss the factors that influence them.
    Topic: Moral, Character and Self Development

    Character is a set of emotional, cognitive and behavioural patterns learned from experience; it continues to evolve throughout life and is dependent on one's moral development. Moral development is the development of proper attitudes and behaviours towards others, based on social and cultural norms, rules and laws. Two factors influence moral and character development. (1) Temperament (genetic factors, nature): human beings differ in temperament — some students are cool minded, some hot headed, some compromising, some dominant, some shy and some cooperative — and researchers estimate that around 50% of traits may be heritable. Genes do not determine character, but they control the development of the nervous system, which in turn controls behaviour. (2) Environment: environment plays a significant role in character building, and this factor is controllable — parents and teachers can modify and improvise certain practices for better character development.

  30. Discuss the components of character.
    Topic: Moral, Character and Self Development

    The components of character include the following. (1) Compassion and empathy: elements of care and taking care of other people's feelings; one must be considerate to others' feelings, and a teacher can teach compassion and empathy through demonstration and modeling. (2) Fairness: taking turns, sharing, and listening to what others have to say; being open-minded and following the rules. (3) Ethical behaviour: characterized by honesty, fairness and equity in interpersonal, professional and academic relationships and in research and scholarly activities; it respects the dignity, diversity and rights of individuals and groups, and requires a person to be caring, kind, tolerant and grateful. (4) Integrity: the quality of being honest and having strong moral principles (moral uprightness); it is a personal choice to uphold oneself to consistent moral and ethical standards, and it involves moral self-efficacy, honesty, truthfulness, reliability and loyalty.

  31. Define self-esteem and self-efficacy. How do they differ from each other?
    Topic: Moral, Character and Self Development

    Self-esteem is a person's overall sense of self-worth or personal value — what our unconscious believes to be true about how worthy, lovable, valuable and capable we are. It has two dimensions: competence (efficacy-based self-esteem — to what degree people see themselves as capable and efficacious) and worth (worth-based self-esteem — to what degree individuals feel they are persons of value). Self-efficacy, as defined by Bandura, refers to an individual's belief in his or her capacity to execute behaviours necessary to produce specific performance attainments; it reflects confidence in the ability to exert control over one's own motivation, behaviour and social environment. The difference is that self-esteem is a general evaluation of one's worth as a person, while self-efficacy is a task-specific belief about one's capability to perform particular behaviours successfully. A weak sense of self-efficacy leads to avoidance of life challenges, focus on failures and low confidence, while a strong sense of self-efficacy encourages taking challenges, develops interest and strong commitment, and enables quick recovery from setbacks.

  32. Discuss Bandura's four sources of acquiring self-efficacy.
    Topic: Moral, Character and Self Development

    According to Bandura (1997), there are four ways of learning and acquiring self-efficacy expectations. (1) Performance accomplishments: gaining relevant experience to achieve success; success boosts one's self-efficacy, while students with low self-efficacy expectations are more apt to give up at the first sign of difficulty. (2) Vicarious learning (vicarious modeling): beliefs are acquired through observation and interpretation — by observing others doing the same or similar task, the learner gets encouragement. (3) Verbal persuasion: beliefs about the self are influenced by others' opinions; encouragement supports self-efficacy while criticism hampers it, and friends, family and teachers influence one's educational and vocational interests, choices and decisions. (4) Physical/affective status: stress and anxiety have a negative effect on self-efficacy as well as on learning, because the brain functions best in a supportive environment rather than under perceived threats.

  33. Discuss the role of the teacher as a counsellor and guide.
    Topic: The Teacher as Counsellor and Guide

    The teacher has a central role as counsellor and guide. According to Stronge (2007), the 'teacher as a person' must possess seven indicators of effectiveness, including caring (listening, gentleness, understanding, knowing students as individuals, warmth and encouragement), fairness and respect (enforcing rules without favouritism, honesty, following through on commitments, not embarrassing students), interaction with students (developing social relationships and being available, which enhances students' self-esteem and sense of belongingness), enthusiasm, motivation (only a motivated teacher can motivate students to learn), a positive attitude towards teaching, and reflective practice (careful review of one's own teaching process). As a guide, the teacher helps children cope with separation anxiety in the early years by creating a comfortable and friendly environment; provides academic guidance by knowing where the student lacks and what remedy is required; builds students' confidence through open discussion, peer review, group assignments and constructive feedback, along with constant motivation, encouragement of co-curricular activities, listening to students' issues and respecting their confidentiality; and manages behaviour by empathetically understanding problems, knowing students as individuals, identifying the triggers of problems, and setting and effectively implementing rules, while providing positive attention, a sense of protection and justice.
