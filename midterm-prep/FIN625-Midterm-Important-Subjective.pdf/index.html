  1. Define credit. What are the two main types of credit?
    Topic: Core Subjective Topics

    As a financial term, credit refers to the granting of a loan and the creation of debt. It is a privilege granted for the purpose of extending time to make payment on a debt, i.e. borrowed money or other finance (such as hire purchase) to be paid back under an arrangement with a lender. Any movement of financial capital is normally quite dependent on credit, which in turn is dependent on the reputation or creditworthiness of the entity which takes responsibility for the funds. Unlike money (in the strict definition), credit cannot itself act as a unit of account. The two main types of credit are: (1) credit linked to a specific item or items, e.g. home loans (mortgages) and personal or shop loans taken for a particular purchase such as a house or a new kitchen; and (2) revolving credit on payment cards, which gives access to a fixed amount of money (the credit limit) that can be spent as the borrower wishes in a wide range of outlets; every repayment restores that proportion of the limit for further spending.

  2. Differentiate between simple interest and compound interest, with the help of an example.
    Topic: Core Subjective Topics

    Interest is charged by lenders to cover the lending risk and to make a profit on their money. Under simple interest, interest is charged only on the original principal: for example, if you borrow Rs 100 at an annual rate of ten per cent, the total cost after one year is Rs 110. Simple interest is rarely charged on borrowings. Under compound interest, interest is charged on the accumulated amount, i.e. on the interest as well, at regular intervals (a day, a week, a month, and so on). For example, if you owe Rs 100 at ten per cent compound interest per year, at the end of year one you owe Rs 110; in year two the lender charges ten per cent of Rs 110, so you owe Rs 121, and so on. With fixed repayment loans, the interest is worked out in advance and added into the repayments, whereas with revolving credit the borrower can repay as much or as little as he wants at any point.

  3. What is a credit score? How do lenders use credit scores?
    Topic: Core Subjective Topics

    A credit score is a numerical expression based on a statistical analysis of a person's credit files, to represent the creditworthiness of that person — the perceived likelihood that the person will pay debts in a timely manner. A credit score is primarily based on credit report information typically sourced from credit bureaus / credit reference agencies. Lenders such as banks and credit card companies use credit scores to evaluate the potential risk posed by lending money to consumers and to mitigate losses due to bad debt. Specifically, lenders use credit scores to determine who qualifies for a loan, at what interest rate, and what credit limits should apply. Credit scoring is not limited to banks: mobile phone companies, insurance companies, employers and government departments employ the same techniques, and the use of credit or identity scoring prior to authorizing access or granting credit is an implementation of a trusted system.

  4. What is an identity score? What data components can it include?
    Topic: Core Subjective Topics

    An identity score is a system for tagging and verifying the legitimacy of an individual's public identity. Identity scores are increasingly being adopted as a means to prevent fraud in business and as a tool to verify and correct public records. Identity scores incorporate a broad set of consumer data that gauges a person's legitimacy. Identity score components can include (but are not limited to): personal identifiers, public records, Internet data, government records, corporate data, predicted behaviour patterns based on empiric data, self-assessed behaviour patterns, and credit records.

  5. What is a credit bureau? Explain the role of data furnishers in the working of a credit bureau.
    Topic: Core Subjective Topics

    A credit bureau (U.S.) or credit reference agency (UK) is a company that provides consumer credit information on individual borrowers. This helps lenders assess creditworthiness — the ability to pay back a loan — and can affect the interest rate applied to loans; interest rates are based on risk-based pricing, a form of price discrimination based on the different expected costs of different borrowers as set out in their credit rating. Credit bureaus collect and collate personal financial data on individuals and businesses from data furnishers with which the bureaus have a relationship. Data furnishers are businesses, utilities, debt collection agencies, public institutions and the courts (public records) that a consumer or business has had a relationship or experience with; they report that experience to the credit bureaus. The data provided by the furnishers, as well as data collected by the bureaus themselves, are aggregated into the bureaus' data repository or files, and the resulting information is made available on request to contributing companies for credit assessment and credit scoring.

  6. What factors determine a borrower's credit rating?
    Topic: Core Subjective Topics

    Credit ratings are determined differently in each country, but the factors are similar and may include: (1) Payment record — a record of bills being overdue will lower the credit rating. (2) Control of debt — lenders want to see that borrowers are not living beyond their means; experts estimate that non-mortgage credit payments each month should not exceed more than 15 percent of the borrower's after-tax income. (3) Signs of responsibility and stability — longevity in the borrower's home and job (at least two years) is perceived as a sign of stability, and having a respected profession can improve a credit rating. (4) Credit inquiries — inquiries are notations on a credit history file; "soft" pulls (e.g. a creditor checking a person's credit periodically, or a credit counseling agency obtaining a report with the client's permission) do not affect the credit score, whereas "hard" inquiries made by lenders directly affect the borrower's credit score, and many inquiries may signal that the person is looking for loans and is a poor credit risk.

  7. What is adverse credit history? How do repeated adverse reports affect a borrower's credit score?
    Topic: Core Subjective Topics

    Adverse credit history — also called sub-prime credit history, non-status credit history, impaired credit history, poor credit history or bad credit history — is a negative credit rating. A negative credit rating is often considered undesirable to lenders and other extenders of credit for the purpose of loaning money or capital. A consumer's or business's credit history is regularly tracked by credit rating agencies, and the detailed account information reported by creditors includes payment history, credit limits, high and low balances, and any aggressive actions taken to recover overdue debts. Credit scores assess the likelihood that a borrower will repay a loan or other credit obligation: the higher the score, the better the credit history and the higher the probability of timely repayment. When creditors report an excessive number of late payments or trouble with collecting payments, a "hit" on the score is suffered; when adverse judgments and collection agency activity are reported, even bigger hits are suffered. Repeated hits can lower the score and trigger a negative credit rating or adverse credit history.

  8. Write a note on the Credit Information Bureau (CIB) of Pakistan.
    Topic: Core Subjective Topics

    The Credit Information Bureau (CIB) is a public sector credit bureau of Pakistan. It was established in 1992 by the State Bank of Pakistan (SBP) under Section 25(A) of the Banking Companies Ordinance, 1962, and is a part of the Banking Surveillance Department of the SBP. The bureau is a repository of credit information of borrowers: member lending institutions provide credit data (personal and loan information) of their borrowers to the bureau, which consolidates, updates and stores the same and provides this information to its member financial institutions in the form of Credit Worthiness Reports (CWR). In this way the CIB aids financial institutions to make well-informed credit decisions in a timely manner, minimizing credit risk. All fund-based and non-fund-based credit facilities, irrespective of any outstanding amount, are reported to the CIB, and reporting is mandatory for all member financial institutions. All banks, Development Financial Institutions (DFIs), Non-Bank Financial Institutions (NBFIs), Modarabas and Micro Finance Banks operating in Pakistan are members of the CIB; membership is mandatory for all Banks/DFIs (per SBP) and NBFCs (per SECP), and no financial institution can access the CIB database without membership. There are also three privately owned credit bureaus in Pakistan: DataCheck, News-VIS Credit Information Systems and ICIL/Pak Biz Info.

  9. What is a Credit Worthiness Report (CWR)? What information is NOT included in a CWR?
    Topic: Core Subjective Topics

    A Credit Worthiness Report (CWR) is a factual statement of the borrower's credit position, on a certain date, compiled on the basis of information received from the member financial institutions. The CWR contains certain financial and non-financial information on borrowers. It only shows the total liabilities (both fund-based and non-fund-based) but does not reflect the names of the lending financial institutions. A CWR does not include race, income, religion, political affiliation, ethnic background, medical history, private affairs details, bank deposit account details, or other information not related to credit. Financial institutions are allowed to access the CWR of any person in the CIB database even if he or she is not its customer, so that they can obtain the CWR of a prospective customer.

  10. Define credit risk. What is the goal of credit risk management?
    Topic: Core Subjective Topics

    Credit risk is most simply defined as the potential that a bank borrower or counterparty will fail to meet its obligations in accordance with agreed terms. The goal of credit risk management is to maximize a bank's risk-adjusted rate of return by maintaining credit risk exposure within acceptable parameters. Banks need to manage the credit risk inherent in the entire portfolio as well as the risk in individual credits or transactions, and should also consider the relationships between credit risk and other risks. The effective management of credit risk is a critical component of a comprehensive approach to risk management and is essential to the long-term success of any banking organization. Although for most banks loans are the largest and most obvious source of credit risk, credit risk also arises in acceptances, inter-bank transactions, trade financing, foreign exchange transactions, financial futures, swaps, bonds, equities, options, and in the extension of commitments and guarantees and the settlement of transactions.

  11. What are the major causes of serious banking problems related to credit risk? Discuss.
    Topic: Core Subjective Topics

    While financial institutions have faced difficulties over the years for a multitude of reasons, the major cause of serious banking problems continues to be directly related to lax credit standards for borrowers and counterparties, poor portfolio risk management, or a lack of attention to changes in economic or other circumstances that can lead to a deterioration in the credit standing of a bank's counterparties. In supervisors' experience, severe credit losses in a banking system usually reflect simultaneous problems in several areas, such as concentrations, failures of due diligence and inadequate monitoring. Many credit problems reveal basic weaknesses in the credit granting and monitoring processes: competitive pressures and the growth of loan syndication techniques create time constraints that interfere with basic due diligence; some problems arise from the absence of testing and validation of new lending techniques; and others arise from subjective decision-making by senior management, such as extending credits to companies they own or are affiliated with, to personal friends, or to meet a personal agenda. Banks should therefore have a keen awareness of the need to identify, measure, monitor and control credit risk, and to determine that they hold adequate capital against these risks and are adequately compensated for the risks incurred.

  12. State the four areas addressed by the Basel Committee's sound practices for managing credit risk.
    Topic: Core Subjective Topics

    The sound practices set out in the Basel Committee's document "Principles for the Management of Credit Risk" specifically address the following areas: (i) establishing an appropriate credit risk environment; (ii) operating under a sound credit-granting process; (iii) maintaining an appropriate credit administration, measurement and monitoring process; and (iv) ensuring adequate controls over credit risk. These practices should also be applied in conjunction with sound practices related to the assessment of asset quality, the adequacy of provisions and reserves, and the disclosure of credit risk. Although the principles are most clearly applicable to the business of lending, they should be applied to all activities where credit risk is present.

  13. Explain the respective roles of the board of directors and senior management in credit risk management.
    Topic: Core Subjective Topics

    The board of directors should have responsibility for approving and periodically reviewing the credit risk strategy and significant credit risk policies of the bank. The strategy should reflect the bank's tolerance for risk and the level of profitability the bank expects to achieve for incurring various credit risks. The board should approve the bank's strategy for selecting risks and maximizing profits, periodically review the financial results of the bank, determine that the bank's capital level is adequate for the risks assumed, and ensure that the bank's remuneration policies reflect its credit risk strategy (remuneration policies that reward unacceptable behaviour, such as generating short-term profits while deviating from credit policies or exceeding established limits, weaken the bank's credit processes). Senior management should have responsibility for implementing the credit risk strategy approved by the board of directors and for developing policies and procedures for identifying, measuring, monitoring and controlling credit risk. Such policies and procedures should address credit risk in all of the bank's activities and at both the individual credit and portfolio levels. Senior management must also ensure that the bank's credit-granting activities conform to the established strategy, that written procedures are developed and implemented, that loan approval and review responsibilities are clearly and properly assigned, and that there is a periodic independent assessment of the bank's credit-granting functions.

  14. Why must all extensions of credit be made on an arm's-length basis? How can connected lending be controlled?
    Topic: Core Subjective Topics

    All extensions of credit must be made on an arm's-length basis because a potential area of abuse arises from granting credit to connected and related parties, whether companies or individuals. Directors, senior management and other influential parties (e.g. shareholders) should not seek to override the established credit-granting and monitoring processes of the bank, and the bank's credit-granting criteria should not be altered to accommodate related companies and individuals. Credits to related companies and individuals must be monitored with particular care and other appropriate steps taken to control or mitigate the risks of connected lending. Controls can be implemented by requiring that the terms and conditions of such credits not be more favourable than credit granted to non-related borrowers under similar circumstances, and by imposing strict limits on such credits. Another method of control is the public disclosure of the terms of credits granted to related parties. Material transactions with related parties should be subject to the approval of the board of directors (excluding board members with conflicts of interest) and, in certain circumstances (e.g. a large loan to a major shareholder), reported to the banking supervisory authorities.

  15. Differentiate between country (sovereign) risk and transfer risk.
    Topic: Core Subjective Topics

    When banks engage in granting credit internationally, they undertake, in addition to standard credit risk, risk associated with conditions in the home country of a foreign borrower or counterparty. Country or sovereign risk encompasses the entire spectrum of risks arising from the economic, political and social environments of a foreign country that may have potential consequences for foreigners' debt and equity investments in that country. Transfer risk focuses more specifically on a borrower's capacity to obtain the foreign exchange necessary to service its cross-border debt and other contractual obligations. Banks that engage in granting credit internationally must have adequate policies and procedures for identifying, measuring, monitoring and controlling country risk and transfer risk in their international lending and investment activities. The monitoring of country risk factors should incorporate the potential default of foreign private sector counterparties arising from country-specific economic factors, and this function is often the responsibility of a specialist team familiar with the particular issues related to country and transfer risk.

  16. What is an internal risk rating system? Discuss its uses in credit risk management.
    Topic: Core Subjective Topics

    An internal risk rating system is an important tool in monitoring the quality of individual credits, as well as the total portfolio. A well-structured internal risk rating system is a good means of differentiating the degree of credit risk in the different credit exposures of a bank. This allows more accurate determination of the overall characteristics of the credit portfolio, concentrations, problem credits, and the adequacy of loan loss reserves. More detailed and sophisticated systems, used primarily at larger banks, can also be used to determine internal capital allocation, pricing of credits, and profitability of transactions and relationships. Typically, the system categorizes credits into various classes designed to take into account the gradations in risk. In order to facilitate early identification of problems, the rating system should be responsive to indicators of potential or actual deterioration in credit risk; credits with deteriorating ratings should be subject to additional oversight and monitoring, for example through more frequent visits from credit officers and inclusion on a watchlist regularly reviewed by senior management. Ratings assigned at the time credit is granted must be reviewed periodically and a new rating assigned when conditions improve or deteriorate. Because of the importance of consistency and accuracy, responsibility for setting or confirming ratings should rest with a credit review function independent of that which originated the credit concerned.

  17. What is concentration risk in a credit portfolio? In what forms can concentrations arise, and how can a bank mitigate them?
    Topic: Core Subjective Topics

    A continuing source of credit-related problems in banks is concentrations within the credit portfolio. Concentrations of risk can take many forms and can arise whenever a significant number of credits have similar risk characteristics. Concentrations occur when, among other things, a bank's portfolio contains a high level of direct or indirect credits to: (i) a single counterparty; (ii) a group of connected counterparties; (iii) a particular industry or economic sector; (iv) a geographic region; (v) an individual foreign country or a group of countries whose economies are strongly interrelated; (vi) a type of credit facility; or (vii) a type of security. Concentrations also occur in credits with the same maturity, and can stem from more complex or subtle linkages among credits. A high level of concentration exposes the bank to adverse changes in the area in which the credits are concentrated. Banks may use alternatives to reduce or mitigate concentrations, including pricing for the additional risk, increased holdings of capital to compensate for the additional risks, and making use of loan participations in order to reduce dependency on a particular sector or group of related borrowers. Banks also have newer mechanisms such as loan sales, credit derivatives and securitization programmes; however, these mechanisms involve risks that must also be identified and managed. Banks must be careful not to enter into transactions with borrowers they do not know, or credit activities they do not fully understand, simply for the sake of diversification.

  18. What is stress testing? Which three areas should stress testing usefully examine?
    Topic: Core Subjective Topics

    Stress testing is an important element of sound credit risk management: banks should take into consideration potential future changes in economic conditions when assessing individual credits and their credit portfolios, and should assess their credit risk exposures under stressful conditions. This "what if" exercise can reveal previously undetected areas of potential credit risk exposure. Stress testing should involve identifying possible events or future changes in economic conditions that could have unfavourable effects on a bank's credit exposures and assessing the bank's ability to withstand such changes. Three areas that banks could usefully examine are: (i) economic or industry downturns; (ii) market-risk events; and (iii) liquidity conditions. Stress testing can range from relatively simple alterations in assumptions about one or more financial, structural or economic variables to the use of highly sophisticated financial models (typically used by large, internationally active banks). Whatever the method used, the output of the tests should be reviewed periodically by senior management and appropriate action taken where the results exceed agreed tolerances; the output should also be incorporated into the process for assigning and updating policies and limits, and stress-test analyses should include contingency plans, such as hedging against the outcome or reducing the size of the exposure.

  19. Differentiate between market-sensitive and liquidity-sensitive credit exposures, giving examples of each.
    Topic: Core Subjective Topics

    Market-sensitive exposures include foreign exchange and financial derivative contracts. Their value is linked to changes in asset prices in financial markets, so the value of such instruments can change very sharply and adversely to the customer, usually with a small but non-zero probability. Liquidity-sensitive exposures include margin and collateral agreements with periodic margin calls, liquidity back-up lines, commitments and some letters of credit, and some unwind provisions of securitizations. Liquidity-sensitive arrangements require a careful analysis of the customer's vulnerability to liquidity stresses, since the bank's funded credit exposure can grow rapidly when customers are subject to such stresses — for example when borrowers utilize liquidity backup lines or commitments during a constricting of normal credit activity. The contingent nature of the exposure in these instruments requires the bank to have the ability to assess the probability distribution of the size of actual exposure in the future and its impact on both the borrower's and the bank's leverage and liquidity. Because these exposures are probabilistic, they can be correlated with the creditworthiness of the borrower: the same factor that changes the value of a market- or liquidity-sensitive instrument can also influence the borrower's financial health and future prospects, so stress testing of the market or liquidity factors is a key element of the analysis.

  20. Explain the four risk components of credit risk under Basel II: PD, LGD, EAD and Maturity.
    Topic: Core Subjective Topics

    Under Basel II, credit risk can be distributed among four risk components. (a) Probability of default (PD): reviewing a borrower's probability of default is basically done by evaluating the borrower's current and future ability to fulfill its interest and principal repayment obligations, taking into account various characteristics of the borrower (natural or legal person). Where repayments are to be financed exclusively from the cash flow of the object being financed, the credit review must also address the viability of the underlying business model. (b) Loss given default (LGD): the loss given default is affected by the collateralized portion as well as the cost of selling the collateral; therefore the calculated value and type of collateral have to be taken into account in designing the credit approval processes. (c) Exposure at default (EAD): in the vast majority of cases, the exposure at default corresponds to the amount owed to the bank; besides the type of claim, the amount of the claim is an important element in the credit approval process. (d) Maturity (M): the remaining maturity of the exposure, which affects the period over which the borrower may default. Accordingly, four factors should be taken into account in the segmentation of credit approval processes: type of borrower, source of cash flows, value and type of collateral, and amount and type of claim.

  21. What is specialized lending? Name its forms under Basel II and explain how its credit review differs from ordinary corporate finance.
    Topic: Core Subjective Topics

    The distinction of so-called specialized lending from other forms of corporate finance is based on the fact that the primary, if not the only, source of reducing the exposure is the income from the asset being financed, and not so much the unrelated solvency of the company behind it, which operates on a broader basis. Therefore, the credit review has to focus on the asset to be financed and the expected cash flow, rather than on the general creditworthiness of the sponsoring company alone. Credit institutions have to distinguish between the following forms of specialized lending in the calculation of regulatory capital: (1) project finance; (2) object finance; (3) commodities finance; and (4) finance of income-producing commercial real estate. This subdivision under Basel II primarily serves to determine the required capital correctly, but it can also prove useful from a procedural point of view in designing credit approval processes.

  22. Compare the standardized approach and the internal ratings-based (IRB) approach under Basel II.
    Topic: Core Subjective Topics

    Basel II provides two approaches to determine the capital requirement: (1) a standardized approach and (2) an internal ratings-based approach (IRB approach). In the standardized approach, risk-weighted assets continue to be calculated as the product of the amount of exposures and supervisory-determined risk weights, where the risk weights are determined by the category of the borrower (sovereign, bank or corporate) and, unlike the earlier Accord, depend on external credit assessments rather than on OECD membership. The IRB approach allows a more risk-sensitive calculation (based on the bank's internal estimates) of the capital required to cover the risks associated with claims than was possible under Basel I and the modified standardized approach; the goal is to use the capital required from an economic point of view as the yardstick for the regulatory capital requirement, provided banks measure the risks in accordance with the regulatory criteria. Both the basic and the advanced IRB approach require the calculation of the probability of default (PD) of a claim or a pool of claims, so the credit rating of individual exposures has an immediate impact on the capital requirement. The IRB approach distinguishes seven asset classes: sovereign exposures, bank exposures, corporate exposures, retail exposures, equity exposures, securitization, and fixed assets.

  23. Describe the sovereign risk weights under the Basel II standardized approach and calculate the risk-weighted assets for a Rs 50 million claim on a sovereign rated A.
    Topic: Core Subjective Topics

    Under the standardized approach, the risk weights for sovereigns are based on external credit assessments: AAA to AA- — 0%; A+ to A- — 20%; BBB+ to BBB- — 50%; BB+ to B- — 100%; below B- — 150%; unrated — 100%. Claims on central banks are assigned the same risk weight as that applicable to their sovereign governments, and the BIS, IMF, ECB and the European Community receive the lowest risk weight applicable to sovereigns and central banks. Alternatively, supervisors may recognize the country risk scores assigned by Export Credit Agencies (ECAs) following the OECD 1999 methodology: ECA risk scores 1, 2, 3, 4 to 6 and 7 correspond to risk weights of 0%, 20%, 50%, 100% and 150% respectively. Calculation: a claim of Rs 50 million on a sovereign rated A falls in the A+ to A- band, which carries a 20% risk weight. Risk-weighted assets = Rs 50 million x 20% = Rs 10 million.

  24. What risk weights apply to claims on corporates under the standardized approach? Calculate the risk-weighted assets for a Rs 8 million loan to an unrated corporate.
    Topic: Core Subjective Topics

    Under the standardized approach, the risk weighting of rated corporate claims (including claims on insurance companies) is: AAA to AA- — 20%; A+ to A- — 50%; BBB+ to BB- — 100%; below BB- — 150%; unrated — 100%. The standard risk weight for unrated claims on corporates will be 100%, and no claim on an unrated corporate may be given a risk weight preferential to that assigned to its sovereign of incorporation. The Committee did not adopt the sovereign floor proposed in the June 1999 Consultative Paper, recognizing that there are legitimate cases where a corporate can have a higher assessment than the sovereign assessment of its home country. Calculation: risk-weighted assets = Rs 8 million x 100% = Rs 8 million.

  25. What credit conversion factors apply to commitments under the standardized approach?
    Topic: Core Subjective Topics

    The credit conversion factor (CCF) for business commitments with an original maturity of up to one year will be 20%. As an exception, a 0% conversion factor will be applied to commitments that are unconditionally cancellable, or that effectively provide for automatic cancellation due to deterioration in a borrower's creditworthiness, at any time by the bank without prior notice. The credit conversion factor for commitments with an original maturity of over one year will continue to be 50%. In addition, a credit conversion factor of 100% will be applied to the lending of banks' securities or the posting of securities as collateral by the bank, including instances arising out of repo-style transactions (repo/reverse repo and securities lending/borrowing transactions) where the credit-converted exposure is secured by eligible collateral. Where banks, acting as agents, arrange a repo-style transaction between a customer and a third party and provide a guarantee to the customer that the third party will perform, the risk to the bank is the same as if it had entered into the transaction as principal, so capital requirements must be calculated as if the bank were a party to the transaction.

  26. Describe the credit approval process errors: substantive errors and procedural errors.
    Topic: Core Subjective Topics

    The credit approval process must be designed to avoid two sources of error. Substantive errors comprise the erroneous assessment of a credit exposure despite comprehensive and transparent presentation — i.e. the risk was fully and correctly presented, but assessed wrongly. Procedural errors may take one of two forms: on the one hand, the procedural-structural design of the credit approval process itself may be marked by procedural errors, which lead to an incomplete or wrong presentation of the credit exposure; on the other hand, procedural errors can result from an incorrect performance of the credit approval process, caused by negligent or intentional misconduct by the persons in charge of executing the process. Credit review aims to create transparency concerning the risk level of a potential exposure and thus helps avoid substantive errors, while the design of the other process components laid down in the internal guidelines is intended to avoid procedural errors. Both types of error are usually determined by the same risk drivers, which are therefore the starting point for the optimal design of credit approval processes in terms of risk.

  27. Explain the "red" and "yellow" criteria used in the plausibility check and preliminary review.
    Topic: Core Subjective Topics

    Before a credit exposure is subjected to a comprehensive credit review, the employee initially in charge should conduct a plausibility check and preliminary review, looking at the completeness and consistency of the documents filed by the borrower to minimize process loops and further inquiries. Sales should also carry out an initial substantive check based on a select few relevant criteria, creating awareness and active assumption of responsibility for credit risk on the part of the sales department. The preliminary check is especially significant in segments with high rejection rates, as a comprehensive credit review ties up considerable resources in these segments; it prevents exposures which will most likely be rejected from tying up capacities in risk analysis, allowing more detailed scrutiny of promising exposures. In practice, the distinction between two types of check criteria has proven successful: red criteria, which, if fulfilled, lead to an outright rejection of the exposure (also referred to as knock-out criteria); and yellow criteria, which, if fulfilled, require the sales staff to present a plausible and well-documented justification of the respective situation — if this justification cannot be made, the exposure also has to be rejected.

  28. Describe the types of standardized rating models used in credit review: heuristic, empirical-statistical, causal and hybrid models.
    Topic: Core Subjective Topics

    Standardized models of data evaluation can basically be divided into heuristic models, empirical-statistical models and causal models; hybrid models combine two or three of these. Heuristic models attempt to take experiences and use them as a basis to methodically gain new insights; in credit review, experience from the lending business is used to try to predict a borrower's future credit standing, so not only the credit assessment factors but also their impact and weighting are based on subjective experience (conjectured business interrelationships, subjective practical experiences and observations, and business theories). Empirical-statistical models try to assess a borrower's credit standing on the basis of objectifying processes: certain credit review criteria of the exposure are compared with an existing database established empirically, making it possible to classify the exposure; the goodness of fit depends greatly on the quality (size and representativeness) of the database used. Causal models derive direct analytical links to creditworthiness on the basis of finance theory and do not use statistical methods to test hypotheses on an empirical basis. Hybrid models try to combine the advantages of several systems: empirical-statistical models are used for those assessment factors for which a database of sufficient quality exists, while the other factors are assessed by means of heuristic systems.

  29. Explain the structure of a standardized credit review (rating) process: financial rating, qualitative rating, base rating, company rating and borrower rating.
    Topic: Core Subjective Topics

    A typical rating process consists of two components: (1) financial rating (or quantitative rating), which comprises an analysis of the financial data available for the credit applicant — the analysis of annual financial statements (backward-looking approach) has a central position, while the analysis of business planning (forward-looking approach) is increasingly employed, usually with automated programs calculating indicators; and (2) qualitative rating, which evaluates qualitative factors in a standardized fashion and requires comprehensive knowledge of the borrower. Using a weighting function, the financial and qualitative ratings are combined, with the result usually referred to as the base rating. In addition, a bank's internal information on the applicant's conduct in the past (e.g. overdrafts) as well as information concerning the industry in which the company operates can be included; in practice the result is often referred to as the company rating. Finally, if companies are affiliated, possible loss-sharing arrangements are included in the rating process, which makes it possible to determine the risk-bearing entities; the inclusion of a loss-sharing arrangement can affect the assessed probability of default positively (assumption of support in a crisis) or negatively (spillover of a crisis to the company). This typically marks the end of the rating process, and the final result is referred to as the borrower rating, which should be awarded and confirmed jointly by the sales and risk analysis employees primarily in charge of the exposure.

  30. Differentiate between personal collateral and physical collateral, giving examples of each. Why must collateral valuation be independent of the PD calculation?
    Topic: Core Subjective Topics

    Collateral is generally divided into personal and physical collateral. In the case of personal collateral, the provider is basically liable with his entire fortune; examples are suretyship, guarantee and letter of support, and collateral promise. In the case of physical collateral, the bank receives a specific security interest in certain assets of the borrower or the collateral provider; examples are mortgage, pledge of movable assets (on securities, goods, bills of exchange), security assignment, and retention of title. The internal guidelines (collateral catalog) should lay down the type of collateral which the bank generally accepts, together with instructions on assessing the collateral and determining its collateral value, applying general principles such as accounting for sustainable value or valuing on the liquidation principle, general risk deductions (haircuts) and deductions for procedural costs. The valuation of collateral must be effected completely independently of the calculation of the borrower's PD in the credit rating process: the collateralized portion does not affect the applicant's probability of default, but determines the bank's loss given default (LGD). This separation ensures that PD and LGD are shown separately, meeting the Basel II requirement of splitting the review into a customer rating which reflects only the PD, and a transaction valuation which also contains a valuation of the collateral to support the credit decision.

  31. Explain the credit decision-making structure: credit authority, the four-eyes principle and the double vote.
    Topic: Core Subjective Topics

    After the offer stage, the individual exposure receives a formal internal approval as part of the credit approval process, and the essential risk-related issue is the definition of credit authority. Credit authority describes the authorization granted by management to exercise discretion in making credit decisions up to a certain amount. In order to comply with the four-eyes principle, this authority can as a rule only be exercised jointly by two or more decision makers. Moreover, a credit decision should always involve people that do not belong to the sales department (double vote — one vote by the front office and one vote by the back office). The level of authority should be commensurate with the experience of the employees in charge of assessing the credit exposure. Based on the nature of the object of the decision, the decision-making structure is usually subdivided into three categories: (1) non-standardized credits, (2) standardized credits, and (3) short-term overdrafts (all instances in which credit lines are exceeded in the short term). For non-standardized credits, the factors to be taken into account in drawing up the decision-making structure are the level of exposure, the value of collateral, the type of borrower and the probability of default.

  32. What are the eligibility criteria for recognizing an external credit assessment institution (ECAI) under the standardized approach?
    Topic: Core Subjective Topics

    National supervisors are responsible for determining whether an ECAI meets the recognition criteria; the supervisory process for recognizing ECAIs should be made public to avoid unnecessary barriers to entry. The criteria include: (1) Objectivity — the methodology for assigning credit assessments must be rigorous, systematic, and subject to some form of validation based on historical experience; assessments must be subject to ongoing review and responsive to changes in financial condition; before recognition, an assessment methodology for each market segment, including rigorous back-testing, must have been established for at least one year and preferably three. (2) Independence — the ECAI should be independent and should not be subject to political or economic pressures that may influence the rating, and the assessment process should be as free as possible from constraints arising from conflicts of interest. (3) International access/transparency — individual assessments should be available to both domestic and foreign institutions with legitimate interests and at equivalent terms, and the general methodology used should be publicly available. (4) Disclosure — the ECAI should disclose qualitative and quantitative information, including the definition of default, actual default rates experienced in each assessment category and assessment transitions. (5) Resources — the ECAI should have sufficient resources to carry out high quality credit assessments, allowing substantial ongoing contact with senior and operational levels within the entities assessed. (6) Credibility — derived in part from the above criteria and from reliance on the ECAI's assessments by independent parties, underpinned by internal procedures to prevent the misuse of confidential information; an ECAI does not have to assess firms in more than one country to be eligible. Banks must use the chosen ECAIs and their ratings consistently for each type of claim and are not allowed to cherry-pick assessments from different ECAIs.
