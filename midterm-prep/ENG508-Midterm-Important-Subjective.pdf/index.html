  1. Distinguish between sentence, utterance and proposition.
    Topic: Semantics: Foundations

    A sentence is an abstract grammatical structure — a well-formed string of words put together by the grammatical rules of a language; it belongs to the language system rather than to any particular occasion of use. An utterance is a particular piece of language — a word, phrase, sentence or sequence of sentences — spoken or written by a particular speaker or writer in a particular context on a particular occasion; it is the most concrete level of language and is an instance of the production of a sentence (or a fragment of one). A proposition is the meaning-content expressed by a declarative sentence when it is uttered to make a statement: it is what can be judged true or false about some state of affairs in the world. A single sentence may express different propositions on different occasions, and the same proposition can be expressed by different sentences.

  2. Distinguish between sense and reference.
    Topic: Semantics: Foundations

    Reference is the relationship between a linguistic expression and the thing in the world it stands for or picks out — the actual object, person or event referred to on an occasion of use. Sense is the meaning an expression has within the language system itself: its relations with other expressions inside the language, independent of any particular occasion of reference. Frank Palmer (1981) therefore described sense as concerned only with intra-linguistic relations. Frege illustrated the distinction with 'the morning star' and 'the evening star': both expressions have the same reference (the planet Venus) but different senses, because they present that reference in different ways. Sense determines reference, and an expression can have sense even when it has no actual referent, as with 'unicorn'.

  3. Enlist any three features of semantics.
    Topic: Semantics: Foundations

    Three important features of semantics are: (1) Semantics is concerned with literal, de-contextualized and grammatical meaning — it studies what words and sentences mean in themselves, abstracted from particular contexts of use. (2) Semantics studies meaning through sense relations and truth conditions: it examines how the meanings of expressions relate to one another (synonymy, entailment, contradiction) and specifies the conditions under which a sentence would be true or false. (3) Semantics is compositional and systematic: the meaning of a complex expression is built up from the meanings of its parts and the way those parts are combined, which is why speakers can understand sentences they have never heard before.

  4. Discuss the borderline between semantics and pragmatics.
    Topic: Semantics: Foundations

    Semantics studies the literal, conventional meaning that expressions have as part of the language system, independent of particular occasions of use, whereas pragmatics studies how context contributes to meaning — how speakers use language and how hearers interpret utterances in real situations. The borderline between them is the point at which context-free meaning ends and context-dependent interpretation begins. Some phenomena sit clearly on one side: the truth conditions of a sentence are semantic, while conversational implicature is pragmatic. Other phenomena are disputed borderline cases — for example, deciding how much of the interpretation of pronouns, indexicals (I, here, now) and presuppositions belongs to semantics and how much to pragmatics. The two fields are complementary: semantics provides the stable linguistic meaning on which pragmatic interpretation works.

  5. What are the limitations of the truth-conditional theory of meaning?
    Topic: Theories of Meaning

    The truth-conditional theory holds that to know the meaning of a sentence is to know the conditions under which it would be true. It has several limitations. First, it applies naturally only to declarative sentences; questions, commands and exclamations have no truth values, yet they are clearly meaningful. Second, sentences can share exactly the same truth conditions but differ in meaning — for example, active and passive versions, or 'John sold the car to Mary' and 'Mary bought the car from John' — so truth conditions alone do not capture everything a speaker knows. Third, many meaningful expressions, such as 'hello', 'ouch' or single words, do not state anything that could be true or false. Fourth, the theory struggles with context-dependent and vague expressions, whose truth cannot be fixed without knowing the situation of utterance. Finally, it says little about how language is actually used to perform actions and convey implied meanings, which is the territory of pragmatics.

  6. Why do the reference theory and the meaning theory influence semanticists?
    Topic: Theories of Meaning

    Semanticists are influenced by the reference theory and the meaning theory because the central problem of semantics — what meaning is and how expressions come to have it — was first formulated in these theories. The reference theory directs attention to the relation between words and the world: it shows that much of meaning can be studied by asking what an expression stands for, and it underlies truth-conditional semantics, since a sentence is true when the states of affairs it refers to actually obtain. The meaning (idea) theory, by contrast, directs attention to the concepts and mental images that expressions evoke in the minds of speakers and hearers, reminding semanticists that meaning also has a psychological dimension. Together the two theories frame the two poles between which semantic analysis still moves: meaning as a relation to the world, and meaning as a content of the mind. A semanticist's treatment of sense, truth, synonymy and ambiguity is therefore shaped by how these theories are evaluated.

  7. Name and briefly explain Leech's seven types of meaning.
    Topic: Kinds of Meaning

    Leech (1981) distinguishes seven types of meaning. (1) Conceptual (denotative/logical) meaning: the central, dictionary meaning of a word, concerned with logical, cognitive content. (2) Connotative meaning: the communicative value an expression has by virtue of what it refers to — the attributes and associations of the referent, e.g. the connotations of 'woman' such as 'frail' or 'emotional'; connotative meanings are peripheral, indeterminate and open-ended. (3) Social (stylistic) meaning: what a piece of language conveys about the social circumstances of its use — dialect, period, province, status, formality and medium. (4) Affective meaning: what reflects the personal feelings of the speaker, including his attitude to the listener or to what he is talking about. (5) Reflected meaning: the meaning which arises in cases of multiple conceptual meaning, when one sense of a word forms part of our response to another sense. (6) Collocative meaning: the associations a word acquires on account of the meanings of words which tend to occur in its environment, e.g. 'pretty' with girl/flower/garden and 'handsome' with boy/man/car. (7) Thematic meaning: what is communicated by the way the speaker organizes the message, in terms of order, focus and emphasis — as in the difference between an active sentence and its passive counterpart.

  8. Define collocation with reference to lexical relations.
    Topic: Lexical Semantics

    A collocation is a familiar grouping of words — a combination of words which habitually appear together and thereby convey meaning by association, occurring more often than would be expected by chance; for example, 'strong tea' is a correct expression while 'powerful tea' is not. Firth expressed the idea in the remark that 'you shall know a word by the company it keeps'. Within lexical relations, collocation belongs to the syntagmatic relations between words — relations between items that co-occur in a sequence — as opposed to paradigmatic relations, which hold between words that can substitute for one another in the same position. Collocative meaning is therefore one of the ways in which the meaning of a word is fixed by its relations with other words, alongside sense relations such as synonymy, antonymy, hyponymy and meronymy.

  9. Differentiate between polysemy and homonymy.
    Topic: Lexical Semantics

    Both polysemy and homonymy involve a single word-form having more than one meaning, but they differ in whether the meanings are related. In polysemy, one word (one lexical item) has several related senses; for example, 'head' of a person, of a department and of a pin are connected senses of the same word, and dictionaries list them under one entry. In homonymy, two or more different words happen to share the same form but their meanings are unrelated; for example, 'bank' (financial institution) and 'bank' (side of a river) are different lexical items, and dictionaries give them separate entries. Homonyms may share both sound and spelling (homonyms proper), only spelling (homographs) or only sound (homophones). The practical test is relatedness of meaning and etymology: related senses indicate polysemy, unrelated meanings indicate homonymy.

  10. What is a semantic field? Give an example.
    Topic: Lexical Semantics

    A semantic field is a segment of reality symbolized by a set of related words — a set of words which share a common semantic property and which together cover a particular conceptual area, such as body parts, landforms, diseases, colours, foods or kinship relations. The meaning of each word in the field is partly determined by its relations with the other words in the same field: the words are not synonyms, but they are used to talk about the same general phenomenon and are in a sense 'incompatible' with one another — a creature cannot be denoted both as a lion and as an elephant at the same time. For example, the colour terms white, black, red, green, yellow, blue, brown, purple, pink, orange and grey form the semantic field of colour in English, and the words anger, happiness and sadness form part of the field of emotional states. The kinds of semantic fields vary from culture to culture.

  11. What are the advantages of collocation in language study and use?
    Topic: Lexical Semantics

    Knowledge of collocation is advantageous in several ways. First, it helps in understanding meaning: since a word's meaning is partly given by the company it keeps, knowing the habitual partners of a word helps a hearer or reader fix its intended sense and disambiguate it in context. Second, it makes language production natural and fluent: correct collocations such as 'strong tea' and 'heavy rain' (rather than 'powerful tea' or 'strong rain') are a mark of native-like command of a language. Third, it aids vocabulary learning and lexicography, because words are learned and recorded more effectively in their typical combinations than in isolation. Finally, collocations are largely predictable and conventional, so they reduce the burden of interpretation: a listener can anticipate how an utterance will continue and process language faster.

  12. What is entailment? How is it different from presupposition?
    Topic: Sentence Relations and Truth

    Entailment is a relation between sentences based on truth: a sentence p entails a sentence q when the truth of p guarantees the truth of q — whenever p is true, q must also be true (for example, 'Ahmed killed the bird' entails 'The bird died'). Presupposition is a different relation: a sentence presupposes something when it takes that information for granted as already established background (for example, 'Ahmed's brother is a doctor' presupposes that Ahmed has a brother). The standard test that separates them is behaviour under negation. An entailment disappears under negation — 'Ahmed did not kill the bird' no longer entails that the bird died — whereas a presupposition survives negation: 'Ahmed's brother is not a doctor' still presupposes that Ahmed has a brother. Entailment is therefore a purely truth-based, semantic relation, while presupposition depends on what the speaker assumes to be shared knowledge in the context.

  13. Explain the thematic roles Agent, Patient, Theme and Experiencer with examples.
    Topic: Sentence Semantics: Roles and Cases

    Thematic (semantic) roles describe the part played by the participants in the situation expressed by a verb. The Agent is the initiator of some action, capable of acting with volition — for example, in 'Rashid cooked the meat', Rashid is the Agent. The Patient is the entity or person undergoing the effect of the main action of a verb, undergoing some change of state; it is reserved for an entity acted upon and changed by the verb's action — the bushes in 'John cut down these bushes' are the Patient. The Theme is the entity which is changed or moved by the action of a verb, or whose location is described by the verb, but which remains constitutionally unchanged — for example, the book in 'The book is in the library', or the rock in 'Awais threw the rock'. The Experiencer is the entity which is aware of the action or state described by the predicate but is not in control of it — for example, Kamran in 'Kamran heard the door shut'.

  14. Explain Fillmore's cases: Agentive, Instrumental, Dative, Factitive and Locative.
    Topic: Sentence Semantics: Roles and Cases

    In Fillmore's case grammar, a case is the functional relation that a noun group bears to the main verb of a sentence. Fillmore suggested six cases. Agentive is the typically animate, perceived instigator of the action — the doer, as in 'John opened the door'. Instrumental is the inanimate force or object causally involved in the action or state — the means by which the action is performed, as in 'I write a letter with a pen'. Dative is the animate being affected by the action or state, as in 'John gave Mary a book', where Mary is affected. Factitive is the object resulting from the action or state — something that comes into existence through the verb, as in 'They built a house'. Locative is the location or spatial orientation of the action or state, as in 'The baby was hiding behind the curtain'. The sixth case, Objective, is the semantically most neutral case, representing the entity whose role is identified purely by the verb itself.

  15. Define grammatical categories, and discuss grammatical categories and syntactic functions.
    Topic: Sentence Semantics: Roles and Cases

    Grammatical categories are the classifying properties in terms of which grammatical units are described in a language — properties such as number, gender, person, tense, aspect, mood, voice and case. According to Palmer, the two most familiar grammatical categories are gender and number; for example, the category of number in English has two terms (singular and plural), while in Sanskrit it is a three-term system (singular, dual and plural). Grammatical categories are realized through syntactic functions: the elements of a clause perform functions such as subject, predicate, object (direct and indirect), complement and adjunct. Subjects and objects are typically marked by their position in the sentence and by agreement with the verb. Traditional grammarians generally assumed that grammatical categories were essentially semantic — that is, each category was taken to correspond to a category of meaning, such as time for tense or sex for gender — although the correspondence is never perfect.

  16. Differentiate between an argument and a semantic role.
    Topic: Sentence Semantics: Roles and Cases

    An argument is a syntactic notion: it is a noun phrase (or other expression) that a predicate requires in order to complete its meaning and form a complete sentence — a verb like 'give' takes three arguments (giver, gift and recipient), while 'laugh' takes one. A semantic (thematic) role is a semantic notion: it is the part that a participant plays in the situation described by the verb — Agent, Patient, Theme, Goal, Instrument and so on. The two notions belong to different levels of analysis: arguments are counted and positioned by the syntax of the clause, while roles classify what the participants do or undergo in the event. They correspond only loosely: the same role can appear in different argument positions (the Patient is the object in 'John broke the window' but the subject in 'The window broke'), and the same argument position can carry different roles. Semantics therefore needs both notions — argument structure to state what a verb combines with, and roles to state what the combination means.

  17. Name and explain any three lexical rules.
    Topic: Lexicon and Lexical Rules

    Leech (1981) identifies three major types of lexical rules, each deriving one lexical entry from another. (1) Morphological derivation: this involves a change in the morphological specification of an entry, typically by adding an affix to the base — for example, deriving 'hopeless' from 'hope' by adding the suffix '-less', or 'teacher' from 'teach' by adding '-er'. (2) Conversion: this is a change in the syntactic function (and usually the meaning) of an item without any corresponding change in its morphological form — for example, the verb 'catch' being used as a noun, as in 'a good catch'. (3) Semantic transfer: traditionally called metonymy, this derives a new sense of a word by transferring its meaning to something associated with it — for example, in 'The neighbourhood objected to his plans', 'neighbourhood' is transferred from the place to the people living there. Together these rules account for the creative, productive aspect of the lexicon.

  18. What is morphological derivation?
    Topic: Lexicon and Lexical Rules

    Morphological derivation is a type of lexical rule in which one lexical entry is derived from another through a change in its morphological specification. Typically, an affix is added to the original form, which is called the base: Base = Base + Suffix (or prefix), as when '-less' is added to 'hope' to derive 'hopeless', or '-er' is added to 'teach' to derive 'teacher'. The derived entry may change its grammatical category (a noun may become an adjective, a verb a noun) and its meaning in a rule-governed way. Morphological derivation is subject to partial productivity: not every possible derived form is actually acceptable, which is why lexical entries are graded into actual acceptability, potential acceptability and total unacceptability.

  19. What is meant by potential acceptability in lexical rules?
    Topic: Lexicon and Lexical Rules

    Lexical rules are graded by acceptability. An entry has actual acceptability if it is already established and in current use in the language. An entry has potential acceptability if it is not (yet) in actual use but is allowed for by the lexical rules — it could be formed and understood whenever a need arises; for example, many nouns can potentially take the suffix '-less' or '-able' even if the resulting word has never been used. Below these two grades lies total unacceptability: an entry that is not allowed for in the lexical rules at all, such as '*sheapable', where the suffix '-able', which attaches to verbs, is wrongly added to a noun. Potential acceptability thus shows the gap between the enormous generative power of the lexical rules and the limited use actually made of them, and it explains how speakers can coin and understand new words.

  20. Distinguish between lexicon and grammar.
    Topic: Lexicon and Lexical Rules

    The lexicon and the grammar are two different components of a speaker's knowledge of language. The lexicon is the dictionary-like store of lexical entries: each entry is a combination of morphological, syntactic and semantic specifications, recording what is idiosyncratic and unpredictable about a word — its form, its category and its meaning. The grammar, by contrast, consists of the general rules of the language — the syntactic and morphological rules that combine lexical items into phrases and sentences and that apply across the whole vocabulary. The distinction is one between the particular and the general: what can be predicted by rule belongs to grammar, while what must simply be memorized item by item belongs to the lexicon. The boundary is maintained by lexical rules, which capture the regular, creative relations between entries (derivation, conversion, semantic transfer) so that predictable facts need not be listed separately for every word.

  21. What is lexical and semantic deviation?
    Topic: Lexicon and Lexical Rules

    Deviation is the use of language in a way that departs from the normal rules and expectations of the language system, and it is an important source of new and figurative meanings. Lexical deviation occurs at the level of word formation: a speaker coins a word that does not exist in the lexicon, or forms a word in a way not sanctioned by the lexical rules — a neologism. Semantic deviation occurs at the level of meaning: an expression is used in a sense that violates the normal sense relations between words, producing apparent nonsense or anomaly — as in Chomsky's 'Colourless green ideas sleep furiously', which is grammatically well-formed but semantically deviant. Such deviation is not simply an error: poets and ordinary speakers exploit it to create metaphors and fresh meanings, and items that begin as deviations may become institutionalized in the language.

  22. What is reference? Discuss referential expressions.
    Topic: Pragmatics: Context, Reference and Cohesion

    Reference is the act by which a speaker uses a linguistic expression to pick out or identify an entity in the world — a person, object, place or event — so that the hearer can identify it too; successful reference is a basic collaboration between speaker and hearer. Expressions used for referring are called referential expressions: they include proper nouns (e.g. 'Ali'), definite descriptions (e.g. 'the man in the corner'), pronouns (e.g. 'he', 'it') and demonstratives (e.g. 'this', 'that'). Reference may be exophoric, directed to the context outside the text, when there is no previous mention of the referent in the preceding text, or endophoric, directed inside the text — anaphoric when it refers back to something already mentioned, and cataphoric when it refers forward. Reference is broader than any single device: it covers exophora, endophora and anaphora together, and it is one of the main means by which a text hangs together.

  23. Define lexical cohesion.
    Topic: Pragmatics: Context, Reference and Cohesion

    Lexical cohesion is the cohesion created in a text through the choice of vocabulary — through lexical relations between words in successive parts of the text. Where grammatical cohesion works through reference, substitution, ellipsis and conjunction, lexical cohesion works through the meanings of content words. Its main devices are: repetition of the same lexical item; the use of synonyms or near-synonyms; the use of a superordinate term (e.g. 'flower' after 'rose'); the use of general words (e.g. 'thing', 'people'); and collocation — words that habitually co-occur, such as 'illness' and 'doctor'. By linking sentences through related vocabulary, lexical cohesion helps us to maintain the cohesion of a text and gives it its texture and unity.

  24. Differentiate between coherence and cohesion.
    Topic: Pragmatics: Context, Reference and Cohesion

    Cohesion is a property of the text itself: it is the set of formal links — grammatical cohesion (reference, substitution, ellipsis, conjunction) and lexical cohesion (repetition, synonymy, collocation) — by which sentences in a text are tied together on the surface. Coherence is a property of the interpretation: it is the quality of being meaningful and unified, the sense that the text hangs together as a consistent whole in relation to the context and the reader's background knowledge. A text can be cohesive without being coherent — its sentences may be formally linked yet make no unified sense — and a text can be coherent with very little overt cohesion, because the hearer supplies the connections from context. In pragmatics, the corresponding notion to coherence is relevance: an utterance is interpreted as coherent when it can be seen to be relevant in its context. Cohesion therefore concerns how a text is linked, coherence concerns how it is understood.

  25. Explain syntagmatic relations with reference to lexical relations.
    Topic: Pragmatics: Context, Reference and Cohesion

    Lexical relations are of two broad kinds. Paradigmatic relations hold between words that belong to the same grammatical category and can be substituted for one another in the same position in a structure — this is the vertical axis of selection, as 'red' and 'green' are related in 'a red door' / 'a green door'. Syntagmatic relations, by contrast, hold between two or more words that are co-present in a sequence — the horizontal axis of combination. Combinations based on sequential relations are called syntagms: a sentence is a syntagm of words, and phrases are syntagms of smaller units. Sense relations such as synonymy and hyponymy are paradigmatic, while collocation is the clearest syntagmatic lexical relation, since it concerns which words habitually combine with which. A complete account of a word's meaning needs both axes: what it can replace, and what it keeps company with.

  26. State Grice's Cooperative Principle and name its four maxims.
    Topic: Pragmatics: Cooperative Principle and Relevance

    Grice's Cooperative Principle states: 'Make your conversational contribution such as is required, at the stage at which it occurs, by the accepted purpose or direction of the talk exchange in which you are engaged.' Speakers observe the principle through four maxims. (1) The maxim of Quantity: make your contribution as informative as is required, but not more informative than is required. (2) The maxim of Quality: do not say what you believe to be false, and do not say that for which you lack adequate evidence. (3) The maxim of Relation: be relevant. (4) The maxim of Manner: be perspicuous — avoid obscurity of expression, avoid ambiguity, be brief and be orderly. When a speaker openly flouts a maxim, the hearer does not assume the principle has been abandoned; instead, he draws an inference — a conversational implicature — about what the speaker intends to convey.

  27. Explain the cognitive principle of relevance given by Sperber and Wilson.
    Topic: Pragmatics: Cooperative Principle and Relevance

    Relevance Theory, developed by Dan Sperber and Deirdre Wilson in the 1980s, is built on two principles. The cognitive principle of relevance states that human cognition tends to be geared to the maximisation of relevance. In other words, our perceptual mechanisms, memory and inference processes have evolved so that we automatically tend to attend to those inputs — utterances, sights, thoughts — that are likely to yield the greatest cognitive effects (new conclusions, strengthened or weakened assumptions) for the smallest processing effort. An input is relevant to an individual when it connects with his available background information to produce such cognitive effects; other things being equal, the greater the effects and the smaller the effort, the greater the relevance. This cognitive principle then grounds the communicative principle of relevance: every act of ostensive communication communicates a presumption of its own optimal relevance, which entitles the hearer to follow the path of least effort in interpreting it.

  28. What is meant by input relevance in Relevance Theory?
    Topic: Pragmatics: Cooperative Principle and Relevance

    In Relevance Theory, an input is any stimulus that reaches an individual's perceptual or cognitive systems — an utterance, a sight, a sound, a memory or a train of thought. An input is relevant to an individual at a given time if it connects with background information available to him to yield worthwhile conclusions — positive cognitive effects — that matter to him: for example, by answering a question, resolving a doubt, or correcting a mistaken assumption. Relevance is a matter of degree and is assessed by two comparative conditions: other things being equal, the greater the positive cognitive effects achieved, the greater the relevance; and the smaller the processing effort required, the greater the relevance. Because cognition is geared to the maximisation of relevance, communicators can exploit input relevance: by making an utterance ostensive, a speaker creates an expectation that it will be relevant enough to be worth the hearer's processing effort.

  29. Differentiate between the phatic, directive and aesthetic functions of language.
    Topic: Pragmatics: Functions and History

    These are three of the five functions of language given by Leech. The directive function aims to influence the behaviour and attitudes of others — it is the function of requests, commands, advice and persuasion, as in 'Close the door' or 'You should rest'; its focus is on the hearer and the action to be performed. The phatic function serves to establish and maintain social contact and friendly relations rather than to exchange information: greetings, small talk about the weather, and expressions like 'How do you do?' keep the channels of communication open and express solidarity. The aesthetic function concerns the use of language for its own sake — the pleasure taken in the sounds, rhythms and patterns of words themselves, as in poetry, slogans and wordplay, where the form of the message is enjoyed independently of its content. The three differ in orientation: directive language is hearer-and-action oriented, phatic language is relationship oriented, and aesthetic language is oriented towards the message itself.

  30. Give a brief account of the history of pragmatics, with special reference to its current and recent history.
    Topic: Pragmatics: Functions and History

    The term 'pragmatics' was introduced by the American philosopher Charles Morris in his Foundations of the Theory of Signs (1938), where he divided semiotics into syntax, semantics and pragmatics, pragmatics being the study of the relation of signs to their interpreters. The philosophical groundwork was laid by ordinary-language philosophers, especially the post-war Oxford school: J.L. Austin developed the theory of speech acts, and H.P. Grice presented the Cooperative Principle and the theory of implicature (1975). The use of the word 'pragmatics' to describe a separate field of study, on a par with syntax and semantics, was established during the 1970s: the Journal of Pragmatics was founded (1977), and pragmatics had become a recognized sub-discipline of linguistics by the late 1970s. In its recent history, theorists from the 1970s onwards have worked on formal accounts of phenomena such as scalar implicature, presupposition and conventional implicature; Leech added the Politeness Principle (1983), and Sperber and Wilson proposed Relevance Theory (1986), which recast pragmatics as part of cognitive psychology. Pragmatics has since expanded into areas such as intercultural communication, discourse analysis and language teaching.

  31. Discuss the radical literalist perspective on the borderline between semantics and pragmatics.
    Topic: Pragmatics: Functions and History

    Radical literalism is the view that the literal meaning of a sentence is fully and exclusively determined by the meanings of its words and its syntactic structure, with context playing little or no role in fixing what is said. On this perspective, semantics delivers a complete, context-independent proposition — the literal content — and pragmatics is confined to what the speaker additionally implies beyond that content, such as conversational implicatures. The borderline between the two fields is therefore sharp and is drawn exactly at the literal proposition: everything truth-conditional belongs to semantics, and everything inferred belongs to pragmatics. Critics object that even apparently basic matters — resolving ambiguity, fixing the reference of indexicals and pronouns, and enriching vague expressions — require contextual, pragmatic processes before any complete proposition is available. Radical literalism is thus one pole of the continuing debate about where semantics ends and pragmatics begins, opposed by contextualist views on which pragmatic processes contribute directly to what is said.
