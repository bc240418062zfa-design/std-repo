  1. What is child development? Which experts study this field and why is it studied?
    Topic: Foundations of Child Development

    Child development is the field that studies the growth and development of the child. It is studied by many different kinds of experts, including educationists, teachers, philosophers, psychologists, sociologists, neuroscientists and social workers, who work independently or together to understand the nature of child development. The most important reason to study child development is to understand how growth and development take place in the child. This understanding benefits parents, teachers, educational experts, policy makers and the community in general, and it helps provide insight into the problems a child faces during development.

  2. Is development continuous or discontinuous? Discuss.
    Topic: Foundations of Child Development

    Experts in child development ask whether developmental change is continuous or discontinuous, that is, broken down into stages. The answer depends on how the expert views the developmental process. For most experts, individual growth may be continuous, but when studied it is broken down into stages or periods. Many suggest that the individual changes qualitatively over time, backed by measurable quantitative changes; for example, children express lower levels of cognitive functioning when young compared to when they are older, a change that Piaget and Inhelder (1969) documented with empirical experiments. When stages in which developmental change is minimal lie close together, development appears to be more continuous.

  3. What is developing in the child? Describe any four domains of development.
    Topic: Foundations of Child Development

    (1) Physical development involves the physical growth and development of the individual starting in the womb, through the prenatal period, all the way to late adolescence; body proportions change over development (allometric development), and the brain increases in size until, by six years, it is very close to the adult brain. (2) Cognitive development needs biological maturation (like age) and experience (like schooling) together; according to Piaget, cognitive structures change through many stages, such as the sensorimotor and preoperational stages. (3) Language development is seen in the rapid enrichment of vocabulary, from about 11,000 words in 1st grade to about 80,000 words in 12th grade, along with improvement in grammar. (4) Emotional development: emotions like anger, fear and happiness can be seen in the infant within a few months, while sadness develops after infancy; from day one infants are aware of the emotions of others and by about one year they can draw emotional cues from others. (Other domains include social development, based on reliable attachment to the primary caregiver, and moral development, which occurs along with cognitive development.)

  4. Describe the structure of DNA and explain what a gene is.
    Topic: Genes, Heredity and Environment

    Most human cells contain 46 chromosomes (23 pairs) that are made from deoxyribonucleic acid (DNA) molecules. The DNA consists of two strands of sugar and phosphate molecules arranged like a spiral staircase, where the stairs are made out of nucleotide bases: adenine (A), thymine (T), cytosine (C) and guanine (G). These nucleotides are the alphabets or building blocks of our genetic code. A gene is a segment of DNA (base pairs) that provides instructions for a particular structure, function or trait, for example the color of the eyes, instructions to build the membrane of a cell wall, or instructions for moving chemicals like neurotransmitters in neurons. There are roughly 20-25 thousand genes in human beings.

  5. Differentiate between monozygotic and dizygotic twins.
    Topic: Genes, Heredity and Environment

    Monozygotic (MZ) or identical twins are formed when a zygote divides into two cells and breaks to form two independent zygotes. Because both twins come from the same fertilized egg, they share their genetic information identically (100%). Dizygotic (DZ) or fraternal twins, on the other hand, form through the independent fertilization of two separate sperm and egg cells. Fraternal twins are therefore no more genetically similar than ordinary siblings. This difference is the basis of twin studies: comparing identical and fraternal twins helps estimate the genetic contribution to traits and characteristics.

  6. What is Down syndrome? What causes it and what are its main features?
    Topic: Genes, Heredity and Environment

    Down syndrome is a chromosomal disorder caused by the presence of an extra chromosome, chromosome 21, in the individual's cells. It is marked by maturational retardation. The individual with Down syndrome has a low IQ, facial defects and heart problems, and a shortened life span. Chromosomal disorders like Down syndrome (extra chromosome) and Turner syndrome (a missing chromosome) account for the majority of miscarriages in women and are perhaps nature's way of weeding out serious defects.

  7. Describe three procedures used for prenatal screening and genetic testing.
    Topic: Genes, Heredity and Environment

    (1) Ultrasonography (ultrasound): sound waves are used to present images of the fetus inside the mother's womb; it helps monitor fetal growth and detect defects. (2) Amniocentesis: a needle is inserted into the mother's abdomen and cells are withdrawn from the amniotic fluid; the procedure is used to detect chromosomal and genetic abnormalities in the fetus. (3) Chorionic Villus Sampling (CVS): a catheter is inserted into the uterus and cells are taken to test the health of the fetus. These procedures allow parents and doctors to monitor the healthy growth of the fetus and detect possible disease before birth.

  8. Explain any three ways in which genes and environment interact in the development of traits.
    Topic: Genes, Heredity and Environment

    (1) Range of reaction: the range of possible phenotypes that exist for a particular genotype; the same genotype can be expressed differently depending on the environment. (2) Canalization: genetic limits on the effects of the environment; heredity restricts the development of some characteristics to just one or two outcomes (in experiential canalization, by contrast, the environment limits the expression of genes). (3) Niche-picking: the tendency to pick activities and environments that fit with our genetic predispositions. (4) Probabilistic epigenesis: the likelihood that specific environmental conditions will activate specific genes that will lead to specific outcomes. In all individuals, genetic and environmental factors are intertwined, and traits observable in a person (the phenotype) may or may not reflect the full genotype.

  9. How is heritability estimated? Explain twin studies and adoption studies.
    Topic: Twin and Adoption Studies

    Heritability is an estimate of the degree of genetic influence for a given trait or behavior, and heritability estimates measure the individual differences in complex traits in a specific population that are due to genetic factors; they range between 0.00 and 1.00. There are two main ways to estimate heritability. (1) Twin studies compare measurements of identical (monozygotic) and fraternal (dizygotic) twins. Identical twins are genetically 100% similar while fraternal twins are much less similar, so if a trait is more similar in identical than in fraternal twins raised together, heredity is implicated. (2) Adoption studies compare measurements of children with their adoptive and biological parents; resemblance to biological parents points to genetic influence, while resemblance to adoptive parents points to environmental influence.

  10. What are concordance rates? What do the scores of 0 and 100 indicate?
    Topic: Twin and Adoption Studies

    Concordance rates are a measure of the heritability of traits. They determine what percentage of twin pairs both show a trait when it is present in one twin. These measurements range from 0 to 100%. A score of 0 means that if one twin has the trait, the other twin does not have it, showing no concordance. A score of 100 means that if one twin has the trait, the other twin also has it, showing complete concordance. Higher concordance in identical twins than in fraternal twins indicates a stronger genetic contribution to the trait.

  11. Explain the three types of genetic-environmental correlation.
    Topic: Twin and Adoption Studies

    Genetic-environmental correlation refers to the fact that our genes influence the environment in which we live. It is of three types. (1) Passive correlation: when the child is young, she has no control over her environment; the parents provide the environment, which is itself influenced by the parents' genes. (2) Evocative correlation: the child's genetically influenced characteristics evoke changes in the environment; for example, a happy baby gets more stimulation from caregivers. (3) Active correlation: at an older age, children extend their experiences beyond the family, choosing environments that complement their heredity; this is also called niche picking.

  12. Describe the infant states of arousal and the changes in sleep patterns during early life.
    Topic: The Newborn: Reflexes, Sleep and Early Learning

    The infant states of arousal include regular and irregular sleep cycles, drowsiness, quiet alertness, waking activity and crying. During the first month, newborns spend about two-thirds of their time sleeping. Sleep moves to an adult-like night-day schedule during the first year, and the need for sleep declines from about 18 hours to about 12 hours a day by age 2. This pattern slowly changes toward the adult pattern of about 8 hours of sleep between 5 and 9 years of age. In many cultures infants and young children sleep with one or both parents (co-sleeping), which gives caregivers rest and strength to continue care when the infant wakes.

  13. What is Sudden Infant Death Syndrome (SIDS)? At what age does it peak and how can its incidence be reduced?
    Topic: The Newborn: Reflexes, Sleep and Early Learning

    Sudden Infant Death Syndrome (SIDS) is the unexpected, sudden death of a child under the age of 1 year in which an autopsy does not show an explainable cause of death. It peaks between 2 and 4 months of age and is a leading cause of infant mortality in industrialized nations. The incidence of SIDS can be reduced by quitting smoking, changing the infant's sleeping position, and removing a few bedclothes from the infant's bed.

  14. Describe the steps involved in reaching and grasping in infancy.
    Topic: The Newborn: Reflexes, Sleep and Early Learning

    Reaching and grasping develop through a sequence of steps. (1) Prereaching: the earliest, poorly coordinated attempts to reach toward objects. (2) Reaching with two hands, then with one hand. (3) Ulnar grasp: the infant grasps objects with the fingers against the palm, adjusts the grip to the object, and moves objects from hand to hand. (4) Pincer grasp: the infant uses the thumb and forefinger to pick up things, allowing much finer manipulation. Motor development follows two trends: the cephalocaudal trend (head to tail: control of the head before the arms and trunk, which come before the legs) and the proximodistal trend (center to periphery: control of the head and trunk before the arms and legs).

  15. Differentiate between probability and non-probability samples, giving the main types of each.
    Topic: Research Methods: Sampling and Epidemiology

    When individuals in a population have an equal chance (greater than zero) of being drawn into a sample, it is called a probability sample; when some members of a population have no chance (equal to zero) of being drawn, it is a non-probability sample. Probability samples include: the random sample, in which each member has an equal probability of being selected; the systematic sample, in which sampling begins by randomly selecting one member and then selecting every nth member onwards; and the stratified sample, based on the distinct categories of which the population is composed. Non-probability samples include the quota sample, in which the population's subgroups are proportionately sampled, and the convenience sample, in which members are chosen on the basis of their relative ease of access, such as friends, co-workers or shoppers at a store. Non-probability sampling can suffer from selection bias.

  16. Differentiate between naturalistic and structured observation. Also explain event sampling and time sampling.
    Topic: Research Methods: Self-Reports, Observation and Psychophysiological Methods

    In naturalistic (field) observation, the investigator observes behavior where it actually happens, in its natural setting. In structured observation, which usually takes place in a laboratory situation, the behavior of interest can be evoked and all participants have an equal chance to display the behavior. Two sampling procedures are used in observation. In event sampling, the observer records all instances of a particular behavior (events) during a specific time period. In time sampling, the observer records certain behaviors that occur during a specified time interval. Observation is an important first step in studying any phenomenon, but it can suffer from the observer's influence on participants and from observer bias.

  17. Differentiate between the correlational and experimental methods. Why can correlation not establish cause and effect?
    Topic: Research Methods: Correlation, Experiments and Case Studies

    The correlational method attempts to determine the strength of the relationship between two or more variables or behaviors, for example whether maternal warmth leads to children's compliance. The strength (magnitude) of a correlation is represented by a number between 0 and 1, and its direction by a + or - sign. The experimental method, in contrast, manipulates one variable (the independent variable) and measures its effect on another (the dependent variable), with participants randomly assigned to experimental and control groups. Correlation cannot establish cause and effect because it only shows that two variables vary together; it does not show which variable influences the other, and a third variable may be responsible for the relationship. Only the controlled manipulation of the experimental method can determine a cause-and-effect relationship.

  18. Compare the longitudinal and cross-sectional research designs, giving the advantages and problems of each.
    Topic: Research Methods: Correlation, Experiments and Case Studies

    In the longitudinal design, the same participants are studied repeatedly at different ages. Its advantage is that it permits the study of common patterns and individual differences in development, and of relationships between early and later events and behaviors. Its problems are that age-related changes may be distorted by biased sampling, selective attrition, practice effects and cohort effects, and theoretical and methodological changes in the field can make findings obsolete. In the cross-sectional design, people of different ages are all studied at the same time. It is efficient and is not plagued by selective attrition, practice effects, or changes in the field. However, it does not permit the study of individual developmental trends, and age differences may be distorted because of cohort effects.

  19. What is a case study? Discuss its strengths and weaknesses.
    Topic: Research Methods: Correlation, Experiments and Case Studies

    A case study is an in-depth analysis of a person, such as a child, or a group of individuals, or events. It is used in qualitative research and collects and analyzes descriptive and explanatory data about the case, bringing together a wide range of information through interviews, observations, test scores and psychophysiological measures. Its strengths are its flexibility, as a comparatively flexible method of scientific research, and its emphasis on context: by studying a single subject or a small group, case studies specialize in 'deep data' or 'thick description.' Its weaknesses are its inherent subjectivity, for which it is often criticized as too subjective and even pseudoscientific; the high investment of time and effort it demands; and ethical considerations such as conflicts of interest, biases and opinions.

  20. Describe Freud's psychosexual stages of development.
    Topic: Psychodynamic Theory

    Freud proposed that the child passes through five psychosexual stages, each with a characteristic sexual focus that leaves its mark on adult personality; the foundations of personality are laid down within the first five years of life. (1) Oral stage (birth to 24 months): the infant receives pleasure from the mouth through sucking, chewing and biting; fixation leads to excessive eating or smoking in adulthood. (2) Anal stage (2-3 years): pleasure focuses on bowel and bladder elimination, and the child copes with the demands of control; excessive punishment to control elimination can lead to hostility. (3) Phallic stage (3-7 years): pleasure focuses on the genitals; boys develop erotic feelings towards the mother and hostility towards the father, while girls develop a special attachment towards their fathers. (4) Latency stage (7-11 years): sexuality and its associated pleasures become dormant, and the child explores individuals other than family members. (5) Genital stage (11 years to adulthood): same-gender hostility is suppressed and affection for the opposite sex increases, marking mature sexual interests.

  21. Explain Piaget's concepts of schema, assimilation and accommodation.
    Topic: Piaget and Vygotsky

    For Piaget, a cognitive structure consists of schemas (or schemata), which result from both biological maturation and cumulative experience. A schema is an element of the cognitive structure that engages in a class of actions; grasping, sucking and reaching are schemas because a variety of actions can be made by them. A schema can lead to an overt action (a behavioral response, such as grasping a book) or a covert action (thinking, such as thinking of grasping a golf club). Assimilation is the process of responding to the environment in accordance with one's cognitive structure, in which existing cognitive structures are matched with the physical environment. Accommodation is the process that modifies the cognitive structure, and is more simply a process of learning. Through assimilation and accommodation the cognitive structure develops and grows over the individual's life span.

  22. Describe Piaget's four stages of cognitive development.
    Topic: Piaget and Vygotsky

    (1) Sensorimotor stage (birth to 2 years): the infant learns about the world through sensory and motor interactions, beginning with simple reflex actions and primary circular reactions, and develops object permanence. (2) Preoperational stage (2 to 7 years): the child understands symbols but is quite egocentric, seeing things from his or her own point of view, as shown by the three mountain test; in the period of intuitive thought (4-7 years) problem solving is without logical rules, so conservation problems cannot be solved correctly. (3) Concrete operational stage (7 to 11 years): the child uses symbols and manipulates them logically, masters conservation of number, mass, length and volume, learns reversibility of operations, and by 9-11 years masters area conservation, class inclusion, transitivity, sorting and seriation; however, the problems must be concrete in context. (4) Formal operational stage (11 years to adulthood): the individual can solve abstract problems, systematically search for solutions and test hypotheses, as in the pendulum problem.

  23. What is the zone of proximal development? How does Vygotsky's view of development differ from Piaget's?
    Topic: Piaget and Vygotsky

    Vygotsky defined the zone of proximal development (ZPD) as the distance between the child's actual developmental level, shown by solving a problem independently, and the level of potential development, shown by solving the problem with peer or adult assistance. He suggested that teachers should attempt to keep students in this zone in order to achieve maximum achievement. Vygotsky's sociocultural view differs from Piaget's theory in that development cannot be separated from its social context: cognitive development is an apprenticeship in which children advance through interaction with more mature members of society, learning can lead development, children construct their knowledge socially, and language plays a vital role in mental development. Vygotsky also held that intellectual development may be speeded up with assistance before a stage reaches its maturation, whereas Piaget emphasized biological maturation combined with the child's own experience.

  24. Describe Bandura's Bobo doll study and its conclusions about learning in children.
    Topic: Behaviorism and Social Learning Theory

    Bandura showed in his Bobo doll study that children imitate a model's behavior: after observing an adult model behaving aggressively towards a Bobo doll, children reproduced the aggressive behavior themselves. The study supports social learning theory and leads to three important conclusions. (1) Behavioral learning has a cognitive explanation, because children learn by observing and representing the model's actions. (2) Learning does not require reinforcement; children acquired the behavior simply by watching the model. (3) Reinforcement (incentive) directs learned activity, that is, performance: whether the learned behavior is actually performed depends on vicarious reinforcement, such as seeing the model rewarded or punished. This differs from conventional behaviorism, in which learning is explained only through direct conditioning.

  25. Explain Bronfenbrenner's ecological systems theory with its different layers of environment.
    Topic: Socio-Cultural and Ecological Perspectives

    Bronfenbrenner's ecological systems theory centers on the ecology of the child during development and proposes complex layers of environment, each affecting the child's development; changes in any layer ripple across the other layers, and children are both products and producers of their environment. (1) Microsystem: the innermost layer, consisting of the immediate surroundings, including the person's family, peers, school and neighborhood. (2) Mesosystem: the relations between the different microsystems, or connections between contexts, such as the connection between family experiences and school experiences. (3) Exosystem: settings further removed from the individual that may directly or indirectly affect behavior; for example, what a wife or child experiences at home may be influenced by the husband's experiences at work. (4) Macrosystem: the culture in which individuals live, including socioeconomic status, poverty and ethnicity. (5) Chronosystem: the patterning of environmental events over the life of the individual and socio-historical events or circumstances.

  26. What is imprinting? Explain the sensitive period with reference to the ethological approach.
    Topic: Ethology, Evolution and Attachment

    Ethology, a field with roots in biology and evolution, studies instinctive behaviors, which are complex, pre-programmed, genetically controlled behaviors also called Fixed Action Patterns. Imprinting is a process that modifies instinctive behaviors; it includes filial imprinting (following the mother or a substitute) and sexual imprinting (courting a member of another species). Many behaviors act as releasers that make mothers and caregivers form close bonds of attachment, and these bonds play a vital role in the individual's adult life. Imprinting can take place only during a window of opportunity early in life called the sensitive period, in which the individual is especially responsive to the environment; developmental changes are hard to induce later. In humans too there is a sensitive period, an optimal time for certain capacities to emerge, though its boundaries are less defined than those of a critical period. Lorenz's work on imprinting influenced Bowlby's ethological perspective on child-caregiver attachment.

  27. Describe the three periods of prenatal development.
    Topic: Prenatal Development and Birth

    (1) Period of the zygote (0-2 weeks): this period begins with fertilization, the union of the father's sperm cell with the mother's egg forming the zygote; the zygote divides and grows into a ball-like structure called the blastocyst, implantation takes place, and the placenta starts to form. (2) Period of the embryo (3-8 weeks): the three germ layers (ectoderm, mesoderm and endoderm) form, neurons develop, and arms, legs, face, organs and muscles start developing; the heart begins beating, and by the second month the buds become arms, legs, fingers and toes and the embryo responds to touch. (3) Period of the fetus (9-30 weeks and onwards to birth): this is the period of growth and finishing; in the third month the lungs begin to expand and contract and sex is evident on ultrasound, in the second trimester the mother can feel movements and vernix and lanugo develop, and in the third trimester the age of viability (22-26 weeks) is reached and the cerebral cortex enlarges.

  28. What are teratogens? Discuss the effects of alcohol on the developing fetus.
    Topic: Prenatal Development and Birth

    Teratogens are any kind of drug or substance that interferes with the development of the fetus, usually causing birth defects. Examples include alcohol, cocaine, cigarette smoke (with harmful chemicals such as nicotine, carbon monoxide and cyanide, which damage the placenta and reduce the blood supply, oxygen and nutrients available to the fetus), and viral and infectious diseases such as herpes, syphilis and HIV-AIDS. Alcohol use during pregnancy is catastrophic. Fetal Alcohol Syndrome (FAS) is a syndrome of birth defects caused by prenatal exposure to alcohol. Fetal Alcohol Effects (FAE) include lowered IQ, hyperactivity, growth deficiencies and physical malformations. Teratogens can also lead to babies being born premature or preterm (before 37 weeks of gestation) and with low birth weight (less than 5 1/2 pounds at birth).

  29. Describe the three stages of birth.
    Topic: Prenatal Development and Birth

    (1) Stage 1, Dilation: the gradual opening of the cervix caused by labor contractions; this is the longest stage of birth. (2) Stage 2, Delivery: the baby actually moves through the birth canal and is born. (3) Stage 3, Afterbirth: the placenta and other membranes are delivered through the birth canal after the baby. Childbirth may be natural or prepared, using classes, relaxation and breathing techniques, and the support of a labor coach and birth attendants such as certified nurse-midwives or a doula (social support). Some form of medication, such as analgesics, anesthetics or epidural analgesia, is used in more than 80% of North American births.

  30. Differentiate between preterm and small-for-date babies.
    Topic: Prenatal Development and Birth

    Preterm babies are born weeks before their due date, but they may be of appropriate weight for the length of the pregnancy. Small-for-date babies, in contrast, are below the expected weight for the length of the pregnancy; they may be born at the due date or preterm. Low birth weight babies are at risk of disabilities and health problems, and interventions for preterm infants include the isolette with respirator, feeding tube and intravenous medication, special infant stimulation such as 'kangaroo care', and parent training.

  31. What is the APGAR test? When is it conducted and what is its purpose?
    Topic: Prenatal Development and Birth

    The APGAR test is a brief assessment of the newborn conducted at 1 and 5 minutes after birth. It is used to identify newborns that are at risk and need medical attention, by quickly checking the baby's condition immediately after delivery. Babies who receive prenatal stimulation tend to rate high on APGAR scores. Together with other assessments such as the Neonatal Behavioral Assessment Scale (NBAS), it helps health professionals judge how well the newborn has made the transition to life outside the womb.

  32. Describe the sensory capabilities of the newborn.
    Topic: The Newborn and Infancy

    Touch: newborns display reflex responses to touch on the mouth, palms, soles and genitals, and later engage in exploratory mouthing; pleasurable touch releases endorphins, and the infant is sensitive to pain, which can be relieved with anesthetics, sugar and gentle holding. Taste and smell: infants prefer sweet tastes at birth and quickly learn to like new tastes; they have preferences for odors from birth, are affected by the mother's diet during pregnancy, and can locate odors and identify the mother by smell from birth. Hearing: infants can hear a wide variety of sounds at birth and even before birth, prefer complex sounds to pure tones, learn sound patterns within days, and are sensitive to voices, being biologically prepared to learn language. Vision is the least developed of the senses at birth: the infant is unable to see long distances or focus clearly and has limited binocular ability; by 2 months focus and color vision develop, and by about 6 months acuity, scanning and tracking improve and depth perception becomes better.

  33. Explain experience-expectant and experience-dependent growth in brain development.
    Topic: Brain Development in Infancy

    Brain development in infancy depends on stimulation, which is vital when the brain is growing rapidly. (1) Experience-expectant growth refers to the ordinary experiences that the brain 'expects' in order to grow normally; the young brain is prepared by evolution to receive the common experiences of the species, such as patterned light, sound and touch. (2) Experience-dependent growth refers to additional growth of the brain that occurs as a result of the specific learning experiences of the individual, which vary from person to person and produce new connections throughout life. The young brain also shows plasticity: in infants and young children, parts of the brain are not yet specialized, so they recover better from brain injury, and language recovers better than spatial skills, although some problems with complex mental skills may remain.

  34. What is anorexia nervosa? State its main features and consequences.
    Topic: Growth, Nutrition, Weight and Play

    Anorexia nervosa is an eating disorder associated with being underweight, in which the individual is afraid of gaining weight. It begins early in life, around 9-14 years, and continues as the individual grows. It occurs in girls about 10 times more than in boys and can affect individuals of any race, socioeconomic status or cultural background. Individuals with anorexia usually have a distorted body image. Anorexia is never truly cured, but it can to a great extent be managed by medical assistance. More generally, an underweight individual (below 18.5 BMI) is at risk of a number of physical and mental diseases, such as hyperthyroidism, cancer and tuberculosis, and women suffer from amenorrhea and osteoporosis.

  35. Describe the different kinds of play in childhood and the role of each.
    Topic: Growth, Nutrition, Weight and Play

    (1) Exploratory play (rattles, squeeze toys) is used at an early age in infancy; through it the infant learns elementary motor skills, interacts with the environment and gains better control over the senses. (2) Construction play (blocks, model kits) engages the growing child in creativity. (3) Pretend and role play (dolls, stuffed toys, cars): children become 'mommies' and 'doctors' to their dolls and learn role playing and social interactions. (4) Game and activity play (jigsaw puzzles, video games) improves motor skills, eye-hand coordination and interaction with others. (5) Sports and recreational play (tricycles, bicycles) begins when children are older and stronger; it boosts self-esteem and pleasure in achievement. (6) Media play (paint, paper, musical instruments) makes children learn and become creative. (7) Educational and academic play (books, educational software) supports learning. Together with exercise, which promotes strength, improves cardiovascular fitness, develops athletic skills and maintains appropriate body weight, play is essential for healthy growth and a sense of security.
