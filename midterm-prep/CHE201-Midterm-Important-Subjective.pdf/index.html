  1. Define physical chemistry. Differentiate between the microscopic and macroscopic viewpoints of a chemical system.
    Topic: Thermodynamic Systems and Properties

    Physical chemistry is the study of the underlying physical principles that govern the properties and behavior of chemical systems. In the microscopic viewpoint, the system is studied on the basis of the concept of molecules. In the macroscopic viewpoint, the large-scale properties of matter are studied without explicit use of the molecule concept.

  2. Explain open, closed and isolated systems with their differences.
    Topic: Thermodynamic Systems and Properties

    An open system is one in which transfer of matter between the system and its surroundings can occur. A closed system is one in which no transfer of matter can occur between the system and its surroundings (only energy may be exchanged). An isolated system is one that does not interact in any way with its surroundings; it exchanges neither matter nor energy. An isolated system is always a closed system, but a closed system is not necessarily isolated.

  3. Define extensive and intensive thermodynamic properties with examples. What is a phase?
    Topic: Thermodynamic Systems and Properties

    An extensive property is one whose value is equal to the sum of its values for the parts of the system; mass and volume are extensive properties. An intensive property is one whose value does not depend on the size of the system; density and pressure are intensive properties. If every intensive macroscopic property is constant throughout a system, the system is homogeneous. A homogeneous part of a system is called a phase, and a system of two or more phases is heterogeneous.

  4. State Boyle's law, Charles' law and Avogadro's hypothesis, and show how they combine to give the ideal gas equation.
    Topic: Gas Laws and Real Gases

    Boyle's law: the volume of a fixed quantity of gas at constant temperature is inversely proportional to the pressure (V ∝ 1/P). Charles' law: the volume of a fixed amount of gas at constant pressure is proportional to the absolute temperature (V ∝ T). Avogadro's hypothesis: at constant temperature and pressure, the volume of a gas is directly related to the number of moles (V ∝ n); equal volumes of gases at the same temperature and pressure contain equal numbers of molecules. Combining the three laws, V ∝ nT/P, which gives the ideal gas equation pV = nRT.

  7. What assumptions are made for an ideal gas? Why do real gases deviate from ideal behavior?
    Topic: Gas Laws and Real Gases

    For an ideal gas it is assumed that the gas particles occupy negligible volume and that there are negligible intermolecular interactions. In a real gas the molecules have a finite volume and exert attractive forces on one another, so at high pressure or low temperature the gas deviates from the ideal gas equation.

  8. Write the van der Waals equation and explain the corrections represented by the constants a and b.
    Topic: Gas Laws and Real Gases

    Van der Waals (1873) modified the ideal gas equation to give [p + a(n/V)2](V − nb) = nRT. The constant a accounts for the attractive forces between gas particles: these forces reduce the pressure exerted on the walls, and the correction is proportional to (n/V)2 because it depends on the frequency and the force of collisions. The constant b accounts for the non-zero volume of the gas particles, which behave like small impenetrable spheres; the volume actually available for movement is the container volume minus nb.

  9. Define critical temperature, critical pressure and critical volume, and give their relations with the van der Waals constants.
    Topic: Gas Laws and Real Gases

    The critical temperature is the temperature at and above which the vapor of a substance cannot be liquefied, no matter how much pressure is applied. The critical pressure is the pressure required to liquefy the gas at its critical temperature, and the critical volume is the volume occupied by one mole of the gas under critical conditions. Their relations with the van der Waals constants are: Vc = 3b, Pc = a/27b2 and Tc = 8a/27Rb.

  10. State the Zeroth law of thermodynamics and explain its importance.
    Topic: Laws of Thermodynamics

    The Zeroth law states that two systems that are each found to be in thermal equilibrium with a third system will be found to be in thermal equilibrium with each other. Its importance is that it allows us to assert the existence of temperature as a state function: two systems in thermal equilibrium with each other have the same temperature. Although it was formulated after the first, second and third laws, its statement logically precedes them, and it is needed for the development of thermodynamics.

  11. State the first law of thermodynamics. Define enthalpy and show that ΔH = qp at constant pressure.
    Topic: Laws of Thermodynamics

    The first law is a statement of the conservation of energy: energy can be changed from one form to another, but it cannot be created or destroyed; the total amount of energy and matter in the universe remains constant. The energy of a system changes if heat is transferred to or from it or work is done by it. Enthalpy is defined as H = E + PV. For a change, ΔH = ΔE + Δ(PV); at constant pressure ΔH = ΔE + PΔV. Since the heat absorbed at constant pressure is qp = ΔE + PΔV, it follows that ΔH = qp, i.e., the enthalpy change equals the heat transferred at constant pressure.

  12. Define entropy. State the Clausius and Kelvin-Planck statements of the second law of thermodynamics.
    Topic: Laws of Thermodynamics

    Entropy (S) is a measure of the disorder of a system; in general, greater disorder means greater entropy. It is a state function, and ΔS = Sfinal − Sinitial. Clausius statement: it is impossible to construct a device that operates in a cycle and whose sole effect is to transfer heat from a cooler body to a hotter body. Kelvin-Planck statement: it is impossible to construct a device that operates in a cycle and produces no other effects than the performance of work and the exchange of heat with a single reservoir. The two statements are equivalent: violation of one leads to violation of the other.

  13. State the third law of thermodynamics and discuss its importance.
    Topic: Laws of Thermodynamics

    The third law states that the entropy of a perfect crystalline substance is zero at absolute zero (T = 0 K); it is also known as the Nernst theorem, and it implies that it is impossible to reach a temperature of absolute zero. Its importance is that it allows absolute entropies of substances to be calculated, helps in calculating thermodynamic properties, helps in measuring chemical affinity, explains the behavior of solids at very low temperatures, and helps in analyzing chemical and phase equilibrium.

  14. What is a thermochemical equation? Illustrate with the vaporization of water.
    Topic: Thermochemistry and Heat Capacity

    A thermochemical equation defines the actual change taking place in terms of the formulas of the substances and their physical states (temperature, pressure, and whether solid, liquid or gaseous). For example, the vaporization of water at its normal boiling point is written H2O(l, 373 K, 1 atm) → H2O(g, 373 K, 1 atm), ΔH = 40.7 kJ mol−1, where 40.7 kJ mol−1 is the enthalpy (heat) of vaporization of liquid water. Any thermodynamic quantity such as ΔH associated with a thermochemical equation always refers to the number of moles of substances shown in the equation, and for reactions in solution the concentrations of the dissolved species must also be specified.

  15. Define standard enthalpy of formation and give the general expression for the standard enthalpy change of a reaction.
    Topic: Thermochemistry and Heat Capacity

    The standard enthalpy of formation of a compound is the heat associated with the formation of one mole of the compound from its elements in their standard states. The standard enthalpy change of a reaction is ΔH° = ΣHf°(products) − ΣHf°(reactants). For H2(g) + ½O2(g) → H2O(l), ΔH = −286 kJ; setting the enthalpy of the elements to zero gives Hf° of water = −286 kJ mol−1 at 298 K.

  16. State Hess' law and use it to calculate ΔH° for C(graphite) → C(diamond) from the heats of combustion of graphite (−393.51 kJ mol−1) and diamond (−395.40 kJ mol−1).
    Topic: Thermochemistry and Heat Capacity • 5 marks marks

    Hess' law states that the enthalpy of a given chemical reaction is constant, regardless of whether the reaction happens in one step or in many steps; if a chemical equation can be written as the sum of several other equations, its enthalpy change equals the sum of their enthalpy changes. Given C(graphite) + O2(g) → CO2(g), ΔH° = −393.51 kJ mol−1, and C(diamond) + O2(g) → CO2(g), ΔH° = −395.40 kJ mol−1, subtracting the second equation from the first gives C(graphite) → C(diamond) with ΔH° = −393.51 − (−395.40) = +1.89 kJ mol−1. The law works because enthalpy is a state function.

  17. What is calorimetry? Describe the working of a bomb calorimeter.
    Topic: Thermochemistry and Heat Capacity

    Calorimetry is the measurement of the heat q that flows into or out of a reaction vessel; q is numerically equal to ΔH only under the special condition of constant pressure, with reactants and products at the same temperature. In a bomb calorimeter, combustion is carried out in a strong vessel (the bomb) in oxygen above atmospheric pressure, so the process takes place at constant volume and the vessel withstands the high pressure of the confined explosion; the reaction is started by discharging a capacitor through a thin wire. Bomb calorimetry is used to determine heats of combustion, which are essential for finding standard enthalpies of formation.

  18. Define heat capacity, molar heat capacity and specific heat capacity. Calculate the heat required to raise the temperature of 100 g of water by 10 K (specific heat of water = 4.184 J g−1 K−1).
    Topic: Thermochemistry and Heat Capacity

    Heat capacity is the amount of heat needed to raise the temperature of a defined system by one degree. The molar heat capacity is the heat needed to raise the temperature of one mole of a substance by one degree, while the specific heat capacity is the heat needed to raise the temperature of one gram of a substance by one degree (for water, 4.184 J g−1 K−1). The quantity of heat is q = m × c × ΔT. For 100 g of water raised by 10 K: q = 100 × 4.184 × 10 = 4184 J.

  19. Explain specific latent heat of vaporization and specific latent heat of fusion.
    Topic: Thermochemistry and Heat Capacity

    Specific latent heat is the quantity of heat energy required to change the state of one unit of weight (mass) of a substance with no change of temperature. The specific latent heat of vaporization applies to the change from liquid to gas (or gas to liquid), and the specific latent heat of fusion applies to the change from solid to liquid (or liquid to solid).

  20. Differentiate between reversible and irreversible processes. List the factors that cause irreversibility.
    Topic: Processes and Spontaneity

    A reversible process can be reversed without leaving any trace on the surroundings; both system and surroundings return to their initial states, and the system passes through a series of equilibrium states. Reversible processes do not occur in nature and are only idealizations. An irreversible process cannot be reversed in this way. Factors that cause irreversibility include friction, unrestrained expansion and compression, mixing, heat transfer through a finite temperature difference, inelastic deformation and chemical reactions; examples are combustion, diffusion, free expansion and throttling.

  21. What is a spontaneous process? Give the entropy criterion for spontaneity and any three examples.
    Topic: Processes and Spontaneity

    A spontaneous process is capable of proceeding in a given direction without needing to be driven by an outside source of energy; spontaneous changes proceed when left to themselves. For a spontaneous process ΔSuniverse = ΔSsystem + ΔSsurroundings > 0, while for an equilibrium process ΔSuniverse = 0. Spontaneity does not imply speed: the decay of diamond into graphite is spontaneous but takes millions of years, because the rate depends on the kinetics, not on spontaneity. Examples of spontaneous processes are a smell diffusing in a room, ice melting in lukewarm water, salt dissolving in water and iron rusting.

  22. Define chemical equilibrium and write the equilibrium constant expression for aA + bB ⇌ cC + dD. State the important rules for using Kc.
    Topic: Chemical Equilibrium and Gibbs Free Energy

    Equilibrium is the state in which the rate of the forward reaction equals the rate of the reverse reaction, so all reactant and product concentrations remain constant. For aA + bB ⇌ cC + dD, Kc = [C]c[D]d / [A]a[B]b. Rules: Kc is constant only for a specific reaction at a specific temperature and changes if the temperature changes; pure solids and pure liquids (including solvents) are not included in the expression; Kc is often written without units; and the equation must be balanced with the lowest possible integer coefficients.

  23. What is the reaction quotient Q? How is it used to predict the direction of a reaction?
    Topic: Chemical Equilibrium and Gibbs Free Energy

    The reaction quotient Q has the same form as the equilibrium constant but uses the amounts present at a given time, whether or not the system is at equilibrium. If Q = K, the system is already at equilibrium and concentrations will not change. If Q > K, there is too much product, so the reaction proceeds to the left (reverse direction) to reach equilibrium. If Q < K, there is too little product, so the reaction proceeds to the right (forward direction) to reach equilibrium.

  24. Define Gibbs free energy and explain how the sign of ΔG predicts spontaneity and the size of K.
    Topic: Chemical Equilibrium and Gibbs Free Energy

    The Gibbs free energy is G = H − TS. At constant temperature, ΔG = ΔH − TΔS, so ΔG measures the balance between the enthalpy and entropy driving forces of a reaction. If ΔG is negative, the forward reaction is spontaneous and the products dominate at equilibrium (K is large); if ΔG is positive, the reverse reaction is spontaneous and the reactants dominate (K is small); if ΔG = 0, the system is at equilibrium. Under standard conditions, ΔG° = ΔH° − TΔS° and ΔG° = −RT ln K.

  25. State the van't Hoff equation and explain the effect of temperature on K for endothermic and exothermic reactions.
    Topic: Chemical Equilibrium and Gibbs Free Energy

    The van't Hoff equation describes the temperature dependence of the equilibrium constant. A plot of ln K against 1/T at constant pressure is a straight line with slope −ΔH/R. For an endothermic reaction the slope is negative, so as temperature increases the equilibrium constant increases. For an exothermic reaction the slope is positive, so as temperature increases the equilibrium constant decreases. The equation may be derived from the Gibbs-Helmholtz equation, which gives the temperature dependence of the Gibbs free energy.

  26. Explain Le Chatelier's principle in terms of changes in concentration, pressure and temperature.
    Topic: Chemical Equilibrium and Gibbs Free Energy • 5 marks marks

    Le Chatelier's principle: if a dynamic equilibrium is disturbed by changing the conditions, the position of equilibrium moves to counteract the change. Concentration: increasing the concentration of a reacting species shifts the equilibrium so that the species is consumed (Qc becomes greater than Kc and the net change is in the reverse direction if a product is added). Pressure (gases only): increasing the pressure shifts the equilibrium towards the side with fewer gas molecules; decreasing the pressure shifts it towards more gas molecules; adding an inert gas at constant volume causes no shift. Temperature: increasing the temperature favors the endothermic reaction (the system absorbs the extra heat), while decreasing the temperature favors the exothermic reaction; for an exothermic forward reaction, raising the temperature moves the equilibrium to the left.

  27. Define surface tension and explain its molecular origin. How is viscosity measured with an Ostwald viscometer?
    Topic: Properties of Liquids and Water

    Surface tension is the energy required to increase the surface area of a liquid by a unit amount. It results from an imbalance of intermolecular attractive (cohesive) forces: a molecule in the bulk liquid experiences cohesive forces in all directions, whereas a molecule at the surface experiences only net inward cohesive forces. The surface molecules therefore have a higher energy state, and the liquid tries to maintain a minimum surface area. Viscosity is a liquid's resistance to flow, arising from friction between its molecules. In an Ostwald viscometer, which consists of two reservoir bulbs and a capillary tube, the time taken for the liquid to flow between two graduated marks is measured, and the viscosity is calculated from ν = Kt, where K is found using a liquid of known viscosity and density such as water.

  28. Define refractive index. Which factors affect it, and how does temperature change it?
    Topic: Properties of Liquids and Water

    The refractive index is the ratio of the speed of light in a medium relative to its speed in a vacuum, calculated as n = sin(θi) / sin(θr) from the angles of incidence and refraction. It is affected by two factors: temperature and the wavelength of light. A higher temperature makes the liquid less dense and less viscous, so light travels faster and the refractive index is smaller; a lower temperature gives a larger refractive index. The refractive index also varies with wavelength, so monochromatic light is used; the sodium D line at 598 nm is the wavelength most frequently used for a refractometer.

  29. Define dipole moment. Why is CO2 non-polar while H2O is polar, although both contain polar bonds?
    Topic: Properties of Liquids and Water

    When atoms in a molecule share electrons unequally, a dipole moment is created; it is the magnitude of the charge Q at either end of the dipole times the distance r between the charges, μ = Q × r. The larger the difference in electronegativities of the bonded atoms, the larger the dipole moment. CO2 contains polar C=O bonds, but the molecule is linear and completely symmetric, so the two bond dipoles cancel and the overall dipole moment is zero; the molecule is non-polar. H2O is bent (bond angle 104.5°), oxygen carries a partial negative charge and the hydrogens partial positive charges, the dipoles do not cancel, and the molecule is polar.

  30. Discuss the unusual properties of water. Why does ice float on liquid water?
    Topic: Properties of Liquids and Water

    Water has an unusually high boiling point for its small molecular weight because extra energy is needed to break its hydrogen bonds; its heat of vaporization is high (41 kJ/mol), and besides mercury it has the highest surface tension of all liquids, both due to hydrogen bonding. Water becomes denser on cooling only until 4 °C; below that it becomes less dense. On freezing, the molecules arrange into an open crystalline hexagonal structure, the volume increases by about 9%, and ice becomes less dense than liquid water, so ice floats. This floating ice insulates the water below, allowing aquatic ecosystems to survive in winter.

  31. What are colligative properties? Write the four colligative properties with their formulas.
    Topic: Solutions and Colligative Properties • 3 marks marks

    Colligative properties are properties of solutions that depend only on the number (concentration) of solute particles present and not on their identity or type. The four colligative properties are: (1) vapor pressure lowering, ΔP = Xsolute × P°; (2) freezing point depression, ΔTf = Kf × m; (3) boiling point elevation, ΔTb = Kb × m; and (4) osmotic pressure, Π = nRT/V. Each property is proportional to the solute concentration multiplied by a constant, and their measurement allows molar masses of solutes to be determined.

  32. State Raoult's law and explain why adding a solute lowers the vapor pressure of a solvent.
    Topic: Solutions and Colligative Properties

    Raoult's law states that the vapor pressure of a solvent above a solution is equal to the vapor pressure of the pure solvent at the same temperature scaled by the mole fraction of the solvent present: Psolution = Xsolvent × P°solvent. Adding a solute lowers the vapor pressure because the solute particles take up space at the surface, so fewer solvent molecules can escape into the gas phase. The vapor pressure lowering is directly proportional to the mole fraction of the solute, ΔP = Xsolute × P°. Raoult's law works for ideal solutions and fairly well for the solvent in dilute solutions.

  33. Define osmosis and osmotic pressure, and give the formula for osmotic pressure.
    Topic: Solutions and Colligative Properties

    Osmosis is the diffusion of a fluid through a semipermeable membrane, which allows only solvent molecules to pass and does not let the solute pass through. The osmotic pressure of a solution is the pressure difference needed to stop the flow of solvent across the membrane. It is proportional to the molar concentration of the solute particles and is given by Π = nRT/V, where n is the number of moles of solute, V the volume of solution, R the ideal gas constant (0.0821 L atm mol−1 K−1) and T the temperature in Kelvin.

  34. Differentiate between ideal and non-ideal solutions with examples.
    Topic: Solutions and Colligative Properties

    An ideal solution obeys Raoult's law at all compositions and temperatures; its enthalpy of solution is zero (ΔHsolution = 0), so the change in Gibbs energy on mixing is determined solely by the entropy of mixing. It is formed from components of identical molecular size and structure whose intermolecular attractions A–A, B–B and A–B are almost the same, e.g., n-hexane and n-heptane, and CCl4 and SiCl4. A non-ideal (real) solution deviates from Raoult's law over the range of composition, with ΔHsolution greater or less than zero; on increasing dilution, a non-ideal solution tends to become ideal.
