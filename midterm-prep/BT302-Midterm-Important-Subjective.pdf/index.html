  1. Define immunology and antigen. Also differentiate between antigen, immunogen and hapten.
    Topic: Immune System Overview & Basic Definitions

    Immunology is the study of the immune system, including its functions, disorders, and responses to pathogens and antigens. An antigen is a substance that reacts with the products of a specific immune response — i.e. a toxin or other foreign substance which induces an immune response in the body, especially the production of antibodies (e.g. proteins, peptides, carbohydrates). An immunogen is a substance that induces a specific immune response. A hapten is a substance that is non-immunogenic by itself but can react with the products of a specific immune response (it becomes immunogenic when attached to a carrier).

  2. Define heptane.
    Topic: Immune System Overview & Basic Definitions

    Heptane is a hydrocarbon compound with the chemical formula C7H16. It is a colorless, flammable, volatile liquid that is commonly used as a solvent in laboratories and industrial processes.

  3. Define phylaxis.
    Topic: Immune System Overview & Basic Definitions

    Phylaxis refers to the protection against infection and disease. It is a general term used to describe the body's defensive mechanisms, including the immune system's response to prevent and combat pathogens.

  4. If a new virus or bacteria enters the body of a grown man, which immune system will overcome the disease? Explain briefly.
    Topic: Immune System Overview & Basic Definitions

    The adaptive immune system, particularly involving B cells and T cells, will overcome the disease. B cells produce antibodies targeting the pathogen, while T cells destroy infected cells. This response is specific to the new virus or bacteria, ensuring effective elimination and long-term immunity.

  5. If a newborn baby acquires an infection, which type of immune system helps him to overcome the infection? Briefly explain.
    Topic: Immune System Overview & Basic Definitions

    In newborns, the innate immune system plays a crucial role in combating infections. This system provides immediate, non-specific defense mechanisms against pathogens. Components such as skin barriers, mucous membranes, phagocytes, and natural killer cells act as the first line of defense, helping to prevent the entry and spread of pathogens.

  6. Define humoral and cellular (cell-mediated) immunity.
    Topic: Immune System Overview & Basic Definitions

    Humoral immunity is immunity brought about by the activation of B lymphocytes (B cells), which produce antibodies. Cellular (cell-mediated) immunity is immunity brought about by the activation of T lymphocytes (T cells).

  7. Explain briefly pathogen-associated molecular patterns (PAMPs).
    Topic: Immune System Overview & Basic Definitions

    The antigenic determinants recognized by the innate immune system are purely non-specific in nature, due to the non-specific attribute of the innate immune system. These determinants are present on the surface of pathogens in the form of various molecular configurations called pathogen-associated molecular patterns (PAMPs). PAMPs include cell membrane proteins, lipopolysaccharide and flagellar proteins. For these antigenic determinants, immune cells carry pattern recognition receptors (PRRs), which bind non-specifically with the corresponding PAMPs in order to generate an immune response.

  8. Describe the anatomical (physical) barriers of the immune system.
    Topic: Barriers, Innate Immunity & Phagocytosis

    Anatomical barriers include the skin and squamous epithelial membranes, which provide physical protection. Mechanical factors such as desquamation, peristalsis, ciliary movement, and the flushing actions of tears and saliva remove microbes. Mucus in the respiratory and gastrointestinal tracts traps pathogens. Chemical factors include fatty acids in the skin, lysozymes, phospholipases, antimicrobial peptides (defensins), low pH in sweat and GIT secretions, and surfactants in the lungs. Normal flora in the skin and GIT prevent bacterial colonization by secreting toxins and competing for nutrients. Physical barriers of the innate immune system listed in the current paper: skin and mucous membranes prevent pathogen entry; respiratory tract cilia and mucus trap and remove pathogens; gastrointestinal tract acidity and enzymes kill pathogens; the blood-brain barrier restricts pathogen entry into the central nervous system; eye and ear protective mechanisms prevent pathogen entry.

  9. Name and describe the cells of the cellular barrier.
    Topic: Barriers, Innate Immunity & Phagocytosis

    Cells of the cellular barrier are key components of the immune system's first line of defense: 1. Neutrophils: Rapidly respond to infections, phagocytose bacteria, release antimicrobials. 2. Macrophages: Phagocytose pathogens, present antigens, secrete cytokines. 3. Dendritic Cells: Capture and present antigens to T cells. 4. NK Cells: Destroy infected/cancerous cells by inducing apoptosis. 5. Eosinophils: Combat parasitic infections, participate in allergic reactions.

  10. Define phagocytosis and explain the process of phagocytosis.
    Topic: Barriers, Innate Immunity & Phagocytosis

    Phagocytosis is a vital cellular process in which specialized cells called phagocytes engulf and digest solid particles like bacteria, microorganisms and cellular debris. It is essential for eliminating pathogens, removing dead cells and debris, recycling cellular components and initiating immune responses. The process involves several stages: 1. Chemotaxis / Recognition: Phagocytes move towards infection sites due to chemical signals and recognize the particle or microorganism. 2. Attachment: Phagocytes adhere to foreign particles using specific receptors. 3. Engulfment: Phagocytes extend their membrane (pseudopodia) to surround the particle, forming a phagosome. 4. Phagosome Formation: The phagosome matures and fuses with lysosomes (forming a phagolysosome). 5. Digestion: Lysosomal enzymes break down and degrade the phagosome's contents. 6. Exocytosis: Indigestible material is expelled from the cell. Phagocytic cells include neutrophils, macrophages, dendritic cells and monocytes.

  11. Write a note on oxygen-dependent and oxygen-independent intracellular killing.
    Topic: Barriers, Innate Immunity & Phagocytosis

    Intracellular killing of bacteria occurs through two main methods: oxygen-independent killing, in which phagocytes release bactericidal enzymes, and oxygen-dependent killing, which involves a respiratory burst. In the latter, increased glucose and oxygen consumption after phagocytosis leads to the production of oxygen-containing bactericidal radicals, including halide ions, which kill bacteria.

  12. What is the role of the immune system against intracellular pathogens?
    Topic: Barriers, Innate Immunity & Phagocytosis

    The immune system employs distinct strategies to combat intracellular pathogens. In the innate response, granulocytes — neutrophils rapidly respond to infections, while basophils and mast cells release histamine during allergic reactions; megakaryocytes produce platelets essential for blood clotting and wound healing. In combating intracellular pathogens, cell-mediated immunity led by T lymphocytes is crucial. Cytotoxic T lymphocytes recognize antigens presented by infected cells' class I MHC molecules, controlling cytosolic pathogens like viruses, while helper T lymphocytes recognize antigens from vesicular pathogens such as intracellular bacteria.

  13. Name the cells which are involved in the immune system.
    Topic: Cells of the Immune System

    The cells involved in the immune system are: 1. T cells (CD4+ and CD8+), 2. B cells, 3. Natural Killer (NK) cells, 4. Macrophages, 5. Dendritic cells, 6. Neutrophils, 7. Eosinophils, 8. Basophils and Mast cells, 9. Plasma cells. Their main roles (past-paper notes): basophils release histamine; eosinophils kill antibody-coated parasites; neutrophils phagocytose antibody-coated pathogens; mast cells release histamine from damaged cells; monocytes develop into macrophages; macrophages engulf and digest microorganisms; dendritic cells present antigens to T cells; B cells differentiate into antibody-producing cells; plasma cells secrete antibodies; T cells kill virus-infected cells and regulate the activities of other white blood cells; natural killer cells kill virus-infected and cancerous cells.

  14. What are lymphocytes? Write their types and functions.
    Topic: Cells of the Immune System

    Lymphocytes are a type of white blood cell that plays a crucial role in the immune system. There are two main types: 1. B cells (B lymphocytes): Produce antibodies to recognize and bind to specific antigens; activate humoral immunity; provide long-term immunity. 2. T cells (T lymphocytes): Recognize and kill infected cells or foreign substances; activate cell-mediated immunity; regulate immune responses. Functions of lymphocytes: (1) Recognition and elimination of pathogens; (2) Production of antibodies (B cells); (3) Activation of immune responses (T cells); (4) Regulation of immune responses (T cells); (5) Development of immunological memory (B cells and T cells); (6) Surveillance and monitoring of the body for infections and cancer (T cells).

  15. Which cells are produced by the myeloid lineage (myeloid stem cells)? (Marks: 5)
    Topic: Cells of the Immune System

    Myeloid stem cells give rise to various types of blood cells, including: 1. Erythrocytes (Red Blood Cells) — transport oxygen and carbon dioxide. 2. Megakaryocytes (Platelets) — blood clotting and wound repair. 3. Granulocytes: Neutrophils (phagocytosis and killing of bacteria); Eosinophils (combat parasitic infections and participate in allergic reactions); Basophils (release histamine and other mediators of inflammation). 4. Monocytes — differentiate into macrophages and dendritic cells, which are involved in phagocytosis and antigen presentation. These cells play crucial roles in immune responses, inflammation, and blood cell production.

  16. Write the functions of granulocytes and megakaryocytes.
    Topic: Cells of the Immune System

    Megakaryocytes are large bone marrow cells responsible for platelet production. They serve as the source of platelets, which are vital for blood clotting (hemostasis), wound healing, and modulating immune responses by interacting with other immune cells. Granulocytes are a type of white blood cell with granules in their cytoplasm. They include: 1. Neutrophils: First responders to infection; engulf and destroy bacteria and fungi by phagocytosis; crucial for innate immunity. 2. Eosinophils: Involved in combating parasites and in allergic reactions. 3. Basophils: Involved in allergic responses; release histamine, contributing to inflammation and allergy symptoms. Mast cells (a related cell type) are cells of the immune system whose role is to release histamine; they release histamine during inflammation and allergic reactions and are important for defense against pathogens and allergic responses.

  17. What are effector helper T cells?
    Topic: Cells of the Immune System

    Effector helper T cells are those cells which stimulate the responses of other cells — phagocytic macrophages, B cells, and cytotoxic T cells are all stimulated by them.

  18. What is the function of effector B cells?
    Topic: Cells of the Immune System

    Effector B cells, also called plasma cells (plasmocytes), are white blood cells that secrete large volumes of antibodies. They are transported by the blood plasma and the lymphatic system. Effector cells are the relatively short-lived cells that defend the body in an immune response; when naive lymphocytes are stimulated by their specific antigen, they proliferate and differentiate — most become effector cells which function and then die, while others become long-lived memory cells.

  19. Write a note on memory cells and natural killer (NK) cells. (Marks: 3)
    Topic: Cells of the Immune System

    Memory cells: When naive lymphocytes are stimulated by their specific antigen, they proliferate and differentiate. Most become effector cells, which function and then die, while others become long-lived memory cells. Natural killer (NK) cells (also known as K cells and killer cells) are a type of lymphocyte (a white blood cell) and a component of the innate immune system. NK cells play a major role in the host rejection of both tumours and virally infected cells.

  20. What are secondary lymphoid organs?
    Topic: Lymphoid Organs & Lymphocyte Recirculation

    Secondary lymphoid organs are lymphoid tissues where immune responses are initiated and amplified. They include the lymph nodes, spleen, and mucosal-associated lymphoid tissues (MALT), including gut-associated lymphoid tissue (GALT), Peyer's patches, tonsils and lymphoid follicles. They trap antigens and facilitate interaction between mature lymphocytes and antigens, playing a crucial role in the adaptive immune response by facilitating immune cell activation and proliferation.

  21. What is lymphocyte recirculation? (Marks: 3)
    Topic: Lymphoid Organs & Lymphocyte Recirculation

    Lymphocyte recirculation is the process by which lymphocytes, including T cells and B cells, constantly migrate (continuously circulate) between the blood and lymphoid tissues/organs. This movement facilitates immune surveillance and responses throughout the body and allows a rapid response. Lymphocytes exit the bloodstream into lymphoid organs like lymph nodes, spleen, and Peyer's patches via high endothelial venules. Within these tissues, they encounter antigens, undergo activation, and differentiate into effector cells. After completing their immune functions, lymphocytes re-enter circulation through lymphatic vessels, allowing them to migrate to other sites to continue immune surveillance and responses. Recirculation is regulated by adhesion molecules and chemokines, which control lymphocyte migration and localization.

  22. Write the functions of lymph nodes. (Marks: 5)
    Topic: Lymphoid Organs & Lymphocyte Recirculation

    Functions of lymph nodes: They form the first line of response to antigens. The secondary follicle (germinal center) is the site of B-cell proliferation, mutation and differentiation. Specificity is high. More than 90% of B cells die through apoptosis. After antigen stimulation, lymphocyte numbers increase up to 50 times in the efferent lymphatic vessel. Enlargement of lymph nodes is called lymphadenopathy.

  23. Write the functions of the spleen.
    Topic: Lymphoid Organs & Lymphocyte Recirculation

    Functions of the spleen: It filters out older RBCs. It responds to antigens in the circulatory system. It produces activated B cells.

  24. Write the functions of the thymus.
    Topic: Lymphoid Organs & Lymphocyte Recirculation

    Functions of the thymus: It takes in immature T cells and puts out mature (immunocompetent) T cells. It increases the diversity of T cells through selection.

  25. Differentiate between T-dependent and T-independent antigens.
    Topic: Antigens, Immunogens & Adjuvants

    T-cell dependent antigens: These are antigens which need the help of T-cells for mounting an immune response; otherwise they cannot. T-dependent antigens require the help of T-cells for activation of B-cells. Such antigens are protein in nature. Moreover, these antigens carry a variety of antigenic determinants (epitopes) with few copies. T-cell independent antigens: These are antigens which can directly activate B-cells without the help of T-cells. Such antigens are non-protein and usually polysaccharides in nature. These antigens carry the same kind of epitopes in polymeric form. Moreover, such antigens are most resistant to degradation, and they induce only limited immune responses.

  26. What should be the chemical nature of a good immunogen?
    Topic: Antigens, Immunogens & Adjuvants

    A good immunogen should be complex and large, typically with a molecular weight greater than 10,000 daltons, to ensure it can be effectively recognized and processed by the immune system. It should be foreign to the host and chemically diverse, with multiple epitopes to elicit a strong immune response. Immunogens must also be stable enough to be recognized but degradable by antigen-presenting cells for presentation on MHC molecules. Chemically, immunogens — which induce immune responses — are primarily proteins or large polysaccharides. They can also include lipoproteins, lipopolysaccharides, or nucleoproteins. Proteins are strong antigens due to their complex structure. Carbohydrates, nucleic acids, polypeptides, hormones, and lipids vary in immunogenicity.

  27. Describe the two types of adjuvants.
    Topic: Antigens, Immunogens & Adjuvants

    Adjuvants are substances that enhance (potentiate) the immune response to weak antigens. Two types are: 1. Freund's adjuvant: Freund's Complete Adjuvant is a water-in-oil emulsion containing antigen with heat-killed Mycobacterium tuberculosis; it stimulates both humoral and cell-mediated immune responses. Freund's Incomplete Adjuvant is also a water-in-oil emulsion containing antigen but without the heat-killed Mycobacterium tuberculosis; it is used as a booster antigen dose (mixed in equal parts with the antigen). 2. Alum (chemical) adjuvant: Aluminum salts (alum), like those in DTP vaccines, function by gradually releasing the antigen, increasing interaction with Toll-like receptors (TLRs) and activating mononuclear phagocytes. This prompts heightened cytokine secretion, bolstering the immune response and inducing antibody production and T-cell activation. (Biological adjuvants, derived from various bacteria, stimulate the immune system through bacterial components and augment the response to vaccine antigens.)

  28. What is the role of adjuvants in polyclonal antibody production? (Marks: 5)
    Topic: Antigens, Immunogens & Adjuvants

    Adjuvants are crucial in polyclonal antibody production as they enhance the body's immune response to antigens. For the production of polyclonal antibodies, the adjuvants used are: (1) Freund's Complete Adjuvant, and (2) Freund's Incomplete Adjuvant. Freund's Complete Adjuvant is a water-in-oil emulsion containing antigens with heat-killed Mycobacterium tuberculosis, and it stimulates both humoral and cell-mediated immune responses for antibody production, giving a robust reaction. Freund's Incomplete Adjuvant is also a water-in-oil emulsion containing antigens without heat-killed Mycobacterium tuberculosis, and is used as a booster antigen dose. However, adjuvants must be chosen carefully to balance immune stimulation and potential toxicity, ensuring optimal antibody production without adverse effects.

  29. What are polyclonal antibodies?
    Topic: Antigens, Immunogens & Adjuvants

    Polyclonal antibodies are sourced from multiple antibody-producing B lymphocytes. They exhibit specificity to multiple epitopes on the same antigen and are obtained from serum containing antibodies with diverse affinities. Mainly belonging to the IgG class, polyclonal antibodies are valuable tools in research and diagnostics due to their ability to recognize various antigenic sites, enhancing the detection and quantification of target molecules in biological samples.

  30. What type of immune response is elicited by alloantigens?
    Topic: Antigens, Immunogens & Adjuvants

    Alloantigens elicit both cell-mediated and humoral immune responses.

  31. Explain the basic structure of antibodies.
    Topic: Antibodies — Structure, Classes & Diversity

    Antibodies consist of two identical light (L) chains and two identical heavy (H) chains, forming a heterodimer (Y-shaped) structure. Each chain has variable (V) and constant (C) regions. The variable regions determine antigen specificity and contain complementarity-determining regions (CDRs) for antigen binding; the two identical antigen-binding sites are formed at the tips. Constant regions maintain sequence consistency within antibody classes and facilitate carbohydrate attachment. A hinge region, formed by the two heavy chains along with the Fc (tail) region, provides flexibility, allowing the antibody to change shape upon antigen binding and improving the efficiency of antigen binding and cross-linking. Heavy chains typically have four domains, while light chains have two, each with an intra-chain disulfide bond. Oligosaccharides attached to the CH2 domain enhance stability and modulate immune responses.

  32. On which basis are antibodies divided into types? Explain the types (classes) and subtypes of antibodies.
    Topic: Antibodies — Structure, Classes & Diversity

    Antibodies are divided into types (classes) based on the structure of their heavy chain constant region and their function. The five main classes are: 1. IgG: Monomer; provides long-term immunity and crosses the placenta; the most common antibody class. Subtypes: IgG1, IgG2, IgG3, IgG4. 2. IgA: Usually a dimer; protects mucosal surfaces and is found in secretions (local immunity). Subtypes: IgA1, IgA2. 3. IgM: Pentamer; the first antibody produced in the initial (primary) immune response; activates the complement system. No subtypes. 4. IgE: Monomer; involved in allergic reactions and defense against parasites. No subtypes. 5. IgD: Monomer; expressed on mature B cells and acts as a receptor on naive B cells; activates antibody production. No subtypes.

  33. What are the characteristics of antibodies?
    Topic: Antibodies — Structure, Classes & Diversity

    Antibodies have several characteristics: 1. Specificity for antigens: They recognize specific epitopes. 2. Affinity for antigens: The strength of antibody-antigen binding. 3. Valence: The number of antigen-binding sites, which determines antibody functionality. 4. Isotype (class) and subclass: Defines antibody function and localization. 5. Idiotype: The unique epitope recognition that determines antibody specificity. In addition: antibodies are present on B-cell membranes and secreted by plasma cells, conferring antigenic specificity and leading to B-cell proliferation; they are effectors of humoral immunity, circulating in the blood and neutralizing or marking antigens for elimination; they share structural features, bind to antigens, and participate in the immune response; and they show heterogeneity, being produced in response to multiple epitopes on complex antigens.

  34. Briefly describe the sub-regions of the variable region of an antibody.
    Topic: Antibodies — Structure, Classes & Diversity

    The variable region of antibodies, present on both heavy and light chains, has two sub-regions: 1. Hypervariable regions (HVR): Characterized by variability in amino acid sequences; they form the complementarity-determining regions (CDRs). 2. Framework regions: Located between the hypervariable regions; they group and subgroup immunoglobulins and are products of different variable region genes.

  35. How are the fragments of antibodies obtained?
    Topic: Antibodies — Structure, Classes & Diversity

    Antibody fragments are obtained through enzyme digestion. Papain digestion of immunoglobulin G (IgG) produces two Fab fragments (each of 45,000 molecular weight) that bind antigens, and one Fc fragment (50,000 molecular weight) that does not bind antigens but crystallizes during cold storage. Pepsin digestion further separates the antigen-binding properties from the rest of the antibody, isolating the parts responsible for binding antigens from other segments. These processes allow the study and utilization of specific antibody functions.

  36. Describe the process of antibody formation. (Marks: 5)
    Topic: Antibodies — Structure, Classes & Diversity

    Antibody formation takes place in the following steps: 1. Antigen presentation to B cells: Antigens are processed and presented to B cells, which recognize them through their surface-bound antibodies. 2. Activation and proliferation of B cells: Activated B cells multiply and differentiate into plasma cells, which produce antibodies. 3. Differentiation into plasma cells: B cells mature into plasma cells, which specialize in antibody production. 4. Class switching and affinity maturation: B cells can switch to producing different antibody classes (e.g. IgG, IgA) and undergo affinity maturation to produce high-affinity antibodies. 5. Antibody production and secretion: Plasma cells produce and secrete antibodies, which enter the bloodstream and other bodily fluids.

  37. What is affinity maturation? (Marks: 3)
    Topic: Antibodies — Structure, Classes & Diversity

    Affinity maturation is the process of increasing the affinity of antibodies for antigens. The affinity of antibodies depends upon the dosage of antigen; a low dosage of antigen causes an increase in the affinity of antibodies, which is called affinity maturation. It occurs through somatic hypermutation and selection, and results in higher antibody efficacy and specificity.

  38. Write the role of somatic hypermutation in the diversity of antibodies.
    Topic: Antibodies — Structure, Classes & Diversity

    Somatic hypermutation is pivotal for antibody diversity, enabling the creation of antibodies with varied specificities against different pathogens. This process accelerates following exposure to infections, further enhancing antibody diversity. It occurs rapidly, approximately a million times faster than usual mutations. The protein AID particularly aids in altering the DNA of antibody genes, facilitating this diversification without impacting vital genetic components.

  39. What is V(D)J recombination?
    Topic: Antibodies — Structure, Classes & Diversity

    V(D)J recombination is a vital process in generating antibody diversity. It involves the joining of various gene segments, such as V and J in light chain genes and V, D, and J in heavy chain genes. Recombination is regulated by Recombination Signal Sequences (RSS), consisting of conserved heptamer and nonamer sequences separated by one-turn and two-turn signals. Enzymes like RAG1 and RAG2 facilitate this recombination process, which is crucial for the immune system's functionality.

  40. How do B cells form IgM antibody? Write about the IgM immunoglobulin. (Marks: 5)
    Topic: Antibodies — Structure, Classes & Diversity

    IgM is the first class of antibody to appear on the surface of a developing B cell, and it is the major class secreted in the early stages of a primary antibody response. In its secreted form, IgM is a pentamer, and a J chain is required for pentamer formation. The binding of an antigen to a single secreted pentameric IgM molecule can activate the complement system, which can either mark the pathogen for phagocytosis or kill it directly.

  41. What is the function of the Fc region of an antibody? (Marks: 2)
    Topic: Antibodies — Structure, Classes & Diversity

    IgG is the only antibody that can pass from mother to fetus. Placental cells that are in contact with the mother's blood have Fc receptors that are used to grab IgG and pass it to the fetus.

  42. Differentiate between endogenous and exogenous immunoglobulins. (Marks: 5)
    Topic: Antibodies — Structure, Classes & Diversity

    Endogenous immunoglobulins: These are produced by B cells in response to antigens, and they recognize and bind to specific epitopes. Exogenous immunoglobulins: These are administered externally (e.g. intravenous immunoglobulin) and provide immediate immunity against specific pathogens or toxins.

  43. What are monoclonal antibodies?
    Topic: Monoclonal Antibodies & Hybridoma

    The antibodies which are produced from a single antibody-producing B-cell are called monoclonal antibodies. These antibodies are also able to bind with a single and unique antigen binding site (epitope). Monoclonal antibodies mainly consist of a single subtype of IgG, for example IgG1, IgG2 and IgG3.

  44. Briefly describe the uses of monoclonal antibodies. (Marks: 5)
    Topic: Monoclonal Antibodies & Hybridoma

    Monoclonal antibodies are utilized in disease management, immune cell identification, tumor typing and analysis, cancer treatment, and cell sorting. Specific uses include: 1. Cancer treatment (e.g. rituximab): Targets cancer cells for destruction. 2. Autoimmune disease treatment (e.g. adalimumab): Blocks inflammatory pathways. 3. Infectious disease treatment (e.g. palivizumab): Neutralizes pathogens. 4. Diagnostic tests (e.g. flow cytometry): Identifies and characterizes cells / specific antigens. 5. Research applications (e.g. immunohistochemistry): Studies protein expression, function and localization. They aid in preventing, diagnosing, and treating diseases, targeting cell surface markers, classifying tumors, studying solid tumors, and separating cells using techniques like Fluorescence Activated Cell Sorting (FACS).

  45. Write a brief note on hybridoma.
    Topic: Monoclonal Antibodies & Hybridoma

    Hybridomas are created by fusing a lymphoid tumor (myeloma) cell with a normal B-lymphocyte. This hybrid cell line combines the immortality of the tumor cell with the single-specificity antibody production capability of the B-lymphocyte, enabling continuous production of monoclonal antibodies.

  46. Write the difference between T cells and B cells.
    Topic: T Cells, B Cells & Their Receptors

    T cells develop in the thymus, and B cells develop in the bone marrow (in mammals). Most lymphocytes die in the central lymphoid organs. 1. T Cells: These cells (thymus cells) are central to the adaptive immune system and are the major cellular component of cell-mediated immunity. They play various roles, such as activating other immune cells and directly attacking infected or abnormal cells. T cells can only connect to virus antigens on the outside of infected cells (i.e. they recognize antigen presented on cell surfaces). 2. B Cells: These cells (bone marrow- or bursa-derived cells) are primarily responsible for humoral immunity (relating to antibodies). They produce antibodies, which are proteins that help identify and neutralize pathogens. B cells can connect to antigens right on the surface of the invading virus or bacteria.

  47. Differentiate between B-cell receptors (BCR) and T-cell receptors (TCR).
    Topic: T Cells, B Cells & Their Receptors

    BCR (B Cell Receptor): Found on the surface of B cells; recognizes and binds to antigens directly (intact/soluble antigens); triggers B cell activation, proliferation, and antibody production; composed of membrane-bound immunoglobulins (Ig); responsible for humoral immunity; reflects the specificity of the antibodies the B cell produces. TCR (T Cell Receptor): Found on the surface of T cells; recognizes and binds to antigenic peptide fragments presented by MHC molecules on antigen-presenting cells; triggers T cell activation, proliferation, and initiation of cell-mediated immunity; composed of TCR alpha and beta chains (or gamma and delta chains); responsible for cell-mediated immunity; relies on MHC presentation.

  48. Describe the T-cell receptor (TCR) and its structure.
    Topic: T Cells, B Cells & Their Receptors

    T-Cell Receptors (TCR) are essential for antigen recognition by T cells. They primarily recognize protein antigens presented by major histocompatibility complex (MHC) molecules on nucleated cells. TCRs categorize T cells based on the MHC molecules they recognize: helper T cells bind to peptides presented by class II MHC molecules, while cytotoxic T cells bind to peptides presented by class I MHC molecules. Antigen recognition by TCRs requires presentation by MHC molecules, and their specificity is dependent on the class of MHC molecules presenting the peptide fragments. This recognition triggers T cell activation and initiation of immune responses. Structure of TCR: The T-cell receptor is a surface receptor found on T-lymphocytes. It binds to antigens presented by MHC molecules. Structurally, TCR resembles immunoglobulin and is part of the immunoglobulin superfamily. TCR is a heterodimer composed of one alpha (alpha) and one beta (beta) chain.

  49. How are cytotoxic T cells activated?
    Topic: T Cells, B Cells & Their Receptors

    Cytotoxic T cells, or CD8+ T cells, become activated when their receptors recognize antigens on infected cells presented by MHC-I molecules. This recognition triggers a signaling cascade, leading to T cell activation, proliferation, and differentiation into killer cells. These activated cytotoxic T cells then target and eliminate infected or abnormal cells through various mechanisms, ultimately helping to control infections and prevent the spread of pathogens. Cytotoxic T cells provide protection against intracellular pathogens such as viruses and some bacteria and parasites that multiply in the host cytoplasm, where they are sheltered from attack by antibodies.

  50. What is clonal selection of lymphocytes? Write its function.
    Topic: T Cells, B Cells & Their Receptors

    Definition: Clonal selection is the process by which lymphocytes with unique receptor specificity undergo activation, differentiation, and selection against non-self antigens. Function: It ensures the generation of a diverse lymphocyte repertoire capable of recognizing and responding to foreign molecules while maintaining tolerance to self-antigens, thereby facilitating effective immune responses and preventing autoimmune reactions.

  51. What is MHC? Write the difference between MHC-I and MHC-II (and the classes of MHC). (Marks: 5)
    Topic: MHC & Antigen Presentation

    The Major Histocompatibility Complex (MHC) is a group of genes encoding cell surface proteins that play a critical role in the immune response. MHC Class I: Present on all nucleated cells; presents intracellular (endogenous) antigens to CD8+ T cells (cytotoxic T cells), which kill infected cells — thus identifying infected or aberrant cells. MHC Class II: Found mainly on antigen-presenting cells; presents extracellular (exogenous) antigens to CD4+ T cells (helper T cells), which coordinate and initiate immune responses against extracellular pathogens. MHC Class III: Involved in the complement system and inflammation. MHC genes are highly polymorphic: many variations exist, allowing for greater diversity in immune responses. Peptide binding (past-paper notes): There are two main structurally and functionally distinct classes of MHC proteins — class I MHC proteins present foreign peptides to cytotoxic T cells, and class II present foreign peptides to helper T cells. The MHC groove can accommodate an extended peptide about 8-10 amino acids long; in extended conformation, the terminal amino group binds to an invariant pocket at one end of the groove and the terminal carboxyl group binds to an invariant pocket at the other end. The pockets recognize peptide backbone features common to all peptides, so each class I MHC protein can bind peptides of diverse sequence. Class II can accommodate longer peptides, usually about 13 amino acids; the peptide is not bound by its ends but is held in the groove by parts of its backbone that bind to invariant pockets formed by conserved amino acids lining the class II peptide-binding groove.

  52. What is antigen processing and presentation? Describe the process.
    Topic: MHC & Antigen Presentation

    Antigen processing and presentation are crucial steps in the immune response, involving the association of protein fragments with major histocompatibility complex (MHC) molecules for T cell recognition. Process overview: (1) Proteolysis — proteins are fragmented within cells. (2) MHC association — fragments associate with MHC molecules. (3) Expression — peptide-MHC complexes are displayed on the surface of antigen-presenting cells (APCs) like macrophages. Class I MHC: Presents degradation products from intracellular (endogenous) proteins in the cytosol; recognized by cytotoxic T cells (CD8+), leading to cell killing. Class II MHC: Presents fragments from extracellular (exogenous) proteins in intracellular compartments; recognized by helper T cells (CD4+), leading to immune activation and coordination.

  53. Differentiate between exogenous and endogenous antigen processing.
    Topic: MHC & Antigen Presentation

    Endogenous antigens originate within cells, such as viruses; they are processed via proteasomes and presented on MHC I molecules on all nucleated cells. Exogenous antigens, like bacteria, are internalized through endocytosis, processed by proteases in endosomes, and presented on MHC II molecules mainly by antigen-presenting cells like macrophages, dendritic cells, and B-cells.

  54. Write the effector functions of complement proteins.
    Topic: Complement System & Effector Mechanisms

    The secondary effector functions of complement proteins enhance immune responses: 1. Opsonization: Proteins like C3b, iC3b, and C4b tag microbes, enhancing phagocytosis by forming complexes recognized by complement receptors on phagocytes. 2. Chemotaxis: Proteins like C5a attract phagocytic cells to infection sites, activating neutrophils, basophils, and macrophages, and inducing adhesion molecule expression on endothelial cells for phagocyte migration. 3. Anaphylaxis: Proteins like C4a, C3a, and C5a trigger exaggerated immune responses by causing basophils and mast cells to degranulate, releasing mediators that lead to smooth muscle contraction, vasodilation, and bronchoconstriction.

  55. Explain neutralization against extracellular pathogens.
    Topic: Complement System & Effector Mechanisms

    Neutralization involves antibodies binding to surface antigens on pathogens, blocking their interaction with host cells. This prevents pathogens from attaching and entering host cells, neutralizes toxins they produce, and facilitates their clearance by phagocytes. Antibodies bound to pathogens also activate the complement system, leading to pathogen lysis, and promote phagocytosis by immune cells.

  56. What is ADCC?
    Topic: Complement System & Effector Mechanisms

    ADCC is Antibody-Dependent Cell-Mediated Cytotoxicity. Cells capable of cytotoxicity express Fc receptors. The antibody binds to the target cell and the cytotoxic cells bind to the Fc portion of the antibody; the antibody provides the specificity. Examples of cells capable of ADCC are macrophages, neutrophils and eosinophils.

  57. What is CFT?
    Topic: Complement System & Effector Mechanisms

    CFT stands for Complement Fixation Test, a laboratory technique used to detect the presence of specific antibodies or antigens in a patient's blood serum. It measures the ability of antibodies to fix complement, which is a part of the immune system's response to pathogens.

  58. What are cytokines? Write their types. (Marks: 5)
    Topic: Cytokines & Immune Mediators

    Cytokines are a diverse group of non-antibody proteins released by cells that act as intercellular mediators, especially in immune processes. They are low molecular weight soluble proteins (polypeptides, less than 30 kD), produced in response to microbes and other antigens. Types: 1. Monokines — produced by mononuclear phagocytes (monocytes). 2. Lymphokines — produced by activated T cells, primarily helper T cells. 3. Interleukins — cytokines made by one leukocyte and acting on other leukocytes. 4. Chemokines — cytokines with chemotactic activities.

  59. Explain the functions, mode of action and properties of cytokines.
    Topic: Cytokines & Immune Mediators

    Functions — cytokines are classified according to their biologic actions into three groups: 1. Mediators and regulators of innate immunity: Produced by activated macrophages and NK cells in response to microbial infection; they act mainly on endothelial cells and leukocytes to stimulate the early inflammatory response to microbes. 2. Mediators and regulators of acquired immunity: Produced mainly by T lymphocytes in response to specific recognition of foreign antigens; they include IL-2, IL-4, IL-5, IL-13, IFN, transforming growth factor-beta (TGF-beta) and lymphotoxin (TNF-beta). 3. Stimulators of haematopoiesis: Produced by bone marrow and leukocytes; they stimulate growth and differentiation of leukocytes — stem cell factors, IL-3, IL-7, GM-CSF. Mode of action: Autocrine — act on the same cell that has secreted the cytokine. Paracrine — act on a nearby cell. Endocrine — act on a distant cell reached through the circulation. Properties: (1) Produced by cells involved in both natural and specific immunity. (2) Mediate and regulate immune and inflammatory responses. (3) Secretion is brief and limited; they are not stored as pre-formed molecules, and synthesis is initiated by new, short-lived gene transcription. (4) Pleiotropic — a single cytokine can act on several different cell types, and different cell types can secrete the same cytokine. (5) Redundancy — similar functions can be stimulated by different cytokines. (6) They often influence the synthesis of other cytokines; they can produce cascades, or enhance or suppress production of other cytokines, exerting positive or negative regulatory mechanisms for immune and inflammatory responses. (7) They often influence the action of other cytokines — antagonistic cytokines cause opposing activities, while additive/synergistic effects occur when two or more cytokines act together. (8) They bind to specific receptors on target cells with high affinity; receptors for cytokines are heterodimers (sometimes heterotrimers) that can be grouped into families in which one subunit is common to all members of a given family. (9) Cellular responses to cytokines are generally slow (hours) and require new mRNA and protein synthesis.

  60. What are interferons?
    Topic: Cytokines & Immune Mediators

    Interferons are chemicals that interfere with the ability of viruses to attack other body cells. They are produced by dendritic cells (IFN-alpha) and fibroblasts (IFN-beta).

  61. Write the names of the chemicals that attract phagocytes to the site of infection. (Marks: 2)
    Topic: Cytokines & Immune Mediators

    The chemicals that attract phagocytes are the chemokines (chemotactic cytokines, e.g. IL-8) and complement fragments such as C5a; they draw phagocytic cells to the site of infection by chemotaxis.

  62. What are the types of hypersensitivity? (Marks: 5)
    Topic: Hypersensitivity & Immune Responses

    The types of hypersensitivity are: Type I, Type II, Type III and Type IV. Types I, II and III are antibody-mediated, while Type IV is cell-mediated.

  63. Write the mechanism of type III hypersensitivity.
    Topic: Hypersensitivity & Immune Responses

    Mechanism of Type III Hypersensitivity: Antigens combine with antibody within the circulation and form immune complexes. Wherever in the body they are deposited, they activate the complement system. Polymorphonuclear cells are attracted to the site. The result is inflammation and tissue injury.

  64. What is anaphylaxis?
    Topic: Hypersensitivity & Immune Responses

    Anaphylaxis is the form of immune response in which an exaggerated response is generated against an allergen. The molecules which produce such a response are called anaphylatoxins. It is a severe, systemic allergic reaction involving IgE-mediated release of histamine and other mediators. Symptoms include hives, itching, swelling, and anaphylactic shock.

  65. Write six symptoms of GVHR (graft-versus-host reaction).
    Topic: Hypersensitivity & Immune Responses

    Symptoms of GVHR: 1. Skin rash; 2. Emaciation (becoming thin); 3. Retarded growth; 4. Diarrhoea; 5. Hepatomegaly; 6. Splenomegaly; 7. Increase in bilirubin production; 8. Bile ducts are damaged; 9. Anaemia.

  66. Write the functions of cell-mediated responses.
    Topic: Hypersensitivity & Immune Responses

    The primary function of the cell-mediated response is to eliminate intracellular pathogens and to eliminate tumor cells. Both antigen-specific and non-specific cells are involved: antigen-specific cells are cytotoxic and helper T cells; non-specific cells are macrophages, neutrophils and NK cells. Both specific and non-specific responses require cytokines.

  67. Write two diseases for which inactivated bacterial cell or viral particle vaccines are used.
    Topic: Vaccines

    Inactivated bacterial cell vaccines: 1. Anthrax, 2. Cholera, 3. Plague. Inactivated viral particle vaccines: 1. Hepatitis A, 2. Influenza, 3. Polio.

  68. Write the diseases for which live attenuated vaccines are used.
    Topic: Vaccines

    Live attenuated vaccines are used for: tuberculosis, typhoid, measles, polio, rotavirus, chickenpox and yellow fever.

  69. How does a capsular antigen serve as a vaccine? Which cells are activated by these antigens? Give an example.
    Topic: Vaccines

    Capsular polysaccharides: The virulence of some pathogenic bacteria depends primarily on the anti-phagocytic properties of their hydrophilic polysaccharide capsule. Coating of the capsule with antibodies and/or complement greatly increases the ability of macrophages and neutrophils to phagocytose such pathogens, so capsular polysaccharides serve as vaccine antigens that activate these phagocytic cells. Example: The current vaccine for Streptococcus pneumoniae, which causes pneumococcal pneumonia, consists of 23 antigenically different capsular polysaccharides.

  70. Write the factors that affect antigen and antibody tests. (Marks: 3)
    Topic: Antigen-Antibody Tests & ELISA

    Factors affecting antigen and antibody tests include: 1. Affinity: The strength of binding between a single antigenic determinant and a single antibody site. Higher affinity results in more stable interactions. 2. Avidity: The overall strength of binding between multivalent antigens and antibodies, influenced by the valency of both. Greater avidity leads to more stable and easily detectable reactions. 3. Antigen and antibody ratio: The ratio determines the nature of the complex, with equivalence facilitating lattice formation and larger complexes being more detectable. 4. Physical form of the antigen: The physical form affects the reaction type, with particulate antigens causing agglutination and soluble antigens causing precipitation. 5. Sample quality and handling: Contaminated or degraded samples can lead to false negatives or false positives. 6. Test sensitivity and specificity: A test with low sensitivity may miss detecting the antigen, while a test with low specificity may detect non-specific substances. 7. Interfering substances or cross-reactivity: Substances in the sample can interfere with the test or react with the test reagents, leading to inaccurate results.

  71. Write the advantages/applications of ELISA and differentiate between direct and indirect ELISA. (Marks: 5)
    Topic: Antigen-Antibody Tests & ELISA

    ELISA (Enzyme-Linked Immunosorbent Assay) is a solid-phase immunoassay. Advantages: high sensitivity and specificity; easy to perform and automate; can detect small amounts of antigen or antibody; non-invasive. Applications: (1) Viral antigen detection, e.g. identifying Hepatitis B surface antigen (HBsAg) in patient serum; (2) Antibody determination, e.g. detecting antibodies against viruses such as anti-HCV antibodies; (3) Measuring serum cytokine levels, e.g. IL-1 and TNF-alpha. Direct ELISA: Detects antigen directly. The antigen is immobilized on the plate (solid phase) and an enzyme-conjugated antibody binds to it, indicating the presence of the antigen. Indirect ELISA: Detects antibody against an antigen. The antigen is immobilized on the plate; the patient's primary antibody binds to it, and a secondary antibody conjugated to an enzyme binds to the primary antibody, indicating the presence of the antibody.

  72. What are the three components of coagulation?
    Topic: Related Topics Asked in the Sources

    Coagulation, also known as clotting, is the process by which blood changes from a liquid to a gel, forming a blood clot to stop bleeding after an injury. Its three components are: 1. Platelets: These cellular blood components form a plug at the injury site, initiating clot formation (primary hemostasis). 2. Coagulation Factors: These proteinaceous components, including fibrinogen and prothrombin, participate in a cascade to form fibrin strands that strengthen the platelet plug. 3. Fibrin: This protein forms threads that cross-link and stabilize the platelet plug, leading to clot formation (secondary hemostasis).

  73. What is a proto-oncogene? Write its function. (Marks: 2)
    Topic: Related Topics Asked in the Sources

    A proto-oncogene is a normal gene that can become an oncogene, either after mutation or increased expression. Proto-oncogenes code for proteins that help to regulate cell growth and differentiation. Proto-oncogenes are often involved in signal transduction and execution of mitogenic signals, usually through their protein products. Upon activation, a proto-oncogene (or its product) becomes a tumor-inducing agent, an oncogene. Some oncogenes, usually involved in early stages of cancer development, increase the chance that a normal cell develops into a tumor cell, possibly resulting in cancer.

  74. Write the steps when mast cells are activated and leukocytes enter the site of injury. (Marks: 5)
    Topic: Related Topics Asked in the Sources

    1. Expansion of capillaries to increase blood flow (seen as blushing or a rash). 2. Increase in the permeability of the microvasculature structure to allow escape of fluid, plasma proteins, and leukocytes from the circulation — edema. 3. Exit of leukocytes from the capillaries and their accumulation at the site of injury.
