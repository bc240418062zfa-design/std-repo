  1. Distinguish between 'disease' and 'illness' as defined in health psychology.
    Topic: Introduction to Health Psychology

    A disease is a characteristic grouping of physical signs and symptoms that has a specific name and can often be traced to a specific causal agent; it is what a physician recognizes as a specific disorder. Illness is a broader term involving people's beliefs about the state of their physical well-being and the behaviors they engage in. Illness beliefs may arise from a specific disease or simply from the way a person feels, and it is illness that motivates people to seek out a physician.

  2. What is the wellness approach to health, and what are its key elements?
    Topic: Introduction to Health Psychology

    Wellness is an approach to health that focuses on balancing the many dimensions of a person's life — physical, social, spiritual, emotional, intellectual, and environmental/occupational — by increasing health-enhancing conditions and behaviors rather than merely minimizing illness. A key element is striving for balance: when all six dimensions are high and in balance, optimal health and well-being result. Wellness is also a process; becoming healthier and making healthy choices matters more than reaching a final state.

  3. How have the patterns of illness changed from the past to the present in developed countries?
    Topic: Introduction to Health Psychology

    In earlier centuries, people in developed nations suffered and died chiefly from dietary diseases (caused by malnutrition) and infectious diseases (acute illnesses caused by microorganisms such as bacteria and viruses). Today, the main health problems and causes of death are chronic diseases — degenerative illnesses that develop or persist over a long period. About two-thirds of all deaths in developed nations are caused by three chronic diseases: heart disease, cancer, and stroke. This shift occurred because preventive measures and medical care reduced deaths from infectious diseases while life expectancy increased.

  4. Explain Hippocrates' humoral theory of disease.
    Topic: Introduction to Health Psychology

    Hippocrates, called the Father of Medicine, proposed that the body contains four fluids called humors. When the mixture of these humors is harmonious or balanced, a person is in a state of health; disease occurs when the mixture is faulty or imbalanced. He recommended eating a good diet and avoiding excesses to help achieve humoral balance. Greek philosophers, especially Plato, also proposed that the mind and the body are separate entities, a view that dominated thinking for more than a thousand years.

  5. What does the term 'psychosomatic' actually mean, and what was the focus of psychosomatic medicine?
    Topic: Introduction to Health Psychology

    The term psychosomatic does not mean that a person's symptoms are imaginary; it means that the mind and the body are both involved. Psychosomatic medicine studied how psychological factors relate to real physical health problems such as ulcers, high blood pressure, asthma, migraine headaches, and rheumatoid arthritis. Its research base began with Walter Cannon's 1932 observation that physiological changes accompany emotion, suggesting that emotion can cause changes that may in turn cause disease.

  6. Define behavioral medicine, and distinguish it from behavioral health.
    Topic: Introduction to Health Psychology

    Behavioral medicine was defined at a 1977 conference at Yale University as the interdisciplinary field concerned with the development and integration of behavioral and biomedical science knowledge and techniques relevant to health and illness, and the application of this knowledge and these techniques to prevention, diagnosis, treatment, and rehabilitation. Behavioral health, which emerged at about the same time, emphasizes the enhancement of health and the prevention of disease in healthy people rather than the diagnosis and treatment of disorders in sick people.

  7. According to the biomedical model, what causes illness, who is responsible for illness, and how should illness be treated?
    Topic: Introduction to Health Psychology

    According to the biomedical model, diseases come from outside the body or originate as internal involuntary physical changes caused by chemical imbalances, bacteria, viruses, or genetic predisposition. Individuals are not seen as responsible for their illness; they are victims of external forces causing internal changes beyond their control. Treatment — such as vaccination, surgery, chemotherapy, and radiotherapy — aims to change the physical state of the body, and responsibility for treatment rests with the medical profession, whose recommendations must be followed.

  8. How does health psychology's biopsychosocial perspective differ from the biomedical model on the relation between health and illness and the patient's role?
    Topic: Introduction to Health Psychology

    Health psychology challenges the mind–body split by suggesting a role for the mind in both the cause and the treatment of illness. From the biopsychosocial perspective, health and illness are not qualitatively different states but exist on a continuum, and individuals progress along it from healthiness to illness and back again. Because illness results from a combination of biological, psychological, and social factors, the whole person should be treated, and the patient is in part responsible for treatment — for example, by taking medicine and changing beliefs and behaviors.

  9. What is a 'system' in Engel's systems concept, and how are systems organized?
    Topic: Introduction to Health Psychology

    A system is a dynamic entity with components that are continuously interrelated — for example, the body (with its immune and nervous systems of tissues and cells), the family, the community, and society. Systems are nested in levels: cells are within the person, who is within a society. A system at one level is affected by, and can affect, systems at other levels, so illness in one part of the body can have far-reaching effects on the person's social relations with family and community.

  10. What is the life-span perspective, and why is it important in health psychology?
    Topic: Introduction to Health Psychology

    The life-span perspective holds that people change over time through development, and that each portion of the life span is affected by happenings in earlier years and affects those in later years. Characteristics of a person are therefore considered with respect to prior development, current level, and likely future development. The kinds of illnesses people have change with age — children suffer mostly short-term infectious diseases, while many people in late adulthood suffer heart disease, cancer, and stroke — and the roles of biological, psychological, and social systems likewise change across development.

  11. Differentiate between registered nurses (RNs) and licensed practical nurses (LPNs) in terms of duties and training.
    Topic: Health-Related Careers

    Registered nurses (RNs) assess and record patients' symptoms and progress, conduct tests, administer medications, assist in rehabilitation, and instruct patients and families in maintaining health; their college or university training takes 4 or 5 years and leads to a baccalaureate degree. Licensed practical nurses (LPNs) perform nursing activities requiring less training, such as taking and recording temperatures and blood pressures, administering certain medications, changing dressings, and helping patients with personal hygiene. LPN training programs take about a year to complete.

  12. Why did the field of health psychology emerge? Discuss the major factors.
    Topic: Health-Related Careers

    The most important factor was the changing pattern of illness: acute infectious disorders such as tuberculosis and pneumonia were once the major causes of death, but now chronic illnesses — especially heart disease, cancer, and diabetes — are the main contributors to disability and death, and psychological and social factors are implicated in their causes and long-term management. Other factors include the expansion of health care services and rising costs, the increased acceptance of health psychologists within the medical community, the demonstrated contributions of psychological interventions to health, and the methodological contributions health psychologists make to research teams.

  13. Describe the structure of a neuron and explain how messages pass from one neuron to another.
    Topic: The Nervous System

    A neuron has a cell body from which clusters of branches called dendrites project; dendrites receive messages from adjacent neurons. The messages travel through a long, slender projection called the axon, which splits into branches whose tips have small swellings called synaptic knobs. The knobs connect to the dendrites of other neurons across a fluid-filled gap, and this junction is called a synapse; messages cross the gap from the knobs to adjacent neurons and so eventually reach their destination.

  14. Explain the functions of the thalamus and the hypothalamus.
    Topic: The Nervous System

    The thalamus is a pivotal structure that functions as the chief relay station of the nervous system, directing sensory messages — such as pain or visual images — to appropriate points in the cerebrum, and relaying commands from the motor cortex out to the skeletal muscles. The hypothalamus, a small structure just below the thalamus, plays an important role in emotion and motivation; it affects eating, drinking, and sexual activity, arousing hunger and thirst when the body lacks nutrients or water. It also maintains homeostasis — a state of balance or normal function among body systems — controlling adjustments such as shivering when cold and perspiring when warm.

  15. Compare the functions of the sympathetic and parasympathetic divisions of the autonomic nervous system.
    Topic: The Nervous System and Endocrine Glands

    The sympathetic nervous system mobilizes and expends energy in responding to emergencies, strong emotions, and strenuous activity: it speeds up the heart, dilates arteries to increase blood flow to the heart and skeletal muscles, constricts arteries to decrease blood flow to the skin and digestive organs, decreases salivation, and increases perspiration. The parasympathetic division acts alongside and often in opposition to the sympathetic division, regulating quiet, calming processes that conserve and store energy — for example, it regulates digestion by increasing salivation and stomach contractions, and after an emergency has passed it helps restore the normal body state.

  16. Describe the role of the adrenal glands and their hormones in the body's response to emergencies and stress.
    Topic: The Nervous System and Endocrine Glands

    The adrenal glands, located on top of the kidneys, release several important hormones in response to emergencies and stress. Cortisol helps control swelling when we are injured, although continued high levels over a long time can be harmful, leading to high blood pressure and ulcers. Epinephrine and nor-epinephrine (adrenalin and noradrenalin) work with the sympathetic nervous system to speed up heart and respiration rates and increase the liver's sugar output for quick energy. Because these hormones travel in the bloodstream, some of their impact continues for a while after the emergency has passed.

  17. Describe the journey of food through the digestive system and explain how absorption occurs in the small intestine.
    Topic: Digestive and Renal Systems

    Food is broken down mechanically, for example by chewing, and chemically by enzymes, beginning with saliva in the mouth, and is pushed down the esophagus to the stomach by peristalsis. In the stomach it is mixed for 3 or 4 hours with gastric juices, including hydrochloric acid and pepsin, to form a semi-liquid mixture that passes into the duodenum. In the small intestine the mixture becomes alkaline and pancreatic enzymes break carbohydrates into simple sugars, fats into glycerol and fatty acids, and proteins into amino acids. Absorption occurs through the folded wall of the small intestine, whose many projections contain capillaries, which absorb amino acids, simple sugars, water, some fatty acids, vitamins and minerals, and lacteals, which accept glycerol and the remaining fatty acids and vitamins.

  18. What are peptic ulcers and hepatitis? State their causes and main features.
    Topic: Digestive and Renal Systems

    Peptic ulcers are open sores in the lining of the stomach or intestine, usually in the duodenum, and abdominal pain is their chief symptom. They appear to result from excess gastric juices chronically eroding the lining when there is little or no food in the stomach, although bacterial infection can also play a role, and people with high stress seem more susceptible. Hepatitis is a class of viral diseases in which the liver becomes inflamed and unable to function well, with flu-like symptoms followed generally by jaundice, a yellowing of the skin. Hepatitis A is transmitted through contaminated food, water and utensils, while hepatitis B and C occur through sexual contact, transfusion of infected blood and sharing of contaminated needles, and some forms can lead to permanent liver damage.

  19. Explain the structures of the respiratory tract and the mechanism of breathing.
    Topic: The Respiratory System

    After air enters through the nose or mouth, it travels past the larynx, down the trachea and bronchial tubes, and into the lungs, where the tubes divide into smaller bronchioles ending in millions of tiny air sacs called alveoli. Each alveolus is a thin membrane enmeshed in capillaries, allowing oxygen, carbon dioxide and other gases to pass quickly between air and bloodstream. When we inhale, the rib muscles draw the ribs up and outward and the diaphragm contracts and pulls downward, enlarging the lung chambers and pulling air in. When we exhale, these muscles relax and the elasticity of the lungs forces the air out, like a balloon.

  20. Distinguish between systolic and diastolic blood pressure and list the factors of fluid dynamics that determine blood pressure.
    Topic: The Respiratory System

    Blood pressure is the force exerted by blood on the artery walls. Systolic pressure is the maximum force produced in the arteries when the heart pumps, while diastolic pressure is the resting force in the arteries between contractions, while the heart fills with blood, so a reading is expressed as systolic over diastolic, such as 120 over 80. Blood pressure is determined by five aspects of fluid dynamics: cardiac output, blood volume, peripheral resistance, elasticity of the blood vessels, and viscosity or thickness of the blood. As cardiac output, blood volume, resistance or viscosity increase, or as vessels become less elastic, blood pressure rises.

  21. Describe the three types of formed elements in blood and the condition associated with each.
    Topic: Blood Composition

    Formed elements make up about 45% of blood volume. Red blood cells are the most abundant, about 5 million per cubic millimeter, are formed in bone marrow, live about 3 months, and contain hemoglobin, which transports oxygen; anemia is the condition in which red blood cells or hemoglobin are below normal. Leukocytes are white blood cells, the least abundant formed element, which serve protective functions such as engulfing bacteria; leukemia is a malignant disease in which abnormal white blood cells are produced in extremely high quantities. Platelets are granular fragments from bone marrow that plug tiny wounds and help blood to clot; in hemophilia they do not function properly because the blood lacks a critical protein, impairing clotting.

  22. What are antigens, and how does the immune system respond to transplanted tissue and to harmless substances?
    Topic: Blood Composition

    An antigen is any substance that can trigger an immune response, because the immune system recognizes it as 'not self' rather than a normal body constituent; bacteria, fungi, protozoa and viruses are examples, recognized by aspects of their protein coats and DNA. The immune system also tends to recognize transplanted organ tissue as 'not self', which is what physicians mean by rejection, so acceptance is encouraged by closely matching donor and recipient tissues or by using drugs to suppress the immune system. For many people the system also mounts an attack against normally harmless substances such as pollen, animal dander and particular foods, producing allergies, and the specific substances that trigger these reactions are called allergens.

  23. Compare cell-mediated immunity and antibody-mediated (humoral) immunity.
    Topic: Soldiers of the Immune System

    Cell-mediated immunity operates at the level of the cell and its soldiers are T cells, which matured in the thymus; killer T cells directly destroy transplanted foreign tissue, cancerous cells and body cells already invaded by antigens. Antibody-mediated immunity attacks bacteria, fungi, protozoa and viruses while they are still in body fluids and before they have invaded body cells, focusing on the antigens directly. Its soldiers are B cells, which give rise to plasma cells that produce antibodies, a process often induced by helper T cells or inhibited by suppressor T cells. Helper and suppressor T cells thus regulate both processes, linking the two forms of specific immunity.

  24. Describe the body's three lines of defense in an immune response.
    Topic: Soldiers of the Immune System

    The first line of defense is the skin and the mucous membranes lining the respiratory and digestive tracts, which act as barriers and are coated with fluids containing antibodies and other antimicrobial substances. Once an antigen penetrates this barrier, it encounters the second line of defense, in which phagocytes attack and consume invaders and present the antigen to B cells and helper T cells, prompting plasma cells to produce antibodies that attach to microorganisms and aid their destruction. Antigens that invade body cells encounter the third line of defense, in which killer T cells destroy the invaded cells. As the invasion subsides, suppressor T cells slow the responses, and memory B and T cells remain ready for a faster response if the same antigen invades again.

  25. Explain the three approaches to conceptualizing stress: stimulus, response, and process.
    Topic: The Phenomenon of Stress

    The stimulus approach focuses on the environment and describes stress as a stimulus, referring to physically or psychologically challenging events or circumstances, called stressors, such as catastrophic events, major life events and chronic circumstances. The response approach focuses on people's reaction to stressors, including psychological responses involving behavior, thought patterns and emotions and physiological responses involving heightened bodily arousal; the person's response is called strain. The process approach includes stressors and strains but adds the relationship between the person and the environment, involving continuous interactions and adjustments called transactions. In this view the person is an active agent who can influence the impact of a stressor through behavioral, cognitive and emotional strategies, so the same stressor produces different amounts of strain in different people.

  26. Distinguish between primary appraisal and secondary appraisal, including the judgments involved in primary appraisal.
    Topic: The Phenomenon of Stress

    Cognitive appraisal is the mental process by which people assess whether a demand threatens their well-being and what resources are available for meeting it. Primary appraisal is the assessment of the meaning of a situation for well-being, which can yield a judgment that the event is irrelevant, benign-positive, or stressful. Events appraised as stressful are further appraised in terms of harm-loss, the damage already done, threat, the expectation of future harm, and challenge, the opportunity for growth, mastery or profit. Secondary appraisal is the ongoing assessment of the resources available for coping, such as judging 'I can do it if I work hard' or 'I can't do it—I know I'll fail', and the stress experienced often depends on the judged fit between demands and resources.

  27. Describe Selye's general adaptation syndrome (GAS) and its three stages.
    Topic: Factors That Lead to Stressful Appraisals

    The general adaptation syndrome is the series of physiological reactions the body makes when stress is long-lasting. In the alarm reaction, which is like the fight-or-flight response, arousal first drops below normal for a moment and then rapidly rises as the hypothalamus-pituitary-adrenal axis is activated, with the pituitary secreting ACTH and the adrenal glands releasing epinephrine, norepinephrine and cortisol, fully mobilizing the body. In the stage of resistance, if the stressor continues, the body tries to adapt, arousal declines somewhat but remains above normal, hormones are replenished, and the ability to resist new stressors may be impaired, producing diseases of adaptation such as ulcers, high blood pressure and asthma. In the stage of exhaustion, prolonged arousal weakens the immune system and depletes energy reserves, so disease, damage to internal organs and possibly death may occur.

  28. How do ambiguity and controllability affect stress appraisals?
    Topic: Factors That Lead to Stressful Appraisals

    Ambiguity, a lack of clarity in a situation, affects stress appraisals in different ways depending on its type. Role ambiguity, when information about a person's function or task is unclear or confusing, often increases stress because people are uncertain about their actions and decisions. Harm ambiguity, when the likelihood of harm or the availability of resources is unclear, has a variable effect depending on personality, beliefs and experience—one seriously ill person may draw hope from it while another may believe a poor prognosis is being hidden. Controllability also matters: people tend to appraise an uncontrollable event as more stressful than a controllable one, through either behavioral control, performing an action that affects the event, or cognitive control, using a mental strategy such as distracting attention or developing a plan.

  29. How does stress affect cognition, and how can cognition in turn perpetuate stress?
    Topic: Psychosocial Aspects of Stress

    High levels of stress can impair cognitive functioning, often by distracting attention, so that students in a stressful exam may neglect or misinterpret information or fail to remember answers they studied well. Children exposed to chronic noise who try to tune it out may develop generalized cognitive deficits because they have difficulty knowing which sounds to attend to, yet stress can also enhance attention and memory toward the stressor, as epinephrine and norepinephrine enhance memory of emotional experiences. The reverse also occurs: thinking can make stress chronic, as in Sonia's repeated imagining that her fiancé was seeing other women. Andrew Baum found that people near Three Mile Island who still felt stress years later were those who had trouble keeping thoughts and fears about the accident out of their minds, so such thoughts perpetuated their stress.

  30. Describe the three basic types of conflict as sources of stress within the person.
    Topic: Psychosocial Aspects of Stress

    Conflict arises from opposing motivational forces producing approach and avoidance tendencies, and is a major source of stress. Approach/approach conflict arises when we are attracted toward two appealing goals that are incompatible, such as wanting to lose weight while delicious fattening foods are available; it is generally resolved fairly easily, though stress increases with the importance of the decision. Avoidance/avoidance conflict occurs when we face a choice between two undesirable situations, such as two treatments with very undesirable side effects; people usually try to postpone or escape, vacillate, or let someone else decide, and find it difficult and very stressful. Approach/avoidance conflict arises when a single goal has both attractive and unattractive features, as with smokers who want to improve their health but wish to avoid weight gain and cravings, and it can be stressful and difficult to resolve.

  31. Describe temperamentally 'difficult' babies and explain why they are a source of stress for parents.
    Topic: Sources of Stress

    Each baby is born with certain personality dispositions called temperaments, and pediatric nurses and physicians broadly describe infants as 'easy' or 'difficult'. Difficult babies cry a great deal, often very loudly, and efforts to soothe them do not work very well. They resist being introduced to new foods, routines, and people, and their patterns of sleep, hunger, and bowel movements are hard to predict from day to day. Only about 10% of babies are classified as difficult, displaying most or all of these traits fairly consistently. Because a child who reacts very negatively to minor irritations is very stressful to parents, a difficult temperament adds considerably to parental stress, although many difficult children gradually change toward easy traits over time.

  32. How do children's reactions to divorce differ by age, and what can parents do to enhance their children's adjustment to divorce?
    Topic: Sources of Stress

    According to Judith Wallerstein, children's reactions to divorce depend partly on their age: very young children may feel responsible for the divorce, worry that the custodial parent will also leave, and develop sleep disturbances, whereas older children and adolescents tend to react with anger, often siding with one parent and blaming the other. Adapting to divorce usually takes several years, and some family members may never adjust fully. Parents can enhance adjustment by maintaining a loving, secure home life and telling the children in advance of the impending separation. They should encourage open communication, answer questions truthfully but sensitively, and gear information to the children's level of understanding with concrete, accurate explanations. Parents can also recruit help from relatives, counselors, and school personnel, and encourage the children to have contact with both parents.

  33. Describe the Social Readjustment Rating Scale (SRRS), including how it was developed and how a person's stress score is obtained.
    Topic: Measuring Stress

    The Social Readjustment Rating Scale was developed by Thomas Holmes and Richard Rahe (1967) and is the most widely used scale of life events. The researchers constructed a list of major life events derived from clinical experience and had hundreds of men and women of various ages and backgrounds rate the amount of readjustment each event would require for the average person. These ratings were used to assign each event a value reflecting its stressfulness, ranging from 100 points for death of a spouse to 11 points for minor violations of the law. To measure stress, respondents check off the events that happened to them during a given period, usually not more than the past 24 months. The researcher then sums the values of the checked items to obtain a total stress score.

  34. What are the main weaknesses or criticisms of the Social Readjustment Rating Scale (SRRS)?
    Topic: Measuring Stress

    One criticism is that some SRRS items are vague or ambiguous: 'change in responsibilities at work' does not indicate how much change or whether it involves more or less responsibility, and 'personal injury or illness' gives the same score to someone who had the flu and someone who became paralyzed, which reduces the precision of the instrument. A second criticism is that the scale does not consider the meaning or impact of an event for the individual, ignoring the person's subjective appraisal; for example, the score for 'death of spouse' is the same regardless of age, dependence on the spouse, or the length and happiness of the marriage. A third problem is that the scale does not distinguish between desirable and undesirable events, even though studies have found that undesirable life events are correlated with illness but desirable events are not. Partly because of these weaknesses, the correlation between SRRS scores and illness is only about 0.30, which is not very strong.

  35. Explain the buffering hypothesis: how does social support protect people against the negative effects of high stress?
    Topic: Psychosocial Factors Modifying the Impact of Stress

    Social support refers to the perceived comfort, caring, esteem, or help a person receives from other people or groups, and psychologists propose that it affects health by protecting the person against the negative effects of high stress — it provides a buffer against stress. Buffering works in at least two ways. First, when people encounter a strong stressor, such as a major financial crisis, those with high levels of social support may be less likely to appraise the situation as stressful, because they expect that someone they know will help them, for example by lending money or giving advice, so they judge that they can meet the demands. Second, social support may modify people's response to a stressor after the initial appraisal: someone may provide a solution to the problem, convince them the problem is not very important, or cheer them on to look on the 'bright side'. People with little social support are much less likely to have these advantages, so the negative impact of stress is greater for them.

  36. Define hardiness and resilience, and name the components of each.
    Topic: Psychosocial Factors Modifying the Impact of Stress

    Hardiness is a broader array of personality characteristics proposed to differentiate people who do and do not get sick under stress. It includes three characteristics: control, people's belief that they can influence events in their lives; commitment, people's sense of purpose or involvement in the events, activities, and people in their lives; and challenge, the tendency to view changes as incentives or opportunities for growth rather than threats to security. Resilience is a related trait that seems to include high levels of three components: self-esteem, personal control, and optimism. Resilient people appraise negative events as less stressful and bounce back from life's adversities, recovering their strength and spirit; for example, resilient children develop into competent, well-adjusted individuals despite growing up under extremely difficult conditions.

  37. Explain the two routes — direct (physiological) and indirect (behavioral) — by which stress can lead to illness.
    Topic: How Stress Affects Health

    The causal sequence between stress and illness can involve either of two routes. The indirect, behavioral route occurs when stress affects behavior, which in turn leads to illness or worsens an existing condition; for example, people who experience high levels of stress consume more alcohol, cigarettes, and coffee than less stressed people, and behavioral factors such as alcohol use and carelessness play a role in their relatively high accident rates. Stressful periods also produce haphazard meals, less regular bedtimes, delays in getting medical attention, and failures to follow physicians' recommendations. The direct route results from the changes stress produces in the body's physiology, especially when stress is chronic and severe. These physiological changes involve reactivity in the cardiovascular, endocrine, and immune systems, which is linked to conditions such as coronary heart disease, hypertension, and infectious diseases.

  38. How do stress hormones affect the immune system, and what is the role of the brain in this relationship according to psychoneuroimmunology?
    Topic: How Stress Affects Health

    The release of catecholamines and corticosteroids during arousal can impair the functioning of the immune system; for example, increases in cortisol and epinephrine are associated with decreased activity of T cells and B cells against antigens, and this decrease in lymphocyte activity appears important in the development and progression of a variety of infectious diseases and cancer. High levels of stress also reduce the production of enzymes that destroy chemical carcinogens and repair damaged DNA. Psychoneuroimmunology studies how the nervous, endocrine, and immune systems form a feedback loop: the nervous and endocrine systems send chemical messages in the form of neurotransmitters and hormones that increase or decrease immune function, while cells of the immune system produce chemicals, such as ACTH, that feed information back to the brain. The brain appears to serve as a control center maintaining a balance in immune function, since too little immune activity leaves the individual open to infection and too much activity may produce autoimmune diseases.

  39. Distinguish between emotion-focused coping and problem-focused coping, giving examples of each and stating when each tends to be used.
    Topic: Coping with Stress

    According to Richard Lazarus and his colleagues, coping can serve two main functions: it can alter the problem causing the stress, or it can regulate the emotional response to the problem. Emotion-focused coping is aimed at controlling the emotional response to the stressful situation, through behavioral approaches such as using alcohol or drugs, seeking emotional social support, or distracting activities like sports or watching TV, and cognitive approaches such as changing the meaning of the situation or denying unpleasant facts. People tend to use emotion-focused approaches when they believe they can do nothing to change the stressful conditions, as when a loved one dies. Problem-focused coping is aimed at reducing the demands of the stressful situation or expanding the resources to deal with it, as in quitting a stressful job, negotiating an extension for paying bills, devising a new study schedule, or seeking medical treatment. People tend to use problem-focused approaches when they believe their resources or the demands of the situation are changeable.

  40. Briefly describe any four coping strategies identified by Folkman, Lazarus and their colleagues, indicating whether each is problem-focused or emotion-focused.
    Topic: Coping with Stress

    Planful problem-solving (problem-focused) involves analyzing the situation to arrive at solutions and then taking direct action to correct the problem, such as a hospital patient studying information about different specialists before choosing one. Confrontive coping (problem-focused) involves taking assertive action, often involving anger or risk-taking, to change the situation, such as fighting an insurance company that balks at paying for a desired treatment. Escape-avoidance (emotion-focused) involves thinking wishfully about the situation or taking action to escape or avoid it, such as fantasizing that the problems will go away or avoiding them by sleeping or using alcohol a lot. Positive reappraisal (emotion-focused) involves trying to create a positive meaning from the situation in terms of personal growth, sometimes with a religious tone, such as becoming a better or stronger person from the experience or developing a stronger faith. (Seeking social support is unusual in that it can serve either a problem-focused or an emotion-focused function.)

  41. Describe time management as an approach to organizing one's world better, and explain why each of its elements matters.
    Topic: Reducing the Potential for Stress

    People often feel stress because they are running late or believe they do not have enough time for the tasks of the day, and time management is an important approach for organizing one's time to reduce this potential for stress. Its first element is to set goals, which should be reasonable or obtainable and should include both long-term goals, such as getting a job promotion next year, and short-term ones, such as meeting a weekly sales quota. The second element is making daily To-Do Lists with priorities indicated, keeping the goals in mind; these lists should be composed early each morning or late in the preceding day, and each list must be written, because trying to keep it in one's head is unreliable and makes setting priorities difficult. The third element is to set up a schedule for the day, allocating estimated time periods to each item in the list, and adjusting the list if an urgent new task arises during the day.

  42. How can a person's sense of personal control be improved in childhood and in adulthood, according to the lesson?
    Topic: Reducing the Potential for Stress

    When life becomes stressful, people who lack a strong sense of personal control may stop trying and feel helpless, so the main psychological help such people need is to boost their self-efficacy and reduce their passiveness and helplessness. The process can begin very early: parents, teachers, and other caregivers can show a child love and respect, provide a stimulating environment, encourage and praise the child's accomplishments, and set reasonable standards of conduct and performance that the child can regard as challenges rather than threats, which is also likely to enhance the child's resilience or hardiness. Adults' personal control can be enhanced too: employers can give workers some degree of control over aspects of their jobs, allowing them input in decisions about the hours they work, which tasks to work on, and ways to improve the quality of their work. For people with serious chronic illnesses, health psychologists can help those with little control by training them in effective ways to cope with stress.

  43. Describe the procedure of progressive muscle relaxation, including its origin and typical session details.
    Topic: Stress Management

    Progressive muscle relaxation is a technique in which people focus their attention on specific muscle groups while alternately tightening and relaxing these muscles. The idea of teaching people to relax their skeletal muscles to reduce psychological stress was proposed by Edmund Jacobson (1938), who found that muscle tension could be reduced much more if subjects were taught to pay attention to the sensations as they tense and relax individual groups of muscles. In a typical version, the person follows a sequence of muscle groups — for example, hands, forehead, lower face, neck, stomach, and finally legs — tensing each group for 7-10 seconds and then relaxing it for about 15 seconds, paying attention to how the muscles feel. This is usually repeated for the same muscle group two or three times in a session that generally lasts 20 or 30 minutes, and the technique works best in a quiet, non-distracting setting with the person lying down or sitting on comfortable furniture. Research has shown that people trained in relaxation show less cardiovascular reactivity to stressors and stronger immune function.

  44. Explain systematic desensitization: its theoretical basis, the role of the stimulus hierarchy, and how the procedure is carried out.
    Topic: Stress Management

    Systematic desensitization is a method for reducing fear and anxiety, based on the view that fears are learned by classical conditioning — by associating a situation or object with an unpleasant event, as when a person associates visits to the dentist with pain. According to Joseph Wolpe, an originator of the method, reversal comes about through counter-conditioning, whereby the 'calm' response gradually replaces the 'fear' response. An important feature of the method is the stimulus hierarchy, a graded sequence of approximations to the feared situation that brings the person gradually into contact with the source of fear in about 10 or 15 steps; the steps may involve real-life (in vivo) contact, imaginary situations, or symbolic contact through pictures, films, or models. The procedure starts with relaxation exercises, then the steps are presented individually, from least to most fearful, while the person is relaxed and comfortable; once the wariness at one step has passed and the person is calm, the next step is introduced.

  45. What do you know about phobia? Explain with an example.
    Topic: Past Midterm Paper Questions

    A phobia is an intense, persistent and irrational fear of a specific object, situation or activity that poses little or no actual danger. The fear is out of proportion to the real threat, yet it provokes immediate anxiety and leads the person to avoid the feared object or endure it with great distress. For example, a person with arachnophobia has an extreme fear of spiders: even seeing a small spider or its picture can trigger panic-like symptoms such as a racing heart, sweating and trembling, and the person goes out of the way to avoid places where spiders might be present, even though the spider is harmless.

  46. Narrate five symptoms of depression.
    Topic: Past Midterm Paper Questions

    Depression is a mood disorder, and its common symptoms include: (1) a persistently depressed, sad or empty mood most of the day; (2) a marked loss of interest or pleasure in almost all activities (anhedonia); (3) significant changes in appetite and weight, and disturbed sleep such as insomnia or oversleeping; (4) fatigue and loss of energy, along with feelings of worthlessness or excessive guilt; and (5) diminished ability to think or concentrate, indecisiveness, and in severe cases recurrent thoughts of death or suicide.

  47. Name two drugs prescribed by physicians to treat stress.
    Topic: Past Midterm Paper Questions

    Physicians commonly prescribe anti-anxiety drugs (tranquilizers) for stress, for example benzodiazepines such as diazepam (Valium) and alprazolam (Xanax), which reduce tension and anxiety by depressing central nervous system activity. They may also prescribe beta blockers, which control the physical symptoms of stress such as a racing heart and trembling.

  48. What are the disorders of the renal system known as AGN and tubular necrosis?
    Topic: Past Midterm Paper Questions

    Disorders of the renal (kidney) system include acute glomerulonephritis (AGN) and acute tubular necrosis. AGN is the inflammation of the glomeruli — the tiny filtering units of the kidney — often following an infection; it reduces filtration and produces blood and protein in the urine, swelling and raised blood pressure. Acute tubular necrosis is the death of the epithelial cells lining the kidney tubules, usually caused by reduced blood supply (ischemia) or toxic substances; it is one of the most common causes of acute kidney failure, in which the kidneys suddenly fail to filter waste from the blood.

  49. Who is responsible for illness according to the biomedical model?
    Topic: Past Midterm Paper Questions

    According to the biomedical model, no one is responsible for illness — certainly not the individual. Illness is seen as arising from biological changes beyond the person's control, such as bacterial or viral infection, chemical imbalances or genetic predisposition, so the individual is regarded as a victim of an external force causing internal changes. Responsibility for treatment, in this model, rests with the medical profession.

  50. What are phagocytes? Describe their types.
    Topic: Past Midterm Paper Questions

    Phagocytes are white blood cells that protect the body by engulfing and digesting harmful foreign particles, bacteria and dead or dying cells — a process called phagocytosis. Their main types are: (1) neutrophils, the most abundant phagocytes, which are usually the first to arrive at the site of infection; (2) monocytes, which circulate in the blood and mature into macrophages when they enter tissues — macrophages are large, long-lived phagocytes that also present antigens to other immune cells; and (3) dendritic cells, which engulf pathogens and play a key role in activating the immune response.
