  1. When does culture affect behavior? (Marks: 3)
    Topic: Leadership, Culture & the Leader as an Individual

    Culture affects behavior all the time, because the shared values, norms and assumptions of the society and organization in which a person lives and works shape what he or she perceives as right or wrong, acceptable or unacceptable. Employees learn the culture through socialization, and it then guides their attitudes, decisions and day-to-day behavior at work. A healthy culture produces positive behavior, while a weak or negative culture leads to poor behavior and performance.

  2. How do employees learn culture? (Marks: 5)
    Topic: Leadership, Culture & the Leader as an Individual

    Employees learn organizational culture mainly through four ways: (1) Stories — narratives about founders, heroes, successes and failures that anchor the present in the past and legitimize current practices. (2) Rituals — repetitive sequences of activities that express and reinforce the key values of the organization. (3) Material symbols — office layout, dress code, type of vehicles and facilities that convey who is important and what behavior is appropriate. (4) Language — the special terms and jargon members use, which acts as a common denominator that unites members of a culture.

  3. What is locus of control? What is the difference between an internal and external locus of control? (Marks: 5)
    Topic: Leadership, Culture & the Leader as an Individual

    Locus of control is the degree to which people believe they are masters of their own fate. Internals are individuals who believe that they control what happens to them: they attribute outcomes to their own efforts and abilities, take responsibility for results, and generally perform better on complex tasks and in leadership roles. Externals are individuals who believe that what happens to them is controlled by outside forces such as luck, fate or powerful others; they attribute outcomes to external causes, are less satisfied and tend to be more alienated from the work setting.

  4. Describe the Type B personality and its relationship to success. (Marks: 5)
    Topic: Leadership, Culture & the Leader as an Individual

    Type B personalities are relaxed, easy-going and patient. They rarely suffer from a sense of time urgency, do one thing at a time, listen carefully, delegate work comfortably and are seldom competitive or aggressive — the opposite of the hard-driving, impatient Type A. Although Type A's are often assumed to be more successful because of their drive, in practice Type B individuals frequently reach the top levels of organizations: they make sound decisions, are creative rather than merely fast, and their steady, patient style is better suited to senior leadership roles that require judgment rather than speed.

  5. Explain the difference between job satisfaction, job involvement, and organizational commitment. (Marks: 10)
    Topic: Leadership, Culture & the Leader as an Individual

    Job satisfaction is a positive feeling about one's job resulting from an evaluation of its characteristics — it is an attitude toward the job itself (pay, work, supervision, promotion). Job involvement is the degree to which a person identifies psychologically with his or her job and considers the perceived performance level important to self-worth; highly involved employees strongly identify with and really care about the kind of work they do. Organizational commitment is the degree to which an employee identifies with a particular organization and its goals and wishes to maintain membership in the organization. In short: satisfaction is about liking the job, involvement is about identifying with the work, and commitment is about identifying with and staying in the organization.

  6. List down the three main components of an attitude. (Marks: 3)
    Topic: Leadership, Culture & the Leader as an Individual

    An attitude has three components: (1) Cognitive component — the opinion or belief segment; a description of or belief in the way things are (e.g., 'discrimination is wrong'). (2) Affective component — the emotional or feeling segment of an attitude (e.g., 'I don't like my supervisor because he discriminates'). (3) Behavioral component — the intention to behave in a certain way toward someone or something (e.g., 'I am going to complain about my supervisor').

  7. Differentiate between high self-monitoring and low self-monitoring. (Marks: 3)
    Topic: Leadership, Culture & the Leader as an Individual

    Self-monitoring is a personality trait that measures an individual's ability to adjust his or her behavior to external, situational factors. High self-monitors are very sensitive to external cues, can behave differently in different situations, and are capable of presenting striking contradictions between their public persona and their private self; they are often pragmatic, flexible and successful in managerial roles requiring multiple roles. Low self-monitors cannot disguise their behavior; they display their true dispositions and attitudes in every situation, so there is high behavioral consistency between who they are and what they do.

  8. Discuss the leadership styles identified in Kurt Lewin's studies at the University of Iowa. (Marks: 5)
    Topic: Leadership Theories & Styles

    Kurt Lewin and his associates at the University of Iowa explored three leadership styles. (1) Autocratic style: the leader dictates work methods, makes unilateral decisions and limits employee participation. (2) Democratic style: the leader involves employees in decision making, delegates authority and uses feedback as an opportunity for coaching. (3) Laissez-faire style: the leader gives the group complete freedom to make decisions and complete work in whatever way it sees fit. Lewin's research found that group members were more satisfied under a democratic leader than under an autocratic one, although results on performance were mixed.

  9. Differentiate between transactional and transformational leadership. (Marks: 5)
    Topic: Leadership Theories & Styles

    Transactional leaders guide or motivate their followers toward established goals by clarifying role and task requirements; they exchange rewards for effort and performance, monitor deviations and take corrective action (contingent reward and management by exception). Transformational leaders inspire followers to transcend their own self-interests for the good of the organization; they provide vision and a sense of mission, stimulate followers intellectually, give individualized consideration and can have a profound effect on followers' performance. The two are not opposing approaches — transformational leadership builds on transactional leadership and produces higher levels of effort and performance.

  10. Explain Fiedler's contingency model of leadership.
    Topic: Leadership Theories & Styles

    Fiedler's model proposes that effective group performance depends on properly matching the leader's style with the degree of control the situation gives the leader. A leader's basic style — task-oriented or relationship-oriented — is measured by the Least Preferred Co-worker (LPC) questionnaire and is assumed to be fixed. Situational control is determined by three factors: leader–member relations (confidence and trust of subordinates), task structure (degree to which job assignments are procedurized), and position power (authority to hire, fire, reward and punish). Task-oriented leaders perform best in very favorable or very unfavorable situations, while relationship-oriented leaders perform best in moderately favorable situations. Because styles are fixed, effectiveness is improved by changing the situation or replacing the leader.

  11. What are the five common ingredients of great leaders? Comment in the light of the theory provided by Warren Bennis. (Marks: 5)
    Topic: Leadership Theories & Styles

    Warren Bennis, after studying great leaders, identified the competencies (ingredients) that great leaders share: (1) Management of attention — a compelling vision that attracts and focuses followers. (2) Management of meaning — the ability to communicate the vision clearly so that followers understand and align with it. (3) Management of trust — reliability and constancy; followers know where the leader stands at all times. (4) Management of self — knowing one's own strengths and weaknesses and deploying oneself effectively. In Bennis's view, leadership is not a rare skill possessed by a charismatic few; these competencies can be learned and developed, and great leaders are open to all questions, dynamic, motivational, proactive and able to take the team beyond the minds of the followers.

  12. Explain when leaders are effective. Also discuss the characteristics of a leader who fails. (Marks: 10)
    Topic: Leadership Theories & Styles

    Leaders are effective when their style matches the demands of the situation, when followers are committed and competent, and when the leader's actions actually produce the intended outcomes — higher group performance, follower satisfaction and goal achievement. Effective leaders provide vision, build trust, communicate meaning, empower followers and adapt their behavior to situational factors. A leader who fails typically shows the opposite characteristics: incompetence (lacking the skills the job requires), rigidity (unwilling to change), intemperance (lack of self-control), callousness (uncaring toward followers), corruption (putting self-interest above the organization), insularity (disregarding the welfare of outsiders) and poor judgment in building a team — the 'bad leadership' traits described by Kellerman.

  13. How can charismatic leadership hurt an organization? (Marks: 5)
    Topic: Charismatic Leadership

    Charismatic leadership can hurt an organization in several ways. The organization may become excessively dependent on one personality, so performance collapses when the leader leaves or fails. Followers may suspend their own judgment and follow the leader blindly, suppressing dissent and bad news. The leader's strong ego and conviction can produce overconfidence, risky decisions and disregard of rules or ethical limits. To protect the organization, procedures and systems should be developed so that it never depends upon a single personality, and alternate leadership should be prepared to take over in any deadlock.

  14. Suppose you want to become a charismatic leader. What are the key qualities that you must have in yourself to become a charismatic leader? (Marks: 5)
    Topic: Charismatic Leadership

    A charismatic leader must have the following key qualities: (1) Self-confidence — complete confidence in his or her judgment and ability. (2) A vision — an idealized goal that proposes a future better than the status quo, and the ability to clarify its importance. (3) Strong conviction in that vision — perceived as being strongly committed, knowing what he or she wants and being willing to take high personal risk to achieve it. (4) Out-of-the-ordinary (unconventional) behavior — behavior that is novel and counter to the norms, admired by followers as heroic. (5) The image of a change agent — perceived as an agent of radical change rather than a caretaker of the status quo. In addition, charismatic leaders are sensitive to environmental constraints and to followers' needs.

  15. List down the five followership styles. (Marks: 5)
    Topic: Followership

    According to Kelley's followership styles, followers are classified on two dimensions — independent critical thinking and active engagement — into five styles: (1) Exemplary (effective/star) followers — active behavior with high independent critical thinking; they plan, take initiative and add value. (2) Conformist (yes) followers — active in carrying out work but low in critical thinking; they do not question orders. (3) Passive (sheep) followers — passive behavior with low critical thinking; they show neither initiative nor responsibility. (4) Alienated followers — passive in behavior but high in critical thinking; they think critically but often become negative and cynical. (5) Pragmatic (survivor) followers — stand in the middle on both dimensions and change their style to fit the situation, acting like an organizational 'canary in the mine-shaft' that gives early warning signals.

  16. What are the follower traits that influence the leadership process? (Marks: 4)
    Topic: Followership

    The follower traits that influence the leadership process include the followers' personality, position and experience. In practical terms: followers' competence and expertise, their needs and motives (such as need for achievement, power and affiliation), their locus of control and self-monitoring, their expectations of the leader, and their level of commitment and involvement. These traits determine how much direction and support followers need and how they respond to a leader's behavior, so the same leader behavior can produce different results with different followers.

  17. How do leaders become powerful? Identify the two faces of power. (Marks: 3)
    Topic: Power, Influence & Empowerment

    Leaders become powerful by gaining control over resources that others depend on — through their formal position (legitimate authority), control of rewards and punishments, expertise, and the support and motivation of their followers; the scarcer and more important the resource and the fewer its substitutes, the greater the power. Power has two faces: (1) Personal power — used for personal gain; it is dangerous because a person using power for his own interest may destroy the organization. (2) Social power — used to create motivation and to accomplish group and organizational goals, benefiting others rather than the leader alone.

  18. Which power is the ultimate source of an individual's power in an organization? Discuss. (Marks: 3)
    Topic: Power, Influence & Empowerment

    Legitimate power is the ultimate source of an individual's power in an organization. Legitimate power confers on an individual the legitimate authority — flowing from the formal position held in the hierarchy — to control and use organizational resources to achieve organizational goals. Because it is granted by the organization itself and accepted by members as proper authority, it forms the base on which other powers (reward, coercive and the use of expert and referent power within the role) are exercised.

  19. Where are coercive power and reward power counterparts of each other? (Marks: 5)
    Topic: Power, Influence & Empowerment

    Coercive power and reward power are counterparts because both are forms of position (formal) power that rest on the leader's control over outcomes valued by followers, and both work through the followers' dependence on the leader. Reward power is the ability to give positive benefits — pay raises, promotions, recognition, better assignments — to those who comply. Coercive power is the opposite side of the same coin: the ability to punish or withhold those same benefits — demotion, poor assignments, dismissal — from those who do not comply. One influences through giving desired outcomes, the other through threatening to take them away.

  20. List down the impression management strategies adopted by employees. (Marks: 3)
    Topic: Power, Influence & Empowerment

    Impression management strategies adopted by employees fall into two groups. A — Demotion-preventative strategies (used to avoid looking bad): Accounts (explaining away a bad event), Apologies (accepting blame while asking for pardon), and Disassociation (distancing oneself from a failing project or group). B — Promotion-enhancing strategies (used to look good): Entitlements (claiming credit for positive events), Enhancements (claiming more value for an accomplishment than is apparent), Obstacle disclosures (pointing out the difficulties one overcame), and Association (being publicly linked with successful people and projects).

  21. Do you think that empowered employees can prove themselves as an important asset of an organization? Discuss. (Marks: 5)
    Topic: Power, Influence & Empowerment

    Yes. Empowerment gives employees a sense of competence, self-determination, impact and meaning in their work. Empowered employees take ownership of decisions, respond quickly to customers and problems without waiting for approvals, suggest improvements and show higher commitment and job satisfaction. Organizations benefit through better service quality, innovation, faster decisions at lower levels and lower turnover. For empowerment to work, managers must share information, give training and resources, delegate real authority and support employees rather than punishing honest mistakes. Properly empowered employees therefore become a genuine strategic asset of the organization.

  22. What is empowerment? How can managers empower employees?
    Topic: Power, Influence & Empowerment

    Empowerment is the process of giving employees more authority, responsibility and freedom to make decisions about their work — increasing their intrinsic motivation by strengthening their sense of competence, choice (self-determination), impact and meaning. Managers can empower employees by: delegating meaningful authority instead of only tasks; sharing information about goals and performance; providing training and resources so employees are competent to decide; involving employees in goal setting and problem solving (participative management); removing unnecessary rules and close supervision; and rewarding initiative while tolerating honest mistakes.

  23. List down the three approaches of motivation. (Marks: 3)
    Topic: Motivation

    The three approaches of motivation are: (1) Individual differences approach — treats motivation as a function of the individual's traits, needs and characteristics that employees bring to the workplace. (2) Managerial approach — treats motivation as a function of goals and rewards; managers motivate by setting goals and linking rewards to performance. (3) Organizational approach — treats motivation as a function of the work environment; motivation is improved by redesigning jobs, changing organizational practices and creating a supportive environment.

  24. Differentiate motivators from hygiene factors as proposed by Herzberg. (Marks: 5)
    Topic: Motivation

    Herzberg's two-factor theory separates job factors into two sets. Motivators are intrinsic factors related to the content of the job itself — achievement, recognition, the work itself, responsibility and advancement/growth. When present, they increase job satisfaction and motivation; their absence does not cause dissatisfaction, only 'no satisfaction'. Hygiene factors are extrinsic factors related to the context of the job — company policy and administration, supervision, interpersonal relationships, salary and working conditions. When inadequate, they cause dissatisfaction; but improving them only removes dissatisfaction ('no dissatisfaction') without truly motivating. Thus satisfaction and dissatisfaction are not opposites but two separate continuums.

  25. Discuss the theory of equity, and also describe the reasons when employees perceive inequality. (Marks: 5)
    Topic: Motivation

    Equity theory (Adams) states that employees compare their input–outcome ratio (effort, experience, education versus pay, recognition, promotion) with the ratios of relevant others. Employees perceive inequality when: (1) they are under-rewarded — their ratio is lower than the comparison other's, producing anger and feelings of being cheated; or (2) they are over-rewarded — their ratio is higher, producing guilt. Perceived inequity creates tension that motivates the employee to restore equity by reducing inputs, asking for more outcomes, distorting perceptions of self or others, changing the comparison other, or leaving the organization.

  26. Differentiate between social and safety needs in Maslow's hierarchy. (Marks: 3)
    Topic: Motivation

    Safety needs (second level) are the needs for security and protection from physical and emotional harm — job security, safe working conditions, protection against unemployment and future uncertainty. Social needs (third level) are the needs for affection, belongingness, acceptance and friendship — friendly relations with co-workers, being part of a group and a sense of belonging. In Maslow's order, safety needs must be substantially satisfied before social needs become the dominant motivators.

  27. Explain Maslow's hierarchy of needs theory.
    Topic: Motivation

    Maslow proposed that within every human being there exists a hierarchy of five needs: (1) Physiological — hunger, thirst, shelter and other bodily needs. (2) Safety — security and protection from physical and emotional harm. (3) Social — affection, belonging, acceptance and friendship. (4) Esteem — internal factors such as self-respect, autonomy and achievement, and external factors such as status, recognition and attention. (5) Self-actualization — the drive to become what one is capable of becoming, including growth and self-fulfillment. As each need becomes substantially satisfied, the next one becomes dominant; a substantially satisfied need no longer motivates. Physiological and safety are lower-order needs satisfied externally, while social, esteem and self-actualization are higher-order needs satisfied internally.

  28. What are the motives for leadership? (Marks: 3)
    Topic: Motivation

    The motives that drive people to become leaders are mainly three needs: (1) Need for power — the desire to influence, control and have an impact on others; this is the most important need for people who become leaders, and it must be socialized (used for organizational goals) rather than personalized. (2) Need for achievement — the desire to accomplish challenging goals and excel. (3) Need for affiliation — the desire for friendly relationships and being liked, which is usually the least important of the three for leadership effectiveness, since a strong affiliation need can make a leader avoid unpopular decisions.
