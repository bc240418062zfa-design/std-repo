  1. What is financial management? State its main objective.
    Topic: Financial Management Basics & Financial Markets

    Financial management is the process of planning, acquiring and utilizing funds of a business in the most efficient manner so that the returns are maximized. It covers the investment decision (where to invest), the financing decision (from where to raise funds) and the dividend decision. The main objective of financial management is the maximization of shareholders' wealth, i.e. maximizing the market value of the company's shares, rather than mere profit maximization, because wealth maximization considers the timing of returns, risk and the long-run survival of the firm.

  2. Why a company would raise money through debt rather than equity?
    Topic: Financial Management Basics & Financial Markets

    A company may prefer debt financing because: (1) interest paid on debt is a tax-deductible expense, which lowers the overall cost of financing, whereas dividends are paid out of after-tax profit; (2) debt does not dilute the ownership and control of the existing shareholders; (3) debt is generally cheaper than equity because lenders take less risk and therefore demand a lower return; and (4) the fixed interest obligation can increase the return on equity through financial leverage when the firm earns more on the borrowed funds than the interest it pays.

  3. Differentiate between the money market and the capital market.
    Topic: Financial Management Basics & Financial Markets

    The money market is the market for short-term funds and securities with a maturity of one year or less, such as treasury bills, commercial paper and certificates of deposit; it is used mainly for liquidity and working-capital needs. The capital market is the market for long-term funds with a maturity of more than one year, such as shares and bonds; it is used to finance fixed assets and long-term projects of companies.

  4. Briefly discuss the benefits enjoyed by common shareholders over preferred shareholders.
    Topic: Financial Management Basics & Financial Markets

    Common shareholders enjoy several benefits over preferred shareholders: (1) they have voting rights and elect the board of directors, so they control the management of the company, while preferred shareholders normally have no voting rights; (2) they are the real owners of the company and have a residual claim on all earnings left after paying preferred dividends, so their income can grow without limit when the company prospers; (3) they have a residual claim on the assets of the company at liquidation; and (4) they can gain through capital appreciation of the share price, which is generally higher for common stock.

  5. Required rate of return for common stock and preferred stock is different. What is the reason of this difference?
    Topic: Financial Management Basics & Financial Markets

    The required rate of return differs because the risk attached to the two securities is different. Preferred stock carries a fixed dividend and has priority over common stock in dividend payment and in claim on assets at liquidation, so it is less risky and investors require a lower return on it. Common stock is the residual claim: its dividend is neither fixed nor guaranteed and common shareholders are paid last, so it is more risky and investors demand a higher required rate of return as compensation for the extra risk.

  6. What is the time value of money? Why is a rupee received today worth more than a rupee received in the future?
    Topic: Time Value of Money

    The time value of money is the concept that the value of money changes with time: a rupee in hand today is worth more than the same rupee received at a future date. The reasons are: (1) money received today can be invested to earn interest, so it grows over time (earning potential); (2) inflation reduces the purchasing power of future money; and (3) future receipts are uncertain and risky, whereas money in hand is certain. Because of this, cash flows occurring at different times cannot be compared directly and must be converted to a common point in time through compounding or discounting.

  7. What is the future value of a 5-year ordinary annuity that promises to pay you Rs. 300 at the end of each year? Assume that all payments are made @ 10 percent annually for five years.
    Topic: Time Value of Money

    Using the future value of an ordinary annuity formula: FV = PMT x [((1 + r)^n - 1) / r] = 300 x [((1.10)^5 - 1) / 0.10] = 300 x [(1.61051 - 1) / 0.10] = 300 x 6.1051 = Rs. 1,831.53. So the annuity will accumulate to Rs. 1,831.53 at the end of five years.

  8. If Affifa deposits Rs. 25,000 in her saving account at the end of each year for the next 15 years, how much she will get at the end of this period if the interest rate is 10%?
    Topic: Time Value of Money

    This is the future value of an ordinary annuity: FV = PMT x [((1 + r)^n - 1) / r] = 25,000 x [((1.10)^15 - 1) / 0.10] = 25,000 x [(4.17725 - 1) / 0.10] = 25,000 x 31.7725 = Rs. 794,312.04. Affifa will receive approximately Rs. 794,312 at the end of 15 years.

  9. Want to be a millionaire? No problem! Suppose you are currently 21 years old, and can earn 10 percent on your money. How much must you invest today in order to accumulate Rs. 1 million by the time you reach age 65?
    Topic: Time Value of Money

    The investment period is 65 - 21 = 44 years. Using the present value formula: PV = FV / (1 + r)^n = 1,000,000 / (1.10)^44 = 1,000,000 / 66.2640 = Rs. 15,091.13. An investment of only about Rs. 15,091 today at 10% per annum will grow to Rs. 1 million in 44 years, which shows the power of compound interest over a long period.

  10. Differentiate between an ordinary annuity and an annuity due. Also define a perpetuity.
    Topic: Time Value of Money

    An annuity is a series of equal payments made at equal intervals of time. In an ordinary annuity (also called a deferred annuity), payments are made at the END of each period, e.g. annual interest payments on a bond. In an annuity due, payments are made at the BEGINNING of each period, e.g. house rent paid in advance. Because each payment of an annuity due is received one period earlier, its present and future values are higher than those of an ordinary annuity. A perpetuity is an annuity whose payments continue forever, i.e. it has no maturity date; its present value is calculated as PV = PMT / r.

  11. What is capital budgeting? Name and briefly describe the techniques used for evaluating capital projects.
    Topic: Capital Budgeting & Capital Rationing

    Capital budgeting is the process of planning and evaluating long-term investments in fixed assets and projects, whose costs and benefits extend over many years. The main techniques are: (1) Payback Period - the time required to recover the initial investment from the project's cash inflows; (2) Net Present Value (NPV) - the present value of cash inflows minus the initial investment, discounted at the required rate of return; a project is accepted if NPV is positive; (3) Internal Rate of Return (IRR) - the discount rate at which NPV becomes zero; a project is accepted if IRR exceeds the required return; (4) Profitability Index (PI) - the ratio of the present value of cash inflows to the initial investment; and (5) Modified IRR (MIRR) - a modified form of IRR that assumes reinvestment at the cost of capital and gives a single rate for projects with non-normal cash flows.

  12. Suppose OAGDCL Company has a "Project M" which requires initial cash outflow of Rs. 2.8 million. Cash inflow generated by Project M at the end of 1st year is Rs. 7.2 million and Project required more capital of Rs. 6 million at the end of 2nd year. OAGDCL Company wants to evaluate the Project by using capital budgeting technique. Required: Which of the following capital budgeting technique will not give more suitable result to OAGDCL Company and why? Internal Rate of Return (IRR); Modified Internal Rate of Return (MIRR)
    Topic: Capital Budgeting & Capital Rationing

    The Internal Rate of Return (IRR) will not give a suitable result for Project M. Project M has non-normal (non-conventional) cash flows because the sign of the cash flows changes more than once: negative in year 0 (-2.8 million), positive in year 1 (+7.2 million) and negative again in year 2 (-6 million). For such projects the IRR equation produces multiple IRRs, so it becomes impossible to decide which rate to compare with the required rate of return. The Modified Internal Rate of Return (MIRR) removes this defect by discounting/compounding the cash flows at the cost of capital and always gives a single, reliable rate, so MIRR should be used for Project M.

  13. Hammad Inc. is considering two alternative, mutually exclusive projects. Both projects require an initial investment of Rs. 10,000 and are typical, average-risk projects for the firm. Project A has an expected life of 2 years with after-tax cash inflow of Rs. 6,000 and Rs. 8,000 at the end of year 1 and 2, respectively. Project B has an expected life of 4 years with after-tax cash inflow of Rs. 4,000 at the end of each of next 4 years. The firm's cost of capital is 10 percent. If the projects cannot be repeated, which project will be selected, and what is the net present value?
    Topic: Capital Budgeting & Capital Rationing

    NPV of Project A = -10,000 + 6,000/(1.10) + 8,000/(1.10)^2 = -10,000 + 5,454.55 + 6,611.57 = Rs. 2,066.12. NPV of Project B = -10,000 + 4,000 x PVIFA(10%, 4 years) = -10,000 + 4,000 x 3.1699 = -10,000 + 12,679.46 = Rs. 2,679.46. Both projects have positive NPVs, but since they are mutually exclusive and cannot be repeated, the project with the higher NPV is selected. Project B will be selected, with an NPV of Rs. 2,679.46.

  14. Differentiate between a sunk cost and an opportunity cost in capital budgeting, with examples.
    Topic: Capital Budgeting & Capital Rationing

    A sunk cost is a cost that has already been incurred in the past and cannot be recovered, whatever decision is taken now; therefore it is irrelevant and is EXCLUDED from capital budgeting cash flows. Example: the amount already spent on a market research or feasibility study for a proposed project. An opportunity cost is the benefit that is sacrificed by accepting one alternative instead of the next best alternative; it is a relevant cost and is INCLUDED in capital budgeting cash flows. Example: if a company uses its own existing building for a new project instead of renting it out, the rent forgone is the opportunity cost of the project.

  15. What is capital rationing? Why does it arise?
    Topic: Capital Budgeting & Capital Rationing

    Capital rationing is a situation in which a company has limited funds available for investment and therefore cannot accept all positive-NPV projects; it must select the combination of projects that gives the maximum total NPV within the budget ceiling. It arises because of internal limits fixed by management (e.g. a self-imposed budget), imperfect capital markets, high cost or unavailability of external finance, fear of debt, lack of managerial talent and human resources to handle many projects, or because the best projects require very high initial investment.

  16. Mr. Salem wants to make long term investments, he decides to purchase bond from XYZ Company, that pays coupon on its bond quarterly; calculate the intrinsic value of bond under the following circumstances: A 5 year bond with 12% coupon rate is selling at Rs. 1,650. Face value of the bond is Rs. 1,000. Required rate of return is 16%.
    Topic: Bonds & Stock Valuation

    Because the coupon is paid quarterly: quarterly coupon payment = 1,000 x 12% / 4 = Rs. 30; number of periods = 5 x 4 = 20; quarterly required rate = 16% / 4 = 4%. Intrinsic value = 30 x PVIFA(4%, 20) + 1,000 x PVIF(4%, 20) = 30 x 13.5903 + 1,000 x 0.4564 = 407.71 + 456.39 = Rs. 864.10. The intrinsic value of the bond is Rs. 864.10, which is much less than its market price of Rs. 1,650; the bond is heavily overpriced in the market, so Mr. Salem should not purchase it at this price.

  17. ABC Company's stock is currently trading at Rs. 150 and fair value of stock is Rs. 127.50. What should be decision of rational investor in this situation (buying or selling)? Justify your answer.
    Topic: Bonds & Stock Valuation

    The market price of the stock (Rs. 150) is higher than its fair (intrinsic) value (Rs. 127.50), which means the stock is overvalued in the market. A rational investor should SELL the stock if he holds it, and should not buy it at the current price, because the price is expected to fall towards its fair value and the investor would suffer a capital loss. A rational investor buys a stock only when its market price is below its fair value (undervalued) and sells when it is above fair value.

  18. Pharmaceutical company's common stock is expected to pay Rs. 10 dividend. This share is selling in market for Rs. 55.50. What is the value of this stock to an investor who requires 13% rate of return? What would you suggest in this case (buying or selling)?
    Topic: Bonds & Stock Valuation

    Treating the constant dividend as a perpetuity, the value of the stock is: P0 = DIV / r = 10 / 0.13 = Rs. 76.92. The value of the stock to the investor (Rs. 76.92) is greater than its market price (Rs. 55.50), which means the stock is undervalued in the market. The investor should BUY the stock, because it is available at a price lower than its fair value and its price is expected to rise.

  19. Why does the market price of a bond move inversely with the market interest rate?
    Topic: Bonds & Stock Valuation

    A bond pays a fixed coupon fixed at the time of issue. When the market interest rate rises, newly issued bonds offer higher coupons, so the existing bond with its lower fixed coupon becomes less attractive; investors will buy it only at a lower price, and its market price falls until its yield matches the market rate. Conversely, when the market interest rate falls, the existing bond's higher fixed coupon becomes more attractive and its price rises. Hence bond prices and market interest rates move in opposite directions. This effect is stronger for long-term bonds than for short-term bonds.

  20. State the dividend growth model (Gordon's model) for valuing a common stock and explain its components.
    Topic: Bonds & Stock Valuation

    The constant growth (Gordon) model values a share whose dividends are expected to grow at a constant rate g forever: P0 = DIV1 / (r - g), where P0 is the current fair price of the stock, DIV1 is the dividend expected at the end of the coming year (DIV1 = DIV0 x (1 + g)), r is the required rate of return of the investor, and g is the constant growth rate of dividends, which can be estimated as g = ROE x Plowback ratio. The model is valid only when r is greater than g. It can also be rearranged as r = DIV1/P0 + g, i.e. the required return equals the dividend yield plus the capital gain yield (growth rate).

  21. Suppose that your estimates of the possible returns from investing in the common stock of Eagle Eye Productions were as follows: Economic Conditions: Boom - Probability 60%, Returns 55%; Recession - Probability 40%, Returns 45%. You are required to compute the following with the help of provided information: a) expected value of return b) standard deviation of return
    Topic: Risk, Return & Portfolio Theory

    a) Expected return = (0.60 x 55) + (0.40 x 45) = 33 + 18 = 51%. b) Variance = 0.60 x (55 - 51)^2 + 0.40 x (45 - 51)^2 = 0.60 x 16 + 0.40 x 36 = 9.6 + 14.4 = 24. Standard deviation = sqrt(24) = 4.90%. So the expected return of the stock is 51% with a standard deviation of 4.90%.

  22. A security analyst has estimated the following returns on the stocks of 4 large companies: Company A Weightage 25% Expected Return 12%; Company B 25% 11.5%; Company C 25% 10%; Company D 25% 9.5%. You are required to calculate the expected return on this portfolio.
    Topic: Risk, Return & Portfolio Theory

    The expected return of a portfolio is the weighted average of the expected returns of its securities: E(Rp) = 0.25 x 12 + 0.25 x 11.5 + 0.25 x 10 + 0.25 x 9.5 = 3 + 2.875 + 2.5 + 2.375 = 10.75%. The expected return on the portfolio is 10.75%.

  23. How portfolio risk will be affected if its positively correlated securities are replaced by negatively correlated securities? Justify your answer.
    Topic: Risk, Return & Portfolio Theory

    The portfolio risk will decrease. Portfolio risk depends not only on the risk of individual securities but also on the correlation between their returns. Positively correlated securities move in the same direction at the same time, so losses in one are matched by losses in the other and there is little risk reduction. Negatively correlated securities move in opposite directions: when the return of one falls, the return of the other rises, which offsets the fall and stabilizes the overall portfolio return. Therefore replacing positively correlated securities with negatively correlated securities increases the benefit of diversification and reduces the total risk of the portfolio.

  24. Compare 3 Investments in terms of Risk & Return and decide which one is the best Investment? Investment A: Risk (Std Dev) 23.24%, Expected Return 10%; Investment B: Risk (Std Dev) 5%, Expected Return 10%; Investment C: Risk (Std Dev) 30%, Expected Return 30%.
    Topic: Risk, Return & Portfolio Theory

    Because the investments have different expected returns, they should be compared through the coefficient of variation (CV = Standard Deviation / Expected Return), which measures risk per unit of return. CV of A = 23.24 / 10 = 2.32; CV of B = 5 / 10 = 0.50; CV of C = 30 / 30 = 1.00. Investment B has the lowest coefficient of variation, i.e. the least risk for each unit of return; it also offers the same expected return (10%) as Investment A with far less risk. Investment B is therefore the best investment.

  25. Differentiate between systematic risk and unsystematic risk. Which of them can be eliminated through diversification?
    Topic: Risk, Return & Portfolio Theory

    Systematic risk (also called market risk or non-diversifiable risk) affects the whole market and all securities at the same time; it arises from economy-wide factors such as inflation, interest rate changes, recession and political instability. It cannot be eliminated through diversification and is measured by beta. Unsystematic risk (also called company-specific, unique or diversifiable risk) affects only a particular company or industry; it arises from factors such as strikes, management decisions, product failure and loss of a key customer. Unsystematic risk CAN be virtually eliminated by holding a well-diversified portfolio of securities from different industries.

  26. What is the coefficient of variation (CV)? Why is it preferred over standard deviation when comparing investments?
    Topic: Risk, Return & Portfolio Theory

    The coefficient of variation is a relative measure of risk, calculated as CV = Standard Deviation / Expected Return. It expresses the risk per unit of expected return. Standard deviation is an absolute measure, so it cannot fairly compare two investments whose expected returns are very different: a higher standard deviation may simply accompany a much higher return. CV removes this problem by scaling risk with return, so the investment with the lower CV is preferred because it offers less risk for each unit of return.
