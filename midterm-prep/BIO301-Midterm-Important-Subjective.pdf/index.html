  1. Write the sub-types (sub-disciplines) of genetics.
    Topic: A. Mendelian Genetics and Basic Concepts

    There are four sub-disciplines of genetics, although some geneticists classify genetics into many sub-disciplines: (1) Transmission (Classical) Genetics, (2) Population Genetics, (3) Quantitative Genetics, (4) Molecular Genetics.

  2. Write the modern genetics concepts.
    Topic: A. Mendelian Genetics and Basic Concepts

    Modern genetics concepts include: chromosomes and gene(s); Mendelian genetics and non-Mendelian genetics; genotypes and phenotypes; mutations and polymorphism.

  3. What is a gene?
    Topic: A. Mendelian Genetics and Basic Concepts

    A gene is a heredity unit on chromosomes.

  4. What is a trait?
    Topic: A. Mendelian Genetics and Basic Concepts

    A trait is the variation for a character (i.e. brown, black, white etc.).

  5. Define allele.
    Topic: A. Mendelian Genetics and Basic Concepts

    An allele is one of the possible forms of a gene. Most genes have two alleles, a dominant allele and a recessive allele. If an organism is heterozygous for that trait, or possesses one of each allele, then the dominant trait is expressed. Alleles were first defined by Gregor Mendel in the law of segregation.

  6. Define genotype and phenotype.
    Topic: A. Mendelian Genetics and Basic Concepts

    Genotype and phenotype: the genetic make-up of an organism is called genotype, while the physical appearance of an organism is called phenotype.

  7. Define dominant and recessive traits.
    Topic: A. Mendelian Genetics and Basic Concepts

    A dominant trait is one that is phenotypically expressed in heterozygotes. A recessive trait is expressed only when two copies of the gene are present. In Mendel's experiments, the trait that expressed itself over the other (e.g. round over wrinkled) was dominant, while the trait that did not show in the first generation (wrinkled) was recessive.

  8. Define locus.
    Topic: A. Mendelian Genetics and Basic Concepts

    A locus (plural loci) in genetics is a fixed position on a chromosome, like the position of a gene or a marker (genetic marker). Each chromosome carries many genes; humans have an estimated 19,000-20,000 haploid protein-coding genes on the 23 different chromosomes.

  9. Why did Mendel choose the garden pea for his experiments? Write the properties of the garden pea.
    Topic: A. Mendelian Genetics and Basic Concepts

    Mendel used pea plants because they can either self-pollinate or be cross-pollinated, and they have a very short life cycle. Properties of the garden pea: it is easy to cultivate and grew well in his garden; its flowers are hermaphrodite, i.e. the pea plant has both male and female sexual organs; it has a short generation time; it produces many offspring; and its seeds occur in a variety of shapes and colours.

  10. How did Mendel prove heredity in variation among individuals?
    Topic: A. Mendelian Genetics and Basic Concepts

    He deduced that genes come in pairs and are inherited as distinct units, one from each parent. Mendel tracked the segregation of parental genes and their appearance in the offspring as dominant or recessive traits. He recognized the mathematical patterns of inheritance from one generation to the next.

  11. What are the expectations of Mendel's law of inheritance?
    Topic: A. Mendelian Genetics and Basic Concepts

    Gregor Mendel crossed various pure lines of garden peas and, by following their hybrid progeny, observed that traits are inherited as alternate states of independent units of inheritance or genes (which Mendel called "factors"), and that these units come in pairs.

  12. State the law of independent assortment with an example.
    Topic: A. Mendelian Genetics and Basic Concepts

    Alleles for different traits are distributed to sex cells independently of one another; traits are transmitted to offspring independently of one another. Example: a dihybrid cross of true-breeding plants for two traits - a plant with green pod colour and yellow seed colour (GGYY, both dominant) cross-pollinated with a plant having yellow pod colour and green seeds (ggyy, both recessive) gives F1 plants that are heterozygous GgYy.

  13. Define the chromosomal theory of inheritance.
    Topic: A. Mendelian Genetics and Basic Concepts

    The chromosomal theory of inheritance states that genes have specific loci on chromosomes. Mendel proposed the law of segregation and the law of independent assortment to explain the inheritance behind his pea plant traits; later, Boveri and Sutton developed the chromosome theory of inheritance, which stated that chromosomes were responsible for the heredity Mendel observed.

  14. Differentiate between particulate and blending inheritance.
    Topic: A. Mendelian Genetics and Basic Concepts

    The particulate hypothesis was offered by Gregor Mendel, who discovered that traits are inherited in discrete units that we now know as genes. Instead of blending, the offspring inherits a version of a gene, called an allele, from each of the parents. Only one allele is expressed depending on a number of factors. (Blending inheritance, by contrast, held that parental traits blend in the offspring.)

  15. Differentiate between monohybrid and dihybrid cross.
    Topic: A. Mendelian Genetics and Basic Concepts

    Monohybrid cross: cross-fertilization of true-breeding plants which differ in just one character is called a monohybrid cross; with it, Mendel determined the segregation of alleles at a single gene locus. Dihybrid cross: determines whether alleles at two different gene loci segregate dependently or independently.

  16. Define pure cross and hybrid cross.
    Topic: A. Mendelian Genetics and Basic Concepts

    Pure cross: a cross between a true-breed plant/animal with another true-breed plant/animal is called a pure cross. Hybrid cross: an organism that is the offspring of genetically dissimilar parents; in other words, offspring produced by breeding in plants or animals of dissimilar parents.

  17. Differentiate between Mendelian and non-Mendelian genetics.
    Topic: A. Mendelian Genetics and Basic Concepts

    In Mendelian inheritance, each parent contributes one of two possible alleles for a trait. Non-Mendelian genetics covers patterns of inheritance that deviate from a Mendelian pattern, e.g. linkage, maternal effect, epigenetic inheritance and mitochondrial inheritance.

  18. What are the types of variation?
    Topic: B. Variation and Non-Mendelian Patterns

    There are two types of variation: (1) Discontinuous variation - entirely genetically controlled, cannot be altered by external conditions (e.g. blood group, colour blindness, dwarfism). (2) Continuous variation - the situation in which there are many intermediates between the extremes (e.g. shade of hair colour between black and blond).

  19. Differentiate between continuous and discontinuous variation.
    Topic: B. Variation and Non-Mendelian Patterns

    Variation is all the differences which exist between members of the same species. Continuous variation is variation that has no limit on the value that can occur within a population (many intermediates between extremes). Discontinuous variation is entirely genetically controlled, cannot be altered by external conditions, and falls into distinct categories (e.g. blood group, colour blindness, dwarfism).

  20. Differentiate between acquired and inherited variations.
    Topic: B. Variation and Non-Mendelian Patterns

    Inherited variation: variation in a characteristic that is a result of genetic inheritance from the parents. Children usually look a little like their father and a little like their mother, but will not be identical to either parent; inherited variations result from the activity of genes, are genetically controlled and cannot be altered (e.g. blood groups, finger prints, sex). Acquired variation: a non-heritable change in a function or structure of a living organism caused after birth by disease, injury, accident, deliberate modification, repeated use, disuse, misuse, nutrition or other environmental influences during a lifetime.

  21. Define co-dominance with examples.
    Topic: B. Variation and Non-Mendelian Patterns

    Co-dominance is a situation when both alleles appear in the phenotype; neither allele is dominant and both alleles are expressed in a heterozygous individual (parallel behaviour of both alleles). Examples: AB blood group (both A and B alleles equally expressed), roan fur in cattle (white and red hair equally expressed), roan inheritance and Appaloosa horse.

  22. Define incomplete dominance.
    Topic: B. Variation and Non-Mendelian Patterns

    Incomplete dominance is a situation in which neither allele is dominant. A new phenotype appears which is a blend of both alleles.

  23. Define multiple alleles with an example.
    Topic: B. Variation and Non-Mendelian Patterns

    Multiple alleles are three or more alternative forms of a gene (alleles) that can occupy the same locus. However, only two of the alleles can be present in a single organism. Example: the ABO system of blood groups is controlled by three alleles, only two of which are present in an individual.

  24. Define mono-allelic, di-allelic and poly-allelic genes.
    Topic: B. Variation and Non-Mendelian Patterns

    30% of genes in humans are di-allelic - they exist in two forms. 70% are mono-allelic; they exist only in one form. Few are poly-allelic.

  25. Define polygenic traits with an example.
    Topic: B. Variation and Non-Mendelian Patterns

    Most traits are not controlled by a single gene locus, but by the combined interaction of many gene loci. These traits are called polygenic traits. Examples: human height, eye colour and pigmentation.

  26. Define pleiotropy.
    Topic: B. Variation and Non-Mendelian Patterns

    The ability of a gene to affect an organism in many ways is called pleiotropy. Pleiotropy refers to an allele which has more than one effect on the phenotype.

  27. Define epistasis with an example.
    Topic: B. Variation and Non-Mendelian Patterns

    Epistasis is a phenomenon in which the effect of one gene is dependent on the presence of one or more modifier genes; it is a type of polygenic inheritance where alleles at one gene locus can hide or prevent expression of alleles at a second gene locus. Example: in dogs, a dominant allele B gives black coat and recessive b gives brown, but a second gene (e) controls eumelanin deposition in fur; dogs homozygous recessive at this locus (ee) will have yellow fur no matter which alleles are at the first locus.

  28. What are the complications to inheritance patterns?
    Topic: B. Variation and Non-Mendelian Patterns

    Complications to inheritance patterns include: multiple genes - epistasis and polygenic traits; and genes and the environment - sex-influenced traits and incomplete penetrance.

  29. Differentiate between low and full (high) penetrance.
    Topic: B. Variation and Non-Mendelian Patterns

    Low penetrance: an allele with low penetrance will only sometimes produce the symptom or trait with which it has been associated at a detectable level; in low penetrance it is difficult to distinguish environmental from genetic factors. Highly (fully) penetrant: if an allele is highly penetrant, the trait it produces will almost always be apparent in an individual carrying the allele.

  30. Explain genetic data / properties of genetic data.
    Topic: B. Variation and Non-Mendelian Patterns

    Genetic information is personal, permanent and predictive; it is used for diagnostics and treatments, and predictive information allows planning. Its applications include screening (newborn, selected population, carriers) and pharmacogenetics (individualized medicine). A genetic database is one or more sets of genetic data (genes, gene products, variants, phenotypes) stored together with software to enable users to retrieve genetic data, add genetic data and extract information from the data.

  31. What is a chromosome?
    Topic: C. Chromosomes and Chromatin

    Genetic material in cells is organized into chromosomes. Prokaryotes generally have one circular chromosome. Eukaryotes have linear chromosomes in their nuclei.

  32. What are the components of a typical chromosome? (as given in the sources)
    Topic: C. Chromosomes and Chromatin

    In humans, each cell normally contains 23 pairs of chromosomes, for a total of 46. Twenty-two of these pairs, called autosomes, look the same in both males and females. The 23rd pair, the sex chromosomes, differ between males and females.

  33. Write the types of chromosomes.
    Topic: C. Chromosomes and Chromatin

    There are two types of eukaryotic chromosomes: (1) Autosomes - paired chromosomes with the same length, shape, centromere location and genes; any chromosomes other than sex chromosomes are called autosomes. (2) Sex chromosomes - members of a pair of chromosomes that differ between males and females.

  34. Name the types of chromosomes based on centromere position.
    Topic: C. Chromosomes and Chromatin

    On the basis of centromere position, chromosomes are of four types: (1) Metacentric - centromere in the middle, two equal arms. (2) Submetacentric - centromere slightly away from the centre, arms unequal. (3) Acrocentric - centromere near one end, one arm very short. (4) Telocentric - centromere at the very end of the chromosome.

  35. Differentiate between chromosome and chromatin.
    Topic: C. Chromosomes and Chromatin

    DNA exists in chromatin form in the nucleus. The chromatin condenses to form chromosomes during cell division. The chromatin in the nucleus forms a "beads-on-a-string" structure or nucleosome; the "beads" are octamer histone proteins and the "string" is the double-stranded DNA. Chromosomes are the rod-shaped, filamentous bodies present in the nucleus and are the carriers of genes; they are not visible in the active nucleus due to high water content but can be seen during cell division, as chromatin fibres undergo folding, coiling and supercoiling so the chromosomes become progressively thicker and smaller.

  36. Define chromatin. Differentiate between euchromatin and heterochromatin.
    Topic: C. Chromosomes and Chromatin

    The complexes between eukaryotic DNA and proteins are called chromatin. Chromatin is made up of DNA, protein and RNA. There are two forms of chromatin in the interphase nucleus: euchromatin and heterochromatin. Cytologically, heterochromatin stains more intensely than euchromatin; heterochromatin is the tightly packed form of chromatin, often found at the periphery of the nucleus.

  37. By which method can euchromatin be changed into heterochromatin?
    Topic: C. Chromosomes and Chromatin

    Histone methylation, histone deacetylation and the corepressor complex.

  38. What is a nucleosome? Write a note on the different kinds of histone proteins.
    Topic: C. Chromosomes and Chromatin

    A nucleosome is a structural unit of a eukaryotic chromosome, consisting of a length of DNA coiled around a core of histones. Five abundant histones are H1, H2A, H2B, H3 and H4. The core histones are H2A, H2B, H3 and H4, which associate with DNA to form the nucleosome, while H1 (and its homologue H5) are linker histones involved in higher-order structures of chromatin.

  39. Differentiate between nucleosome and nucleotide.
    Topic: C. Chromosomes and Chromatin

    Nucleosome: a structural unit of a eukaryotic chromosome, consisting of a length of DNA coiled around a core of histones. Nucleotide: a compound consisting of a nucleoside linked to a phosphate group; nucleotides form the basic structural unit of nucleic acids such as DNA.

  40. What is the chromosome scaffold?
    Topic: C. Chromosomes and Chromatin

    Scaffold: (1) In genetics, the chromosome structure consisting entirely of non-histone proteins remaining after all the DNA and histone proteins have been removed from a chromosome. (2) In genomic mapping, a series of contigs that are in the right order but not necessarily connected in one continuous stretch of sequence.

  41. What is chromosome topology?
    Topic: C. Chromosomes and Chromatin

    Chromosome compaction is a necessary feature of all living cells including bacteria. It is intricately linked to chromosome topology and the selective pressures introduced by gene organization along the genome sequence. This is referred to as the "transverse" chromosome topology.

  42. What are polytene chromosomes? Give an example.
    Topic: C. Chromosomes and Chromatin

    Polytene (giant) chromosomes are found in the salivary glands of some Diptera. These chromosomes are very long and thick, hence known as giant chromosomes. They were first discovered by Balbiani in 1881 in dipteran salivary glands and are thus also known as salivary gland chromosomes. They have thousands of DNA strands and provide a high level of function in certain tissues such as salivary glands; the best understood are those of Drosophila, Chironomus and Rhynchosciara flies. Giant chromosomes have also been discovered in suspensors of young embryos of many plants, but these do not show the bands so typical of salivary gland chromosomes.

  43. Write the types of banding in chromosomes.
    Topic: C. Chromosomes and Chromatin

    Q banding: Q bands are the fluorescent bands observed after quinacrine mustard staining with UV light; the distal ends are not stained by this technique. R banding: the R bands (reverse) are those located in the zones that do not fluoresce with quinacrine mustard, i.e. they are between the Q bands. C banding: the C bands correspond to constitutive heterochromatin. G banding (Giemsa banding): a technique used in cytogenetics to produce a visible karyotype by staining condensed chromosomes; heterochromatic regions, which tend to be rich in adenine and thymine (AT-rich) DNA and relatively gene-poor, stain more darkly in G-banding.

  44. Which chemicals are used in chromosome banding?
    Topic: C. Chromosomes and Chromatin

    Quinacrine mustard is used in Q banding (fluorescent bands are observed with UV light after this staining), and Giemsa stain is used in G banding to produce a visible karyotype by staining condensed chromosomes.

  45. Define sister chromatids.
    Topic: C. Chromosomes and Chromatin

    A sister chromatid refers to the identical copies (chromatids) formed by the replication of a chromosome, with both copies joined together by a common centromere. In other words, a sister chromatid may also be said to be "one-half" of the duplicated chromosome.

  46. Differentiate between chromosome and chromatid.
    Topic: C. Chromosomes and Chromatin

    A chromosome is made up of two chromatids which are joined by the centromere. The chromatids separate from each other during mitosis to form two new chromosomes. The DNA making up a chromosome is dispersed as chromatin.

  47. What are telomeres?
    Topic: C. Chromosomes and Chromatin

    The two ends of a chromosome are known as telomeres. These are required for the replication and stability of the chromosomes. When telomeres are damaged or removed due to chromosome breakage, the ends can readily fuse or unite with broken ends of other chromosomes.

  48. Define CEN DNA and write its sub-domains. What is the kinetochore?
    Topic: C. Chromosomes and Chromatin

    Within the centromere region are locations where spindle fibres attach, which consist of DNA as well as protein. The actual location where the attachment occurs is called the kinetochore and is composed of both DNA and protein. The DNA sequence within these regions is called CEN DNA; it is 120 base pairs long and consists of sub-domains CDE-I, CDE-II and CDE-III.

  49. What is linker DNA?
    Topic: C. Chromosomes and Chromatin

    Linker DNA is the stretch of DNA between two nucleosomes in the chromatin fibre. It connects adjacent nucleosomes (the "string" between the "beads" in the beads-on-a-string structure) and is the region where the linker histone H1 binds, helping in the higher-order packing of chromatin.

  50. Differentiate between diploid and haploid cells.
    Topic: C. Chromosomes and Chromatin

    Diploid cells are body (somatic) cells; they are called (2n) cells and contain two sets of chromosomes - examples: skin cells, brain cells, bone cells, blood cells; in humans there are ~220 types of somatic cells. Haploid cells are sex cells (gametes); they are (n) cells containing one set of chromosomes - sperms and ova are gametes produced in males and females respectively.

  51. Differentiate between prokaryotic and eukaryotic chromosomes.
    Topic: C. Chromosomes and Chromatin

    In prokaryotes, the circular chromosome is contained in the cytoplasm in an area called the nucleoid; prokaryotes usually have only one chromosome, which bears little morphological resemblance to eukaryotic chromosomes, and it exists as a highly folded and coiled structure dispersed throughout the cell. In eukaryotes, all of the cell's chromosomes are stored inside a structure called the nucleus; each eukaryotic chromosome is composed of DNA coiled and condensed around nuclear proteins called histones, is condensed and visible during cell division, and at the beginning of mitosis consists of two threads (sister chromatids) joined by a centromere.

  52. Differentiate between monocot and dicot chromosomes.
    Topic: C. Chromosomes and Chromatin

    In general, plants have longer chromosomes than animals, and species having a lesser chromosome number usually have long chromosomes. Dicots in general have a higher number of chromosomes than monocots. Chromosomes are longer in monocots than in dicots.

  53. Write the number of chromosomes in gold fish, chicken and fruit fly.
    Topic: C. Chromosomes and Chromatin

    In gold fish the number of chromosomes is 94. In chicken the number of chromosomes is 78, and in fruit fly the number of chromosomes is 8.

  54. Define nucleotide, nucleoside, purine and pyrimidine.
    Topic: D. DNA, Nucleotides and Replication

    A nucleoside consists of a pentose sugar and a nitrogenous base, but no phosphate group. A nucleotide consists of a pentose sugar, a nitrogenous base and one to three phosphate groups; nucleotides form the basic structural unit of nucleic acids such as DNA. Purines and pyrimidines are nitrogenous bases that make up the two different kinds of nucleotide bases in DNA and RNA: the two-carbon nitrogen ring bases (adenine and guanine) are purines, while the one-carbon nitrogen ring bases (thymine and cytosine) are pyrimidines.

  55. Name the three bonding parts which make a complete nucleotide.
    Topic: D. DNA, Nucleotides and Replication

    A nitrogenous base, a pentose sugar, and a phosphate.

  56. Define the G-C rule and the cluster rule.
    Topic: D. DNA, Nucleotides and Replication

    G-C rule: the (G+C)% is constant within a species, but often differs between species. Cluster rule: pyrimidines often occur in clusters, and hence on the complementary strand purines do likewise. (These are among Chargaff's rules on DNA base composition; the GC rule and cluster rule reveal multiple levels of information in our genomes.)

  57. Write a note on leading strand, lagging strand, Okazaki fragments and gyrase.
    Topic: D. DNA, Nucleotides and Replication

    Leading strand and lagging strand: when replication begins, the two parent DNA strands are separated; one strand of DNA is called the leading strand, which is replicated continuously in the 3' to 5' direction, while the other strand is called the lagging strand and is replicated discontinuously in short sections. Okazaki fragments: the small fragments formed during discontinuous replication of the lagging strand are called Okazaki fragments; during lagging strand synthesis, DNA ligase connects the Okazaki fragments after replacement of the RNA primers with DNA nucleotides. Gyrases: the double helical structure is opened by enzyme gyrase; these enzymes remove the helix (supercoiling) and produce a nick to uncoil DNA.

  58. What are the major and minor grooves of DNA? (length and nucleotides in the major groove, as asked in past papers)
    Topic: D. DNA, Nucleotides and Replication

    The strand backbones are closer together on one side of the helix than on the other. The major groove occurs where the backbones are far apart; the minor groove occurs where they are close together. The grooves twist around the molecule on opposite sides. Certain proteins bind to DNA to alter its structure or to regulate transcription or replication. It is easier for these DNA-binding proteins to interact with the bases (the internal parts of the DNA molecule) on the major groove side because the backbones are not in the way. (The sources describe the grooves qualitatively and do not state numeric lengths.)

  59. What was the work of Franklin and Wilkins?
    Topic: D. DNA, Nucleotides and Replication

    Genes are encoded in the sequence of bases, and the double helix model of DNA was supported by the work of Rosalind Franklin and Maurice Wilkins (their X-ray diffraction work on DNA fibres). The structure of DNA was discovered in 1953 by Francis Crick and James Watson.

  60. What is the location of intronic sequences with respect to exons?
    Topic: D. DNA, Nucleotides and Replication

    Sequences that are joined together in the final mature RNA after RNA splicing are exons; introns are the intervening sequences located between exons within a gene. Introns are found in the genes of most organisms and many viruses, and can be located in a wide range of genes, including those that generate proteins, ribosomal RNA (rRNA) and transfer RNA (tRNA).

  61. What is interphase? Enlist the phases of the cell cycle.
    Topic: E. Cell Cycle, Mitosis and Meiosis

    Interphase is the phase of the cell cycle in which a typical cell spends most of its life; during this phase the cell copies its DNA in preparation for mitosis, and it was formerly called the resting phase. Before a cell enters cell division it needs to take nutrients; preparation for division happens in three steps: G1 phase, S phase and G2 phase. The cell cycle has two main phases: (1) Interphase, (2) Mitotic phase (prophase, metaphase, anaphase, telophase).

  62. Differentiate between G1, S phase and G2 phase.
    Topic: E. Cell Cycle, Mitosis and Meiosis

    S phase: synthesis of new DNA for daughter cells. G1 phase: the new cell absorbs nutrients and grows larger. G2 phase: the cell continues to grow, gets too large and needs to divide.

  63. What is the mitotic apparatus?
    Topic: E. Cell Cycle, Mitosis and Meiosis

    The spindle apparatus (or mitotic spindle) refers to the cytoskeletal structure of eukaryotic cells that forms during cell division to separate sister chromatids between daughter cells. Besides chromosomes, the spindle apparatus is composed of hundreds of proteins.

  64. Explain the prophase stage of mitosis.
    Topic: E. Cell Cycle, Mitosis and Meiosis

    Prophase is the first phase of mitosis, the process that separates the duplicated genetic material carried in the nucleus of a parent cell into two identical daughter cells. Prophase is characterized by four events: (1) chromosomes condense and become more visible (DNA wraps around histones that condense it); (2) the nuclear membrane disappears; (3) centrioles are separated and take positions on the opposite poles of the cell; (4) spindle fibres form and radiate toward the centre of the cell.

  65. Write a note on prophase, metaphase, anaphase and telophase of mitosis.
    Topic: E. Cell Cycle, Mitosis and Meiosis

    Prophase: chromosomes condense and become visible, the nuclear membrane disappears, centrioles separate to opposite poles and spindle fibres form. Metaphase: chromosomes line up across the middle of the cell; spindle fibres connect the centromere of each sister chromatid to the poles of the cell. Anaphase: the centromere splits, sister chromatids are separated and become individual chromosomes, and the separated chromatids move to opposite poles (securin destruction releases separase, resulting in split of centromeres); the cell stretches into an oval shape. Telophase: chromosomes start to uncoil, a nuclear envelope forms around the chromosomes at each pole, spindle fibres break down and dissolve, nucleoli reappear and a daughter nucleus is formed in each daughter cell - the effects of prophase are reversed.

  66. Define mitosis and meiosis.
    Topic: E. Cell Cycle, Mitosis and Meiosis

    Cells give rise to new cells through a process called cell division. In mitosis, a cell divides once to produce two clones of itself, each with the same number of chromosomes. In meiosis, each cell divides twice, resulting in four daughter cells (gametes) that contain only half the number of chromosomes compared to the parent and are not necessarily identical.

  67. Differentiate between meiosis I and meiosis II.
    Topic: E. Cell Cycle, Mitosis and Meiosis

    Meiosis I includes crossing over or recombination of genetic material between chromosome pairs, while meiosis II does not. Meiosis I has a long and complicated prophase I, split into five sub-phases. In meiosis II, the chromosomes are further separated into sister chromatids.

  68. What is the role of mitosis and meiosis in the animal body?
    Topic: E. Cell Cycle, Mitosis and Meiosis

    There are two ways cell division can happen in humans and most other animals: mitosis and meiosis. When a cell divides by way of mitosis, it produces two clones of itself, each with the same number of chromosomes (growth and repair of the body). When a cell divides by way of meiosis, it produces four cells, called gametes (reproduction).

  69. Name the stages of prophase I of meiosis.
    Topic: E. Cell Cycle, Mitosis and Meiosis

    Prophase I occupies more than 90% of the time required for meiosis. Its sub-stages are: (1) Leptotene, (2) Zygotene, (3) Pachytene, (4) Diplotene, (5) Diakinesis.

  70. Describe prophase II of meiosis.
    Topic: E. Cell Cycle, Mitosis and Meiosis

    Meiosis II begins without any further replication of the chromosomes. In prophase II, no replication of chromosomes occurs as they are already replicated; centrioles are separated and take positions on the opposite poles of the cell; the nuclear membrane/envelope disappears and breaks down; and spindle fibres form and radiate toward the centre of the cell, forming the spindle apparatus.

  71. What do you know about telophase II of meiosis?
    Topic: E. Cell Cycle, Mitosis and Meiosis

    In telophase II, the chromosomes arrive at opposite poles, the nuclear membrane forms and the chromosomes begin de-condensing. Cytokinesis occurs. The two cells produced by meiosis I divide to form four haploid daughter cells, each with a haploid set of chromosomes and genetically distinct from the others and from the parent cell. When telophase II is over, the cells are entirely separated and their nuclear membranes are fully formed; meiosis is then complete.

  72. Differentiate between telophase I and telophase II.
    Topic: E. Cell Cycle, Mitosis and Meiosis

    The difference between telophase I in meiosis and telophase in mitosis is that, located close to each pole of the spindle, is a haploid set of chromosomes. Limited uncoiling occurs before the chromosomes move onto the second cell division stage in meiosis, telophase II.

  73. Define spermatogenesis and oogenesis. What is the difference between them?
    Topic: E. Cell Cycle, Mitosis and Meiosis

    Meiosis in males is called spermatogenesis and produces sperms; meiosis in females is called oogenesis and produces ova. Spermatogenesis: four sperm cells are produced from a primary spermatocyte. Oogenesis: only one ovum (egg) is produced from each primary oocyte, and the polar bodies die. Meiosis, the process by which gametes are formed, can also be called gametogenesis.

  74. Which type of cell division is present in somatic cells?
    Topic: E. Cell Cycle, Mitosis and Meiosis

    Mitosis. Somatic (body) cells divide by mitosis, producing two diploid daughter cells identical to the parent cell.

  75. Write the Frederick Griffith experiment.
    Topic: E. Cell Cycle, Mitosis and Meiosis

    Griffith's experiments: the first clue that DNA was the carrier of hereditary information came from the work of Fred Griffith in 1928. He observed the phenomenon of transformation in the bacterium Streptococcus pneumoniae. Griffith had isolated different strains of S. pneumoniae: in the virulent strain, each bacterium is surrounded by a polysaccharide coat, which makes the bacterial colony appear smooth on an agar plate (S forms). These virulent forms occasionally get mutated to non-virulent forms, which lack a polysaccharide coat and produce rough-appearing colonies (R forms). Griffith carried out a series of transformation experiments using S-form and R-form strains of S. pneumoniae and mice as the experimental animal.

  76. Write the experiment of T. H. Morgan.
    Topic: F. Linkage, Mapping and Markers

    Eye colour trait is linked with the sex of Drosophila; Morgan with an experiment proved that the eye colour trait is linked with the sex of the drosophila. Experiment: male flies were with white eyes (mutant); female flies were with red eyes (wild type). Progeny F1 was with red eyes - all the flies produced during the F1 generation were with red eyes. Progeny F2 showed a 3:1 ratio (three red eye : one white eye); the white-eyed trait appeared only in males - all females and half the males had red eyes.   94. What is a point mutation? Write its cause/effect.
    Topic: G. Mutations and Genetic Disorders

    A point mutation or substitution is a genetic mutation where a single nucleotide base is changed, inserted or deleted from a sequence of DNA or RNA; point mutations affect single sites on DNA - substitution of one base for another. Point mutations have a variety of effects on the downstream protein product. Cause/effect: the protein may lose its function, which can result in a disease in the organism. For example, sickle cell disease is caused by a single point mutation (a missense mutation) in the beta-haemoglobin gene that converts a GAG codon into GUG, which encodes the amino acid valine rather than glutamic acid.

    A missense mutation. It is a type of point mutation in which a single nucleotide change results in a codon that codes for a different amino acid, so the amino acid sequence of the protein product is changed (e.g. the GAG to GUG change in the beta-haemoglobin gene in sickle cell disease, where glutamic acid is replaced by valine).

  96. What is a frameshift mutation?
    Topic: G. Mutations and Genetic Disorders

    A frameshift mutation is caused by the insertion or deletion of a number of nucleotides in a DNA sequence that is not divisible by three. Because codons are read in triplets, the reading frame of the gene shifts from the point of mutation, so all the amino acids after that point are changed and the resulting protein is usually non-functional.

  97. Differentiate between forward, reverse and neutral mutations.
    Topic: G. Mutations and Genetic Disorders

    Forward mutation: a mutation that changes the wild-type (normal) phenotype into a mutant phenotype. Reverse mutation (reversion): a second mutation that restores the wild-type phenotype, either by exactly reversing the original change or by a suppressor change elsewhere. Neutral mutation: a mutation that has no detectable effect on the phenotype or fitness of the organism - it neither harms nor benefits it.

    Topic: G. Mutations and Genetic Disorders

The altered protein may lose its normal function, which can result in a disease in the organism - for example, the single point mutation in the beta-haemoglobin gene produces abnormal haemoglobin in sickle cell disease.

  100. Write the rules for mutation nomenclature.

To avoid confusion, the description of a variant should be preceded by a letter indicating the type of reference sequence used: "c." for a coding DNA sequence (e.g. c.76A>T), "g." for a genomic sequence (e.g. g.476A>T), "m." for a mitochondrial sequence (e.g. m.8993T>C), "r." for an RNA sequence (e.g. r.76a>u), and "p." for a protein sequence (e.g. p.Lys76Asn). For describing genes/proteins, only official HGNC (HUGO Gene Nomenclature Committee) gene symbols should be used.
