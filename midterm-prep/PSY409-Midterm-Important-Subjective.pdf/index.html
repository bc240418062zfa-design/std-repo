  1. Define positive psychology and describe its three dimensions (levels) of focus.
    Topic: Core Subjective Topics

    Positive psychology is defined as the side of the science of human mind and behaviour that studies and promotes the best of humanity. It is the scientific and practical pursuit of optimal human functioning, shifting focus from only repairing the worst things in life to also building positive qualities. It operates at three levels. At the subjective level it studies positive subjective states such as happiness, joy, life satisfaction, love, contentment, optimism and hope. At the individual level it studies enduring positive traits such as courage, persistence, honesty and wisdom, historically called character strengths or virtues. At the society or group level it focuses on positive institutions, civic virtues, healthy families, healthy work environments and positive communities.

  2. Discuss psychology's preoccupation with pathology and its three forgotten missions before World War II.
    Topic: Core Subjective Topics

    Before World War II, psychology had three major missions: to cure mental illness, to find and nurture genius and talent, and to make normal life more fulfilling. After the war, the last two missions were forgotten and psychology concentrated on treating mental illness and human weakness. A search of Psychological Abstracts since 1887 illustrates this imbalance: there were 8,072 articles on anger, 57,800 on anxiety and 70,856 on depression, compared with only 851 on joy, 2,958 on happiness and 5,701 on life satisfaction, so negative emotions outnumbered positive ones by a 14-to-1 ratio. Seligman therefore argued that psychology is also the study of strength and virtue, and treatment means nurturing what is best within ourselves, not only fixing what is broken.

  3. Explain the historical context that promoted the rise of positive psychology, including the roles of Maslow and Seligman.
    Topic: Core Subjective Topics

    Several historical changes promoted positive psychology. In 1900 life expectancy in the USA was only 45 years, and most people died in infancy, in childbirth, at work or from diseases such as diabetes, tuberculosis and flu. Medical science then transformed survival, so questions about quality of life became more important than mere survival. In 1947 the National Institute for Mental Health was established in the USA, focusing attention on problems and the negative. Mass migration from rural to urban settings also brought crowding, stress and stress-related disorders. Abraham Maslow actually coined the term positive psychology as a chapter title in his 1954 book Motivation and Personality. However, Martin Seligman of the University of Pennsylvania is singled out for igniting the recent explosion of interest and providing the label.

  4. Describe the early Western perspectives on happiness given by Socrates, Plato and Aristotle.
    Topic: Core Subjective Topics

    Greek culture introduced the idea that the good life and the proper path to happiness could be discovered through logic and rational analysis. Socrates believed true happiness could be achieved only through self-knowledge, which would reveal wisdom and the true nature of the person's soul; to know what is truly good, a person must know the essence or core of virtue, 'the good'. Plato's influence appears in any search for happiness that looks beyond sensory experiences toward deeper meaning, such as searching for one's true self or undertaking a spiritual quest. Aristotle sought the 'golden mean' between extremes, a point of balance, harmony and equilibrium leading to eudaimonia. Eudaimonic well-being means fulfilling one's potential and true nature, and Aristotle described twelve basic virtues, including courage, justice, temperance and truthfulness.

  5. Explain the Eastern perspectives of Buddhism and Hinduism, and contrast well-being in individualistic and collectivistic cultures.
    Topic: Core Subjective Topics

    Unlike Westerners, who search for rewards in the physical plane, Easterners seek to transcend the human plane and rise to the spiritual one. Buddhism teaches Four Noble Truths: life is suffering; suffering is caused by ignorance and the resulting craving, attachment and grasping; suffering can be ended by overcoming ignorance; and relief comes through the Noble Eightfold Path of right views, intention, speech, action, livelihood, effort, mindedness and contemplation. Peace is reached in nirvana, a state in which the self is freed from desire. Hinduism describes two paths after death: reincarnation, or no reincarnation when the highest knowledge was achieved, the latter being more glorified. Regarding cultures, individualistic cultures hold the single person above the group and emphasize competition and personal achievement, whereas collectivistic cultures value the group above the individual, emphasizing cooperation, sharing, duty and the welfare of the extended family.

  6. Describe the VIA Classification of Strengths and the Values in Action Inventory of Strengths (VIA-IS).
    Topic: Core Subjective Topics

    The Values in Action (VIA) Classification, developed by Peterson and Seligman (2004), was created because psychology had a shared language for its negative side but no equivalent terminology for human strengths. It was originally commissioned by the Mayerson Foundation to define strength and highest potential and to judge whether positive youth development programs had succeeded. Applying ten criteria for a strength, such as being morally valued in its own right, the authors identified 24 strengths organized under six overarching virtues: wisdom and knowledge, courage, humanity, justice, temperance and transcendence. Its measure, the VIA-IS, contains 240 items, ten for each strength, answered on a 5-point Likert scale in about 30 minutes. Feedback reports the respondent's top five strengths, called signature strengths.

  7. Compare the Gallup Themes of Talent and the Search Institute's 40 Developmental Assets as classification systems of human strengths.
    Topic: Core Subjective Topics

    The Gallup system, by Buckingham and Clifton (2001), is measured by the Clifton StrengthsFinder. Donald Clifton defined talent as naturally recurring patterns of thought, feeling or behaviour that can be productively applied. The instrument uses 180 item pairs and identifies 34 themes, reporting a person's Five Signature Themes; a youth version, the Clifton Youth Strengths Explorer for ages 10 to 14, taps 10 themes and reports the top three. The Search Institute's 40 Developmental Assets, by Benson and colleagues (1998), were developed from the question of what protects children from today's problems. They are commonsense positive experiences and qualities divided into 20 external assets, gained through interactions with people and institutions, and 20 internal assets, the personal characteristics and behaviours stimulating positive development. They are measured by the 156-item Profiles of Student Life survey.

  8. Explain positive outcomes in terms of emotional, psychological and social well-being, and distinguish flourishing from languishing.
    Topic: Core Subjective Topics

    Positive outcomes include happiness, spontaneous reflections of pleasant and unpleasant feelings in immediate experience, and life satisfaction, contentment stemming from small gaps between wants and needs. Emotional well-being has a threefold structure: life satisfaction, positive affect and the absence of negative affect. Ryff (1989) proposed psychological well-being with six components: self-acceptance, personal growth, purpose in life, environmental mastery, autonomy and positive relations with others. Keyes (1998) added social well-being. In Keyes's integrated model, complete mental health combines high emotional, psychological and social well-being. Individuals with these high levels are described as flourishing. Individuals who have no mental illness but low levels of well-being are described as languishing. Diagnostic criteria for flourishing also require no episodes of major depression in the past year and high scores across the three forms of well-being.

  9. Define affect, emotion, happiness and subjective well-being, and describe the PANAS-X.
    Topic: Core Subjective Topics

    Affect is a person's immediate, physiological response to a stimulus, typically based on an underlying sense of arousal; it involves appraising an event as painful or pleasurable, its valence, and automatic arousal. Emotion is awareness of pleasurable or painful experiences with associated autonomic arousal; it has a specific, sharpened quality, always has an object, and is associated with progress in goal pursuit. Happiness is a positive emotional state subjectively defined by each person. Diener defines subjective well-being as a combination of positive affect, in the absence of negative affect, and general life satisfaction. To measure affect, Watson and Clark (1994) developed and validated the Expanded Form of the Positive and Negative Affect Schedule, PANAS-X. It quantifies valence and content: negative affect is best described as general distress, while positive affect includes joviality, self-assurance and attentiveness. Bradburn (1969) had shown pleasant and unpleasant affects are independent, not polar opposites.

  10. Explain Fredrickson's broaden-and-build theory of positive emotions, including the undoing hypothesis.
    Topic: Core Subjective Topics

    Fredrickson (2000) proposed the broaden-and-build theory to explain the cognitive and social effects of positive emotions. She replaced the idea of specific action tendencies, which restrict behavioural options, with momentary thought-action repertoires, which suggest a broad range of options, like taking off blinders. Joy broadens what a person feels like doing, and through broadening, positive emotions build enduring personal resources; Fredrickson and Joiner (2002) found initial positive emotions predicted increases in broad-minded, creative problem solving five weeks later. Joy also induces playfulness, building social and intellectual resources, creativity and brain development, creating an upward spiral. The undoing hypothesis of Fredrickson and Levenson (1998) proposes that positive emotions such as joy and contentment act as antidotes, undoing the lingering cardiovascular effects of negative emotions, which narrow repertoires, for example anger urging attack and fear urging escape.

  11. Define subjective well-being according to Diener and describe its components and how its satisfaction component is measured.
    Topic: Core Subjective Topics

    Diener defines subjective well-being as a combination of positive affect in the absence of negative affect and general life satisfaction. Building on hedonic psychology, it is the subjective evaluation of one's current status in the world, involving experience of pleasure and appreciation of life's rewards. Diener uses subjective well-being as a synonym for happiness. Its satisfaction component is often measured with the Satisfaction with Life Scale by Diener et al. (1985), containing five statements such as life being close to ideal and being satisfied with life. Respondents rate agreement from 1, strongly disagree, to 7, strongly agree, and scores are summed.

  12. Explain the three age-old theories of happiness: need/goal satisfaction, process/activity, and genetic/personality predisposition.
    Topic: Core Subjective Topics

    Need/goal satisfaction theories, associated with Freud and Maslow, propose happiness follows reduction of tension or satisfaction of needs, making happiness a target of psychological pursuits. Process/activity theories argue engaging in particular life activities generates happiness. Csikszentmihalyi proposed that people experiencing flow, engagement in interesting activities matching or challenging their skills, tend to be very happy. Genetic/personality predisposition theories, associated with Diener and Larsen and Watson, view happiness as a stable trait emerging from biological and genetic factors. Research suggests up to 40 percent of positive emotionality and 55 percent of negative emotionality are genetically based, leaving about half unexplained by biology.

  13. Discuss the relationship between wealth and happiness in poor and affluent countries, giving evidence from the handouts.
    Topic: Core Subjective Topics

    In poor countries, where low income threatens basic human needs, being relatively well off does predict greater well-being. In affluent countries, where most can afford life's necessities, affluence matters surprisingly little and more money provides diminishing returns once people are comfortable. Even the 100 wealthiest Americans surveyed by Diener, Horwitz and Emmons were only slightly happier than the average American. Americans became twice as rich between 1957 and 1998, yet those reporting themselves very happy declined slightly from 35 percent to 33 percent. Wealth is therefore like health: its absence can breed misery, but having it guarantees no happiness.

  14. How do work satisfaction, faith and close relationships predict happiness?
    Topic: Core Subjective Topics

    Explain with findings cited in the handouts. Work satisfaction predicts happiness when work and leisure engage one's skills and produce flow, between being overwhelmed and being bored. In a Pakistani survey, work satisfaction was the best predictor of life satisfaction and personal happiness, driven more by intrinsic rewards than money. Religiously active people report higher happiness; 41 percent of those extremely close to God were very happy, compared with 23 percent not close or unbelieving, partly through social support and meaning. Close relationships also matter: 38 percent of people with five or more close friends were very happy, versus 26 percent with fewer, and supported people cope better with stress and illness.

  15. What is emotion-focused coping through emotional approach?
    Topic: Core Subjective Topics

    Describe emotional processing and emotional expression with examples. Emotion-focused coping through emotional approach, developed by Stanton and colleagues, means regulating emotions surrounding a stressful encounter by actively moving toward, rather than away from, that encounter. Its adaptive potential depends on situational context, interpersonal milieu and individual attributes. It involves two processes. Emotional processing is attempting to understand emotions, for example acknowledging emotions, realising feelings are valid and important, and taking time to figure out what one is really feeling. Emotional expression is free and intentional display of feeling, for example feeling free to express emotions, taking time to express them, and letting feelings come out freely. Together they foster understanding and direct attention to central concerns.

  16. Describe the Salovey and Mayer model of emotional intelligence and how emotional intelligence is measured.
    Topic: Core Subjective Topics

    Salovey and Mayer (1990) defined emotional intelligence as the ability to monitor one's own and others' feelings and emotions, discriminate among them, and use this information to guide thinking and action. Their ability model has four branches: perceiving emotions, using emotions to facilitate thought, understanding emotions, and managing emotions. Perceiving involves identifying emotions in faces and designs; using involves harnessing emotions to improve problem solving and creativity; understanding involves grasping complex emotions and their consequences; managing involves regulating mood without becoming repressed or overwhelmed. Mayer and colleagues developed the MSCEIT to measure these branches, while Bar-On's EQ-I mainly measures personality and mood variables such as empathy and happiness.

  17. Explain Carstensen's socioemotional selectivity theory and the age differences it predicts in attention, memory and goals.
    Topic: Core Subjective Topics

    Stanford psychologist Laura Carstensen's socioemotional selectivity theory proposes that as people realise they have a short amount of time left, later years become valuable for emotional meaning. Older people focus less on negative emotions, engage more deeply with emotional content, and savour the good in life and relationships. In laboratory tests, younger participants attended more quickly to negative images, whereas older participants oriented faster to positive images. Young people remembered positive and negative material equally, but older people showed a positivity bias, recalling positive material more quickly. Older participants also savoured positive events longer and pursued here-and-now emotion-focused goals, unlike younger people's future-oriented goals of acquiring information and expanding horizons.

  18. What is emotional storytelling?
    Topic: Core Subjective Topics

    Describe Pennebaker's paradigm and its effects and theoretical explanation. Emotional storytelling is sharing an overwhelming emotional experience with others. Rime (1995) found it is highly likely, with about 95 percent probability, that people share such an experience with a friend or family member the same day, typically within hours. Pennebaker's paradigm is systematic written disclosure across brief sessions. In his 1989 study, students wrote continuously about their most traumatic experiences for four days, without regard to spelling or grammar. The storytelling group was initially more distressed, but from two weeks onward experienced health benefits, including fewer physician visits over the next year. Benefits are explained by disinhibition, cognitive processing and social dynamics, helping people organise thoughts and find meaning.

  19. Define self-efficacy according to Bandura, distinguish it from confidence, and list its developmental antecedents.
    Topic: Core Subjective Topics

    Bandura (1997) defined self-efficacy as people's beliefs in their capabilities to produce desired effects by their own actions. Maddux described it as what I believe I can do with my skills under certain conditions. It differs from confidence, which is a nondescript term for strength of belief without specifying what the certainty is about; a person can be supremely confident they will fail. Perceived self-efficacy specifies a capability level and strength of belief in attaining it. Its developmental antecedents include previous success in similar situations, modelling on successful others, imagining oneself behaving effectively, verbal persuasion by powerful and trustworthy others, and arousal and emotion.

  20. Explain hedonic adaptation and the stability of subjective well-being, and describe the traits of happy people.
    Topic: Core Subjective Topics

    Subjective well-being is relatively stable despite changing life circumstances. People work on a hedonic treadmill: good and bad events temporarily influence mood, but emotional impact dissipates in less time than most suppose, and people return to a neutral set point. This explains why lottery winners and people with backbone injury return toward their baseline, and why Kammann concluded objective life circumstances have a negligible role in a theory of happiness. Costa and McCrae found happiness changed little over six years. Happy people are less self-focused, less hostile and abusive, less vulnerable to disease, and more loving, forgiving, trusting, energetic, decisive, creative, sociable and helpful.

  21. Explain Bandura's situational perspective on self-efficacy and contrast it with the trait perspective, giving one measure for each approach.
    Topic: Core Subjective Topics

    Bandura (1977, 1982, 1997) held staunchly to the situational perspective: self-efficacy should reflect beliefs about using abilities and skills to reach given goals in specific circumstances or domains, for example Schwarzer and Renner's coping self-efficacy scale or Betz's career decision measure. Situation-specific measures robustly predict lower anxiety, higher pain tolerance and better academic performance. The trait perspective, which Bandura argued against, views self-efficacy as enduring over time and circumstances. Its measures include Sherer et al.'s (1982) 23-item Self-Efficacy Scale on a 14-point Likert scale and Chen et al.'s (2001) 8-item New General Self-Efficacy Scale, whose scores relate positively to Sherer's scale.

  22. Describe how self-efficacy influences psychological adjustment and physical health, as discussed in Lesson 15.
    Topic: Core Subjective Topics

    Self-efficacy is implicated in coping with many psychological problems (Maddux, 1995). Lower self-efficacy is linked with depression (Bandura, 1977) and with avoidance and anxiety (Williams, 1995), while higher self-efficacy helps in overcoming eating disorders and abuse and can play a protective, strengths-based role. For physical health, Maddux (2002) proposed two routes. First, elevated self-efficacy increases health-related behaviours, decreases unhealthy ones and helps maintain these changes. Second, it impacts biological processes related to better health, including immune functioning, susceptibility to infections, catecholamines implicated in stress management, and endorphins for muting pain (Bandura, 1997).

  23. Explain Seligman's learned optimism in terms of explanatory style for good and bad events.
    Topic: Core Subjective Topics

    In Seligman's theory, learned optimism is defined by the causal attributions a person makes, following Abramson, Seligman and Teasdale's (1978) reformulation of helplessness. For negative or failure-like events, the optimist makes external, variable and specific attributions - the bad thing was caused by outside, temporary and limited factors. The pessimist does the opposite, making internal, stable and global attributions. For positive events, optimistic parents and teachers model the reverse pattern - internal, stable and global explanations - so children continue to feel good about themselves. Seligman's theory thus emphasises distancing from bad past events rather than simply expecting positive future outcomes.

  24. Compare Seligman's learned optimism with Scheier and Carver's dispositional optimism, including how each is measured.
    Topic: Core Subjective Topics

    Seligman's learned optimism concerns explanatory style: how people explain bad and good events on internal/external, stable/transient and global/specific dimensions. It is measured by the Attributional Style Questionnaire (ASQ; Peterson et al., 1982) for adults and the Children's Attributional Style Questionnaire, or retrospectively by the CAVE content analysis of verbal explanations. Scheier and Carver (1985), in contrast, define optimism as the stable tendency to believe good rather than bad things will happen, emphasising outcome expectancies themselves as the best predictors of behaviour. It is measured by the Life Orientation Test (LOT) and its shorter revision, the LOT-R (Scheier, Carver and Bridges, 1994), developed to remove overlap with neuroticism.

  25. Define hope in Snyder's hope theory and explain its two main components with examples.
    Topic: Core Subjective Topics

    Snyder defines hope as goal-directed thinking in which the person utilises pathways thinking and agency thinking. Only goals with considerable value count; they may be short- or long-term, approach-oriented or preventive, and easy or extremely difficult. Pathways thinking is the perceived capacity to find routes to desired goals, shown in producing alternate routes when the original is blocked and in self-talk such as 'I'll find a way to solve this' - for example, the Hope Scale item 'I can think of many ways to get out of a jam.' Agency thinking is the requisite motivation to use those routes, shown in energetic self-talk such as 'I will keep going' and the item 'I energetically pursue my goals.'

  26. Discuss the childhood antecedents of hope according to Snyder.
    Topic: Core Subjective Topics

    Snyder (1994) proposes hope has no hereditary contributions but is entirely a learned cognitive set about goal-directed thinking, taught inherently through parenting, with its components in place by age two. Pathways thinking reflects basic cause-and-effect learning acquired from caregivers and is acquired before agency thinking. Agency thinking, posited to begin around age one, reflects the baby's increasing insight that she is a causal force in cause-and-effect sequences in her environment. Snyder proposed that strong attachment to caregivers is crucial for imparting hope, and research is consistent with this. Conversely, traumatic childhood events, such as the loss of parents (Westberg, 2001), have been linked to lessening of hope.

  27. How is hope measured?
    Topic: Core Subjective Topics

    Describe the adult, children's and state hope scales. Snyder and colleagues developed several self-report scales. The adult trait Hope Scale (Snyder, Harris et al., 1991) for ages 16 and older has 12 items: four reflect pathways, four reflect agency and four are distracters, answered on an 8-point scale from definitely false to definitely true, with alphas typically in the .80 range. The Children's Hope Scale (Snyder, Hoza et al., 1997) for ages 8 to 15 has six items - three agency and three pathways - on a 6-point scale. The State Hope Scale (Snyder et al., 1996) has six items, three pathways and three agency, tapping here-and-now goal-directed thinking, with alphas often in the .90 range. Factor analyses support the two-factor structure.

  28. Define psychological resilience and explain what researchers mean by 'good adaptation'. Psychological resilience is an individual's capacity to withstand stressors and not manifest psychological dysfunction such as mental illness or persistent negative mood, and to avoid psychopathology despite difficult circumstances. Masten and Reed (2002) define it as a class of phenomena characterised by patterns of positive adaptation in the context of significant adversity or risk. Researchers debate 'good adaptation' - bounced back to what?
    Topic: Core Subjective Topics

    Observers look for a return to normal functioning, such as attainment of developmental milestones, and/or excellence above age expectations. Researchers agree external adaptation - meeting social, educational and occupational expectations - is necessary, but are split on whether internal adaptation, positive psychological well-being, must also be shown.

  29. Describe Werner's core characteristics of resilient children, citing her Kauai study.
    Topic: Core Subjective Topics

    Werner collaborated with Ruth Smith (1982, 1992) on a cohort of about 700 children born on Kauai, Hawaii, one-third considered high risk at birth because of poverty, parental alcoholism and domestic violence. Werner (1995) identified core characteristics across studies. First, resilient children found a nurturing surrogate parent, emotionally detaching from a disturbed parent and often bonding with a teacher as role model, aided by an active, affectionate, good-natured temperament. Second, they had good social and communication skills and at least one close friend. Third, they had creative outlets or hobbies giving pride and mastery. Fourth, they believed life would work out, with optimism, internal locus of control and positive self-concept, combining autonomy with asking for help. Finally, their families held religious beliefs providing meaning.

  30. What is successful aging?
    Topic: Core Subjective Topics

    Discuss the MacArthur study components and Vaillant's lifestyle predictors. Successful aging, a term popularised by Havighurst (1961) as 'adding life to years', was studied in the MacArthur Foundation Study (1988-1996) by Rowe and colleagues with 1,189 high-functioning volunteers aged 70-79 followed for about seven years. It revealed three components: avoiding disease, engaging with life, and maintaining physical and cognitive functioning, supported by mutual socioemotional and instrumental social support. Vaillant (2002), studying Harvard's Study of Adult Development participants, identified lifestyle predictors of healthy aging: not smoking or stopping while young, coping adaptively with mature defences, not abusing alcohol, maintaining a healthy weight, a stable marriage, some exercise and being educated. The most robust predictor of being happy-well rather than sad-sick was using mature coping styles such as altruism and humour.
