  1. What is data and what are the different sources of data?
    Topic: Data and Basic Concepts

    The raw material of Statistics is data. We may define data as figures. Figures result from the process of counting or from taking a measurement. For example: when a hospital administrator counts the number of patients (counting); when a nurse weighs a patient (measurement). Sources of data: (1) Routinely kept records, (2) External sources, (3) Surveys, (4) Experiments.

  2. What is primary data and secondary data? How is each further classified?
    Topic: Data and Basic Concepts

    Primary data is the type of data that is collected by researchers directly from the main sources through interviews, surveys, experiments, etc. Primary data are usually collected from the source — where the data originally originates from — and are regarded as the best kind of data in research. Primary data can further be divided into: (a) Interview method, (b) Survey method, (c) Observation method, (d) Experimental method. Secondary data is the data that has already been collected through primary sources and made readily available for researchers to use for their own research. It is a type of data that has already been collected in the past. A researcher may have collected the data for a particular project, then made it available to be used by another researcher; the data may also have been collected for general use with no specific research purpose, like in the case of the national census. Secondary data can further be classified as: (a) Internal source, (b) External source.

  3. Write the names of the fields where statistics is used.
    Topic: Data and Basic Concepts

    Not only in the field of natural and physical science, but statistics has made much impact on social sciences and is of immense use in these also. These include subjects like History, Political Science, Education, Psychology, Sociology and Logic, etc. Even in mathematics, many theories have been drawn from statistics.

  4. What is random error?
    Topic: Data and Basic Concepts

    A random error is an error that occurs by chance and varies unpredictably from one observation to another, sometimes making the observed value higher and sometimes lower than the true value. It arises from chance fluctuations in measurement and affects the precision of the measurements.

  5. Name the probability sampling designs.
    Topic: Population, Sample and Sampling

    Simple random sampling, stratified sampling, systematic sampling, cluster (area) sampling and multistage sampling.

  6. Write a comprehensive note on sampling and statistical inference. What are the different types of sampling? What are the characteristics of each type of sampling?
    Topic: Population, Sample and Sampling

    Sampling: A sample is a subset of individuals from a larger population. Sampling means selecting the group that you will actually collect data from in your research. For example, if you are researching the opinion of students in your university, you could survey a sample of 100 students. Samples are used to make inferences about populations. Samples are easier to collect data from because they are practical, cost-effective, convenient and manageable. Statistical inference: Statistical inference is the process of using data analysis to deduce properties of an underlying distribution of probability. Inferential statistics analysis infers properties of a population, for example by testing hypotheses and deriving estimates. It is assumed that the observed data set is sampled from a larger population. Types of sampling: There are two major types — probability sampling and non-probability sampling. Probability sampling means that every member of the target population has a known chance of being included in the sample. It includes simple random sampling, systematic sampling, stratified sampling and cluster sampling. Random sampling is analogous to putting everyone's name into a hat and drawing out several names; each element in the population has an equal chance of occurring. In systematic sampling the list of elements is 'counted off', i.e. every k-th element is taken. Cluster sampling is accomplished by dividing the population into groups — usually geographically — called clusters or blocks; the clusters are randomly selected and each element in the selected clusters is used. Stratified sampling also divides the population into groups called strata, but by some characteristic, not geographically (for instance, males and females), and a sample is taken from each of these strata. Non-probability sampling is a sampling technique where the odds of any member being selected for a sample cannot be calculated. An example is convenience sampling, in which readily available data is used — the first people the surveyor runs into. Main characteristics of sampling: In sampling, we assume that samples are drawn from the population and sample means and population means are equal. A population can be defined as a whole that includes all items and characteristics of the research taken into study. We make inference about the population with the help of a sample.

  7. What are parameters and frame?
    Topic: Population, Sample and Sampling

    In order to draw a random sample from a finite population, the first thing that we need is the complete list of all the elements in our population. This list is technically called the FRAME. In statistics, a parameter is any measured quantity of a statistical population that summarises or describes an aspect of the population, such as a mean or a standard deviation.

  8. What is the difference between quota sampling and cluster sampling?
    Topic: Population, Sample and Sampling

    Quota sampling: In this type of sampling, the selection of the sampling unit from the population is no longer dictated by chance. A sampling frame is not used at all, and the choice of the actual sample units to be interviewed is left to the discretion of the interviewer. However, the interviewer is restricted by quota controls. Cluster sampling: Cluster sampling is a probability sampling method in which you divide a population into clusters, such as districts or schools, and then randomly select some of these clusters as your sample. The clusters should ideally each be mini-representations of the population as a whole.

  9. What is the difference between qualitative and quantitative variables?
    Topic: Variables and Measurement Scales

    Quantitative variables: These can be measured in the usual sense. For example: the heights of adult males, the weights of preschool children, the ages of patients seen in a dental clinic. A variable is called a quantitative variable when a characteristic can be expressed numerically, such as age, weight, income or number of children. Qualitative variables: Many characteristics are not capable of being measured. Some of them can be ordered or ranked. For example: classification of people into socioeconomic groups, social classes based on income, education, etc. If the characteristic is non-numerical, such as education, sex, eye-colour, quality, intelligence, poverty, satisfaction, etc., the variable is referred to as a qualitative variable. A qualitative characteristic is also called an attribute.

  10. Define discrete and continuous variables.
    Topic: Variables and Measurement Scales

    A discrete variable is a variable whose value is obtained by counting. Examples: number of students present, number of red marbles in a jar, number of heads when flipping three coins. Continuous variables are numeric variables that have an infinite number of values between any two values. A continuous variable can be numeric or date/time. For example, the length of a part or the date and time a payment is received.

  11. What is data scale?
    Topic: Variables and Measurement Scales

    Scales of measurement in research and statistics are the different ways in which variables are defined and grouped into different categories. Sometimes called the level of measurement, it describes the nature of the values assigned to the variables in a data set. There are four types of data scale: (i) Nominal, (ii) Ordinal, (iii) Interval, (iv) Ratio.

  12. What is frequency distribution and frequency histogram?
    Topic: Frequency Distribution and Graphical Presentation

    Frequency Distribution: A table that organizes data values into classes or intervals along with the number of values that fall in each class (frequency, f). (1) Ungrouped Frequency Distribution — for data sets with few different values; each value is in its own class. (2) Grouped Frequency Distribution — for data sets with many different values, which are grouped together in the classes. Frequency Histogram: A bar graph that represents the frequency distribution. The horizontal scale is quantitative and measures the data values. The vertical scale measures the frequencies of the classes. Consecutive bars must touch.

  13. What are the different types of graphs?
    Topic: Frequency Distribution and Graphical Presentation

    Types of graphs and charts: Bar Chart/Graph, Pie Chart, Line Graph or Chart, Histogram Chart, Area Chart, Dot Graph or Plot, Scatter Plot, Bubble Chart.

  14. What is a pie chart?
    Topic: Frequency Distribution and Graphical Presentation

    A pie chart, sometimes called a circle chart, is a way of summarizing a set of nominal data or displaying the different values of a given variable (e.g. percentage distribution). This type of chart is a circle divided into a series of segments. Each segment represents a particular category.

  15. How to make a box and whisker plot?
    Topic: Frequency Distribution and Graphical Presentation

    In a box plot, we draw a box from the first quartile to the third quartile. A vertical line goes through the box at the median. The whiskers go from each quartile to the minimum or maximum. Steps (as given in the course notes in the same file): (1) Arrange the data in ascending order. (2) Find the median (50% of data), lower quartile (25%) and upper quartile (75%) of the data. (3) Draw a number line that includes the smallest and largest values. (4) Draw three vertical lines at the lower quartile, median and upper quartile above the number line. (5) Join the lines for the lower quartile and upper quartile to form a box. (6) Draw a line from the box at the left side for the smallest value and a line from the box to the right for the largest value. The difference between the upper and lower quartile is called the interquartile range (IQR).

  16. What are the shapes of distribution?
    Topic: Shapes of Distribution, Outliers and Measures of Position

    Symmetric: Data is symmetric if the left half of its histogram is roughly a mirror image of its right half. Skewed: Data is skewed if it is not symmetric and if it extends more to one side than the other. Uniform: Data is uniform if it is equally distributed (on a histogram, all the bars are the same height or approximately the same height). Generally there are two basic types of distribution — a distribution may be symmetric or asymmetric — but in advanced statistics there are seven types of distribution: (i) Symmetric, (ii) Bell shaped, (iii) U shaped, (iv) Skewed, (v) Positively skewed, (vi) Negatively skewed, (vii) Uniform.

  17. What are outliers?
    Topic: Shapes of Distribution, Outliers and Measures of Position

    Unusual data values as compared to the rest of the set. They may be distinguished by gaps in a histogram. An outlier is an observation point that is distant from other observations. An outlier may be due to variability in the measurement or it may indicate experimental error; the latter are sometimes excluded from the data set. A value that 'lies outside' (is much smaller or larger than) most of the other values in a set of data — for example, in the scores 25, 29, 3, 32, 85, 33, 27, 28, both 3 and 85 are outliers.

  18. When was skewness discovered?
    Topic: Shapes of Distribution, Outliers and Measures of Position

    Between the end of the nineteenth century and the beginning of the twentieth century, Pearson, Karl studied large sets of data which sometimes deviated significantly from normality and exhibited considerable skewness.

  19. What is percentile?
    Topic: Shapes of Distribution, Outliers and Measures of Position

    In statistics, a k-th percentile is a score below which a given percentage k of scores in its frequency distribution falls, or a score at or below which a given percentage falls. For example, the 50th percentile is the score below which, or at or below which, 50% of the scores in the distribution may be found.

  20. What is dispersion?
    Topic: Dispersion

    In statistics, dispersion is the extent to which a distribution is stretched or squeezed. Common examples of measures of statistical dispersion are the variance, standard deviation, and interquartile range. For instance, when the variance of data in a set is large, the data is widely scattered.

  21. What is relative measure of dispersion?
    Topic: Dispersion

    Relative measures of dispersion are measures of the variance of a range of values regardless of its unit of measure. This means that the spread of two ranges of values with different measures can be compared directly with relative measures of dispersion.

  22. Explain the range rule of thumb.
    Topic: Dispersion

    The range rule of thumb is a handy method of estimating the range from the standard deviation. It tells us that the range is generally about four times the standard deviation. So if your standard deviation is 2, you might guess that your range is about eight.

  23. Write the general and specific rule for dependent and independent events.
    Topic: Probability and Distributions

    Independent events: Two events are independent if the occurrence of one does not change the probability of the other occurring. An example would be rolling a 2 on a dice and flipping a head on a coin. Rolling the 2 does not affect the probability of flipping the head. Dependent events: Two events are dependent when the outcome of the first event influences the outcome of the second event. The probability of two dependent events is the product of the probability of X and the probability of Y AFTER X occurs.

  24. What are the distribution types?
    Topic: Probability and Distributions

    There are two types of probability distribution which are used for different purposes and various types of the data generation process: Normal or Cumulative Probability Distribution, and Binomial or Discrete Probability Distribution.

  25. What is regression analysis?
    Topic: Regression and Correlation

    Regression analysis is a powerful statistical method that allows you to examine the relationship between two or more variables of interest. While there are many types of regression analysis, at their core they all examine the influence of one or more independent variables on a dependent variable.

  26. Define correlation.
    Topic: Regression and Correlation

    Correlation is a measure of the strength or the degree of relationship between two random variables. A numerical measure of the strength of the linear relationship between two random variables X and Y is known as Pearson's Product-Moment Coefficient of Correlation. Correlation can be linear, positive, perfect positive, negative, perfect negative, and there can be no correlation at all.

  27. What are regression and correlation?
    Topic: Regression and Correlation

    Regression is a statistical method used in finance, investing, and other disciplines that attempts to determine the strength and character of the relationship between one dependent variable (usually denoted by Y) and a series of other variables (known as independent variables). Correlation is a statistical measure that expresses the extent to which two variables are linearly related (meaning they change together at a constant rate). It is a common tool for describing simple relationships without making a statement about cause and effect.

  28. What is the regression equation?
    Topic: Regression and Correlation

    A regression equation is used in statistics to find out what relationship, if any, exists between sets of data. For example, if you measure a child's height every year, you might find that they grow about 3 inches a year. That trend (growing three inches a year) can be modeled with a regression equation. Yi = f(Xi,β ) + ei, where Yi = dependent variable, f = function, Xi = independent variable,β = unknown parameters, ei = error terms.

  29. Write the regression equation and explain its terms.
    Topic: Regression and Correlation

    The equation has the form Y = a + bX, where Y is the dependent variable (that's the variable that goes on the Y axis), X is the independent variable (i.e. it is plotted on the X axis), b is the slope of the line and a is the y-intercept.

  30. What are the categories of diagnostic test?
    Topic: Statistical Tests and SPSS

    There are many different types of tests in statistics, like t-test, Z-test, chi-square test, ANOVA test, binomial test, one sample median test, etc. Parametric tests are used if the data is normally distributed.

  31. What is the Data Editor window (in SPSS)?
    Topic: Statistical Tests and SPSS

    The data editor window is the default window when you run SPSS. The data worksheet works just like a spreadsheet, where a column represents a variable and a row represents a case or an observation.

  32. Define biostatistics. Write its applications.
    Topic: Basic Concepts

    Biostatistics is the use of statistical methods to solve biological problems; it is also called biometry. Statistics itself is a field of study concerned with the collection, organization, summarization and analysis of data, and with drawing inferences about a body of data when only part of the data is observed. Applications of biostatistics: Public Health, Pharmacology, Epidemiology, Medicine, Genetics, Genomics, Proteomics, Bioinformatics, etc.

  33. Define population and sample. Differentiate between a parameter and a statistic.
    Topic: Basic Concepts

    Population: In statistics, population is defined as 'an aggregate of entities for which we have an interest at a particular time.' It can consist of animals, machines, places or cells, etc. For example, if we are interested in knowing the weights of all the children enrolled in elementary schools of the Bahawalpur District, our population will be all the children enrolled in elementary schools of the Bahawalpur District. Sample: A sample is a representative part of a population. A sample survey is a study that obtains data from a subset of a population of our interest, in order to estimate population attributes. In a sample, the measurable quantity is called a statistic. Parameter vs statistic: A population parameter is any summary number, like an average or percentage, that describes the entire population; these are the true values, which are usually unknown, and are denoted by Greek letters, e.g. population mean μ (Mu), population proportionπ (Pi). A sample statistic is any summary number, like an average or percentage, that describes the sample; sample statistics are random variables and are denoted by Latin letters, e.g. sample standard deviation (s), sample proportion (p).

  34. Differentiate between finite and infinite population with examples.
    Topic: Basic Concepts

    Finite population: If a population of values consists of a fixed number of these values, the population is said to be finite. The finite population is also known as a countable population, in which the population can be counted; it is the population of all the individuals or objects that are finite. For statistical analysis, the finite population is more advantageous than the infinite population. Examples: employees of a company, potential consumers in a market. Infinite population: If a population consists of an endless succession of values, the population is an infinite one. The infinite population is also known as an uncountable population, in which the counting of units in the population is not possible. Example: the number of germs in a patient's body is uncountable.

  35. What is a census? Write the characteristics of a good sample.
    Topic: Basic Concepts

    A census is a survey conducted on the full set of observations belonging to a given population; it is a study that obtains data from every member of a population. In most studies, a census is not practical because of the cost and/or time required. Characteristics of a good sample: (1) Representative; (2) It captures most of the variation in the population; (3) Obtained by using proper sampling methodology; (4) Focus on objective; (5) Informative with minimum use of resources.

  36. Differentiate between descriptive and inferential statistics.
    Topic: Basic Concepts

    Descriptive statistics: These are the statistical methodologies which are used to organize and summarize data. This summarization can be in numeric as well as in graphical form, and these descriptive methodologies form the basis of any quantitative analysis of data. Descriptive analysis is also known as Exploratory Data Analysis (EDA). Descriptive statistics is performed using tabular, graphical and numerical methods. Inferential statistics: These are the methods which we use to talk about the population from the information that comes out of the sample — the statistical methodologies using which we reach a conclusion about a population on the basis of the information contained in the sample. Inferential statistics go beyond the immediate data and use probability theory to make inferences or predictions about a population based on a sample of data; this includes techniques like hypothesis testing, confidence intervals, and regression analysis.

  37. Name and explain the four scales of measurement.
    Topic: Variables and Measurement Scales

    Measurement is defined as the assignment of numbers to objects or events according to a set of rules. Each scale of measurement satisfies one or more of the following properties: Identity (each value on the scale has a unique meaning); Magnitude (values have an ordered relationship to one another — some values are larger and some are smaller); Equal intervals (scale units along the scale are equal to one another, e.g. the difference between 1 and 2 equals the difference between 19 and 20); and a minimum value of zero (the scale has a true zero point, below which no values exist). The four types of measurement scales are: (1) Nominal — values only name categories (identity only), e.g. blood group, gender; (2) Ordinal — values can also be ranked in order (identity + magnitude), e.g. social class; (3) Interval — values have equal intervals but no true zero (identity + magnitude + equal intervals); (4) Ratio — values have all four properties, including a true zero point, e.g. height, weight.

  38. What are quantiles? Define quartile, decile and percentile.
    Topic: Variables and Measurement Scales

    The mean and median are special cases of a family of parameters known as location parameters. Other location parameters which help us to determine the position of a particular observation in the data are called measures of position. These measures of position, when they help us divide sample data into n equal parts (or divide the probability distribution into contiguous intervals with equal probabilities), are called quantiles. Types of quantiles: (1) Quartiles — in an array, quartiles are 3 cut points which divide the values into 4 equal parts, denoted by Qk. (2) Deciles — the deciles are 9 values/cut-points that divide the values into 10 equal parts, denoted by Dk. (3) Percentiles — the percentiles are 99 values/cut-points that divide the values into 100 equal parts, denoted by Pk.

  39. Define mean, median and mode with examples.
    Topic: Central Tendency and Dispersion — Numerical Questions

    Mean: The mean, or average, is calculated by summing up all the values in a dataset and dividing the sum by the number of values. Example: for the dataset {12, 15, 18, 22, 25}, Mean = (12 + 15 + 18 + 22 + 25) / 5 = 92 / 5 = 18.4. Median: The median is the middle value when a dataset is ordered. If the dataset has an even number of values, the median is the average of the two middle values. Example: for the dataset {10, 15, 18, 22, 25}, the median is 18; for the dataset {10, 15, 18, 22}, the median is (15 + 18) / 2 = 16.5. Mode: The mode is the value that appears most frequently in a dataset. Example: for the dataset {5, 8, 12, 8, 15, 8, 22}, the mode is 8 because it appears more frequently than any other value.

  40. For the data set {2, 4, 6, 8, 10}, find the range, variance and standard deviation.
    Topic: Central Tendency and Dispersion — Numerical Questions

    Range: The range is the difference between the maximum and minimum values in a dataset. Range = 10 − 2 = 8. Mean: (2 + 4 + 6 + 8 + 10) / 5 = 6. Deviations from the mean: (2 − 6) = − 4, (4− 6) = − 2, (6− 6) = 0, (8− 6) = 2, (10− 6) = 4. Variance: Square the deviations and then sum up and divide by the number of values in the dataset: (16 + 4 + 0 + 4 + 16) / 5 = 40 / 5 = 8. Standard deviation: Take the square root of the variance:√ 8 = 2.83. Coefficient of standard deviation = (Standard deviation / Mean) × 100 = (2.83 / 6) × 100 = 47.16.

  41. Write the steps for constructing a grouped frequency distribution. Also define relative frequency and cumulative relative frequency.
    Topic: Frequency Distribution — Construction

    Steps for grouped frequency distribution: (1) Write the data in ordered array. (2) Find the smallest value (Xo) and largest value (Xm). (3) Find the range, which is the difference between the largest and smallest value. (4) Find the width (class interval), denoted by h: divide the range by the number of chosen classes (often 5 to 15 classes are assumed). (5) Make the number of classes from the smallest value to the largest value based on the class interval. (6) Now draw tally marks and find the frequency of the dataset. Relative frequency shows the portion or percentage of the data that falls in a particular class; it is the ratio of the number of times a value of the data occurs to the total number of outcomes: R.F = frequency of the class / total. The sum of the relative frequencies will always be 1. Cumulative relative frequency is the accumulation of the previous relative frequencies: Cumulative relative frequency = sum of previous relative frequencies + current class relative frequency.

  42. Differentiate between permutation and combination with formulas and examples.
    Topic: Probability and Counting Techniques

    Permutation: Permutations are the arrangements of objects in a specific order. When all objects are distinct, the formula is nPr = n! / (n− r)!. Example: A club consists of four members. How many ways are there of selecting three officers — president, secretary and treasurer? Here n = 4, r = 3, so 4P3 = 4! / (4 − 3)! = 24. When some objects are repeated, P = n! / (n1! × n2! × n3! × ...). Example: the number of permutations of the word 'BOOKKEEPER' (n = 10; O = 2, K = 2, E = 3) is P = 10! / (2! × 2! × 3!) = 151200. Combination: Combinations are the selections of elements without considering the order. The formula is nCr = n! / (r! (n− r)!). Example: From a group of 5 people, form a committee of 2 people: n = 5, r = 2, so C = 5! / (2! (5− 2)!) = 10 combinations. Difference: In a permutation the order of arrangement matters, while in a combination the order does not matter.

  43. Define probability. A fair six-sided die is tossed; find the probability of rolling an even number.
    Topic: Probability and Counting Techniques

    Probability = Number of favorable outcomes / Total number of possible outcomes. Example: Tossing a fair six-sided die, find the probability of rolling an even number. Possible (favorable) outcomes = 2, 4, 6, i.e. 3 outcomes. Total sides = 6. P = 3/6 = 1/2.

  44. Define sampling distribution.
    Topic: Population, Sample and Sampling

    The sampling distribution of a statistic is the probability distribution of that statistic (for example, the sample mean) obtained from all possible random samples of a fixed size n drawn from a population. It describes how the value of the statistic varies from one sample to another, and it is used to make inferences about the population parameters.

  45. What are the different types of events in probability?
    Topic: Probability and Distributions

    The main types of events are: (1) Simple event: an event consisting of exactly one outcome. (2) Compound event: an event consisting of two or more outcomes. (3) Mutually exclusive events: events that cannot occur at the same time, i.e. they have no outcome in common. (4) Independent events: events whose occurrence does not affect the probability of the other event. (5) Dependent events: events whose occurrence affects the probability of the other event. (6) Complementary events: two mutually exclusive events which together cover the whole sample space. (7) Equally likely events: events having the same chance of occurring. (8) Sure event and impossible event: events having probability 1 and 0 respectively.

  46. Define mutually exclusive events and complementary events, with examples.
    Topic: Probability and Distributions

    Mutually exclusive events are events that cannot happen at the same time; they have no common outcome, so P(A and B) = 0. Example: getting a head and getting a tail in a single toss of a coin. Complementary events are a pair of mutually exclusive events whose union is the whole sample space, so their probabilities add up to 1, i.e. P(A) + P(A') = 1. Example: getting a head and not getting a head (i.e. getting a tail) in one coin toss. Every pair of complementary events is mutually exclusive, but mutually exclusive events are complementary only when together they exhaust the sample space.

  47. What is SPSS? Describe its applications in statistical data analysis.
    Topic: Statistical Tests and SPSS

    SPSS (Statistical Package for the Social Sciences) is a widely used computer software package for statistical analysis. Data are entered and edited in its Data Editor window, and the package then processes them and presents the results in tables and graphs. Its main applications are: (1) entering, editing and managing data; (2) computing descriptive statistics such as frequencies, mean, median, mode and standard deviation; (3) drawing charts and graphs such as bar charts, pie charts, histograms and box plots; (4) performing inferential tests such as t-tests, chi-square tests and ANOVA; (5) correlation and regression analysis; and (6) diagnostic test analysis such as sensitivity, specificity and ROC curves. It is extensively used in medical and biological research for the analysis of experimental and survey data.

  48. What is an ROC curve? Write its uses in diagnostic testing.
    Topic: Statistical Tests and SPSS

    An ROC (Receiver Operating Characteristic) curve is a graph used to evaluate the performance of a diagnostic test. It is drawn by plotting sensitivity (the true positive rate) on the y-axis against 1 - specificity (the false positive rate) on the x-axis, for different cut-off values of the test. Its uses are: (1) to measure the overall accuracy of a diagnostic test: the greater the area under the curve (AUC), the better the test; (2) to select the best cut-off value of a test, balancing sensitivity and specificity; and (3) to compare two or more diagnostic tests: the test whose curve lies higher, with the larger AUC, is the better test.
