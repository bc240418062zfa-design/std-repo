  1. Enlist the minor elements and write down the role of each. [Asked in BOTH papers]
    Topic: Microbial Nutrition and Growth Requirements • 5 marks marks

    Phosphorus is generally provided as inorganic phosphate ions, often as a pH buffer. This element is essential for the synthesis of nucleic acids, intermediates of carbohydrate metabolism and compounds involved in energy transduction, e.g. adenosine triphosphate (ATP) and nicotinamide adenine dinucleotide phosphate (NADP). Sulphur is required for the production of the sulphur-containing amino acids cystine, cysteine and methionine, and some vitamins. It is often supplied as an inorganic sulphate or sulphide salt at a concentration of 20-30 mg/L. Other minor elements, mostly calcium, iron, potassium and magnesium, must be provided in relatively small but significant quantities, usually less than 10-20 mg/L. Iron is essential for several oxidation-reduction enzymes, particularly cytochromes, and potassium is required by enzymes involved in protein synthesis and as counter-ion for the DNA phosphate backbone. Magnesium ions are involved in the stabilization of ribosomes, and some are necessary for maintaining cell wall and membrane integrity. Sodium and potassium are used in chemiosmotic energy pumps, but the former (sodium) does not appear to have other specific roles, except in salt 'loving' halophilic microorganisms.

  2. Write the trace elements. Give examples and sources. Also define prototrophs and auxotrophs.
    Topic: Microbial Nutrition and Growth Requirements • 5 marks marks

    Trace elements include cobalt, copper, manganese, molybdenum, nickel, selenium and zinc. These elements are usually required at concentrations of 0.1-1 mg/L, or less, for a number of specific enzymes. Their normal concentrations in water supplies, or as contaminants in other media ingredients, often fulfil this requirement. Overall nutrient demands vary for different microorganisms. Many bacteria can grow on media merely containing carbon and energy sources, and basic mineral elements. Microorganisms that are able to grow on this minimal medium are referred to as prototrophs. However, other microorganisms must be given specific compounds in a part or fully constructed form. Those that are unable to grow without additional organic substances, such as amino acids or vitamins, are called auxotrophs. Culture media for many organotrophic microorganisms often require certain vitamin and growth factor supplements, especially B vitamins (thiamin, B1; riboflavin, B2; pyridoxine, B6; cobalamin, B12; biotin; nicotinic acid; and pantothenic acid). Few microorganisms require fat-soluble vitamins (A, D, E and K); and vitamin C, although often improving growth, is not a true microbial growth factor.

  6. What are the key factors to improve the efficiency of microbial growth?
    Topic: Microbial Nutrition and Growth Requirements

  7. Explain the effect of temperature on the growth of microorganisms. [Asked in BOTH papers]
    Topic: Microbial Growth and Its Measurement • 5 marks marks

    All organisms have an optimum temperature for growth. A typical microorganism, referred to as stenothermal, can grow over a temperature range of approximately 30 °C, while eurythermal organisms grow over even wider ranges. As for temperature optima, mesophiles have optimum growth temperatures in the range 20–45 °C and a minimum around 15–20 °C. Psychrophiles usually have their optimum temperatures below 15 °C and these organisms are often killed by exposure to room temperature. They are able to function at low temperature because their membranes contain a high proportion of unsaturated fatty acids. Organisms with optima above 50 °C are termed thermophiles. Several algal, protozoal and fungal species have temperature maxima up to 55 °C. However, it is only certain prokaryotes that are truly thermophilic, as no eukaryotic microbes grow at such high temperatures. Some extreme (hyper-) thermophiles can grow at 100 °C or higher. For example, Pyrolobus fumarii has an optimum of 106 °C and continues growing up to 113 °C. All of these are archaeans, which are mainly anaerobic sulphate reducers or have other metabolism with lower requirements for thermolabile cofactors such as NADH and NADPH. Microbial spores are particularly heat resistant. These dormant structures have thick spore coats and low water activity. Bacterial endospores contain high levels of calcium dipicolinate, which may constitute up to 15% of their dry weight. Its role is thought to be in stabilizing nucleic acids and proteins.

  8. Define acidophiles, alkalophiles and neutrophiles.
    Topic: Microbial Growth and Its Measurement • 3 marks marks

    True acidophiles have pH optima between 1 and 5.5, and often have mechanisms for the exclusion of protons in order to maintain their internal pH at a higher level. Alkalophiles, such as species of Bacillus and Micrococcus, have pH optima between 8.5 and 11.5, but take up protons to maintain their internal pH at a lower value. The majority of microorganisms are neutrophiles, growing between pH 5 and 9, as most natural environments fall within this range.

  10. Define doubling time, and write the doubling time of E. coli.
    Topic: Microbial Growth and Its Measurement • 3 marks marks

    Doubling time refers to the period it takes for a population of organisms or cells to double in number through natural reproduction. It is a crucial parameter in understanding the growth rate of a population and is often used in microbiology and cell biology to describe the exponential growth of microorganisms or cells under optimal conditions. The doubling time of E. coli, a commonly studied bacterium, can vary depending on factors such as nutrient availability, temperature, and other environmental conditions. Under ideal laboratory conditions, E. coli can have a doubling time of approximately 20 to 30 minutes. This rapid growth rate makes E. coli a valuable model organism for research and biotechnological applications.

  11. Why is E. coli an important industrial microorganism?
    Topic: Microbial Growth and Its Measurement • 2 marks marks

    The organism's rapid growth rate, with a doubling time as low as 20 min, has led to E. coli becoming an important industrial microorganism.

  12. Write the features of E. coli.
    Topic: Microbial Growth and Its Measurement • 5 marks marks

    E. coli (Escherichia coli) is a Gram-negative, rod-shaped (bacillus) bacterium that is a normal inhabitant of the intestine of humans and warm-blooded animals. It is a facultative anaerobe, is motile by peritrichous flagella, and does not form spores. It ferments lactose with the production of acid and gas, and forms characteristic colonies on selective media such as MacConkey and EMB agar. Under favourable conditions it grows very rapidly, with a doubling time of about 20-30 minutes, and its genetics and biochemistry are understood better than those of almost any other organism, which is why it is the most widely used model organism and host for recombinant DNA work in industry and research. Some strains are pathogenic and cause urinary tract infections and diarrhoeal diseases.

  13. Write a note on microbial growth kinetics.
    Topic: Microbial Growth and Its Measurement • 5 marks marks

    Microbial growth kinetics describes the rate at which a microbial population increases and the factors that control it. In batch culture the growth curve shows four phases: (1) the lag phase, in which cells adapt to the new environment and prepare for division, so numbers do not increase; (2) the log (exponential) phase, in which cells divide at a constant maximum rate and the population doubles at regular intervals (the doubling or generation time); (3) the stationary phase, in which nutrient depletion and accumulation of waste products make the growth rate equal to the death rate, so the total number of viable cells stays constant; and (4) the death (decline) phase, in which viable cells die faster than they are produced. During exponential growth the increase follows N = N0 x 2^n, where n is the number of generations. The specific growth rate (mu) is related to the doubling time (td) by mu = 0.693 / td. The dependence of growth rate on the concentration of the limiting nutrient is described by the Monod equation: mu = mu-max x S / (Ks + S), where S is the limiting substrate concentration and Ks is the saturation constant. Temperature, pH, water activity and oxygen supply all affect these kinetic parameters.

  15. What is ATP bioluminometry?
    Topic: Microbial Growth and Its Measurement • 3 marks marks

    The amount of ATP in a sample can be quantified using ATP bioluminometry. This technique utilizes an enzyme-substrate complex, luciferase-luciferin, obtained from the firefly Photinus pyralis, which generates a photon of light for each molecule of ATP.

  16. Write the effect of ionizing elements (radiations) on microbial growth.
    Topic: Microbial Growth and Its Measurement • 3 marks marks

    Ionizing radiations, such as X-rays and gamma rays, have enough energy to ionize molecules in the microbial cell. Their most important effect is damage to DNA - they break one or both strands of the DNA molecule, alter bases and produce harmful free radicals from water. Low doses may cause mutations, while higher doses stop reproduction and kill the cells, so ionizing radiation is used for the sterilization of heat-sensitive materials such as plastic ware, pharmaceuticals and some foods. Sensitivity varies between organisms: bacterial endospores and a few bacteria such as Deinococcus radiodurans are highly resistant, whereas most vegetative cells are readily inactivated.

  17. Write a note on the Embden-Meyerhof-Parnas (EMP) pathway.
    Topic: Microbial Metabolism • 5 marks marks

    The Embden-Meyerhof-Parnas (EMP) pathway is the most common route and is found in all major groups of organisms, including filamentous fungi, yeasts and many bacteria. This pathway can operate under aerobic and anaerobic conditions. It consists of 10 enzyme-catalyzed reactions located within the cytoplasmic matrix. Three key regulatory enzymes (hexokinase, phosphofructokinase and pyruvate kinase) act irreversibly. All other steps are freely reversible, which is important for the biosynthetic role of the pathway during glucose synthesis. Early stages of glucose breakdown consume 2 ATP molecules in the 3-stage formation of fructose 1,6-bisphosphate. This then forms two different triose (C3) phosphates, glyceraldehyde 3-phosphate (GAP) and dihydroxyacetone phosphate (DAP). DAP must be isomerized to GAP before it can be used. Oxidation of the resultant two GAP molecules to pyruvate generates energy in the form of four ATP molecules. The net gain is only two ATP, due to its consumption in the earlier reactions. GLUCOSE (C6) + 2 ADP + 2 Pi + 2 NAD+ -> 2 PYRUVATE (C3) + 2 ATP + 2 NADH + 2 H+

  18. Describe the three examples of anaerobic respiration.
    Topic: Microbial Metabolism • 5 marks marks

    1. Nitrate respiration, which is conducted by facultative anaerobic bacteria. The redox potential of nitrate is +0.42 volts, compared with +0.82 volts for oxygen. Denitrifiers include species of Pseudomonas, Paracoccus denitrificans and Thiobacillus denitrificans. Other facultative anaerobes, including E. coli and relatives, merely reduce nitrate to nitrite, and the enzyme responsible, nitrate reductase, is repressed in the presence of oxygen. 2. Sulphate respiration is practised by a small group of heterotrophic bacteria, which are all obligate anaerobes, e.g. Desulfovibrio species. The product is hydrogen sulphide, formed via several intermediates. 3. Carbonate respiration is performed by archaeans such as Methanococcus and Methanobacterium. They are obligate anaerobes that reduce CO2, and sometimes carbon monoxide, to methane.

  19. What is the location of the tricarboxylic acid (TCA) cycle in eukaryotes and prokaryotes?
    Topic: Microbial Metabolism • 2 marks marks

    Tricarboxylic acid cycle enzymes are located within the mitochondrial matrix in eukaryotes, whereas in prokaryotes they are cytoplasmic.

  20. Write a note on amino acid catabolism in microorganisms (the tricarboxylic acid cycle).
    Topic: Microbial Metabolism • 5 marks marks

    THE TRICARBOXYLIC ACID CYCLE. In many bacteria, yeasts, filamentous fungi, algae and protozoa, further catabolism of pyruvate under aerobic conditions involves its direction into the tricarboxylic acid (TCA) cycle. TCA cycle enzymes are located within the mitochondrial matrix in eukaryotes, whereas in prokaryotes they are cytoplasmic. The step immediately before the cycle involves the oxidative decarboxylation of pyruvate to form the C2 unit acetyl coenzyme A (acetyl CoA), catalysed by the multienzyme complex pyruvate dehydrogenase.

  21. Define autotrophs, phototrophs and organotrophs.
    Topic: Microbial Metabolism • 3 marks marks

    Autotrophs utilize CO2 as their sole or primary source of carbon. Phototrophs use energy derived from light. Organotrophs oxidize preformed organic molecules, such as sugars, to obtain electrons or hydrogen.

  22. Define group translocation.
    Topic: Microbial Metabolism • 3 marks marks

    Some compounds may be modified during uptake. Sugars, for example, can be phosphorylated using phosphoenolpyruvate (PEP) as the phosphate donor. This is referred to as group translocation, which is performed by many prokaryotic cells.

  23. What are hydrolytic enzymes? Give an example.
    Topic: Microbial Metabolism • 3 marks marks

    Hydrolytic enzymes, notably alkaline phosphatase, nucleases and proteases, are secreted into the periplasm from the cytoplasm and are retained close to the cell, as they cannot normally pass through the outer membrane. They break down large impermeable nutrients into smaller molecules that can be transported across the cell membrane into the cell. For example, penicillinase.

  24. Differentiate between primary and secondary metabolites.
    Topic: Microbial Metabolism • 5 marks marks

    Some microbial products are primary metabolites. They are produced during active growth (the trophophase). They include amino acids, organic acids and vitamins, and industrial solvents such as alcohols and acetone. Many of the most important industrial products are secondary metabolites, which are not essential for growth, e.g. alkaloids and antibiotics.

  25. What is batch and fed-batch fermentation?
    Topic: Fermentation Technology • 3 marks marks

    Batch growth involves a closed system where all nutrients are present at the start of the fermentation within a fixed volume. The only further additions may be acids or bases for pH control, or gases (e.g. aeration, if required). In fed-batch systems, fresh medium or medium components are fed continuously, intermittently, or are added as a single supplement, and the volume of the batch increases with time.

  26. Compare fed-batch, continuous and batch fermentation.
    Topic: Fermentation Technology

    Batch fermentation: all the raw materials are added at the beginning of the process, and the fermentation is allowed to proceed until the desired product is formed or the nutrients are depleted. Advantages: simple and easy to operate; suitable for small-scale production and research purposes. Disadvantages: inefficiency in terms of time and resources, as the process stops once the nutrients are depleted or waste products accumulate. Fed-batch fermentation: nutrients are added incrementally during the fermentation process. This allows for better control over the growth conditions, nutrient availability and waste product accumulation. Advantages: better control over the fermentation process; enhanced productivity compared to batch fermentation; allows optimization of specific growth phases. Disadvantages: more complex than batch fermentation; requires monitoring and control of nutrient addition. Continuous fermentation: involves a steady inflow of fresh media and the continuous removal of the fermented broth. This creates a constant environment for microbial growth and product formation. Advantages: high productivity and efficiency; stable and constant conditions for microbial growth; suitable for large-scale industrial production. Disadvantages: complex setup and control; prone to contamination due to continuous operation. Selection criteria: Batch is often used for small-scale production, initial process development, and when the product is sensitive to environmental changes. Fed-batch is suitable when it is important to control nutrient levels and maximize product yield, and is commonly used for large-scale production of high-value products. Continuous is ideal for large-scale, continuous production processes with high efficiency requirements, commonly used in industries where a constant supply of the product is needed. The choice depends on the nature of the microorganism, the product being synthesized, economic considerations, and the specific requirements of the bioprocess.

  27. Write a disadvantage of batch fermentation.
    Topic: Fermentation Technology • 3 marks marks

    Batch fermentation is inefficient for large-scale production due to the intermittent nature of nutrient addition and the need to start and stop the process. It often results in longer overall processing times and lower productivity compared to continuous or fed-batch fermentation.

  32. What are the three types of sterile filters?
    Topic: Fermentation Technology

    There are three main types of sterile filter: (1) Depth filters are thick fibrous or granular filters that remove microorganisms by physical screening, entrapment and adsorption. (2) Membrane filters are thin filters with defined pore sizes, usually of 0.2 or 0.45 micrometres (um), through which the microorganisms cannot pass. (3) High-efficiency particulate air (HEPA) filters are used in laminar flow biological safety cabinets and containment rooms to sterilize the air circulating in the enclosure.

  33. Name three preservatives with examples.
    Topic: Fermentation Technology • 3 marks marks

    Preservatives are chemical substances added to food and other products to inhibit microbial growth and prevent spoilage. Three important examples are: (1) Benzoic acid and its salt sodium benzoate, used in soft drinks, fruit juices, jams and pickles, effective mainly in acidic foods. (2) Sorbic acid and potassium sorbate, used in cheese, baked goods, wines and dried fruits, effective against moulds and yeasts. (3) Sulphur dioxide and sulphites, used in fruit juices, dried fruits and wines, which act both as antimicrobial agents and antioxidants. (Other examples include propionates in bread and nitrites in cured meats.)

  34. Write the difference between the pour plate and spread plate methods.
    Topic: Fermentation Technology

    In the pour plate method, a known volume of the (diluted) sample is mixed with molten agar in a sterile Petri dish; after solidification and incubation, colonies develop both on the surface and within the agar, and the method is mainly used for counting viable bacteria (CFU). In the spread plate method, a small volume of the diluted sample is placed on the surface of a pre-solidified agar plate and spread uniformly with a sterile spreader; all colonies develop only on the surface, are easier to count and isolate, and the cells are not exposed to hot molten agar.

  35. How many kingdoms are the Archaea divided into? Describe them. [Asked in BOTH papers]
    Topic: Microbial Diversity and Cell Structure • 5 marks marks

    The archaea may be divided into three kingdoms. (1) Euryarchaeota are primarily methanogens, such as Methanobacterium and Methanosarcina, and the extreme halophiles Halobacterium and Halococcus. (2) Crenarchaeota are mostly extreme barophiles and thermophiles, which include Pyrodictium, Pyrolobus, Sulfolobus and Thermoproteus. (3) Korarchaeota are hyperthermophiles which, as yet, have not been isolated as pure cultures.

  36. Define plasmid. Where is it present?
    Topic: Microbial Diversity and Cell Structure • 2 marks marks

    Plasmids are relatively small circular extrachromosomal DNA molecules. They are present in the bacterial cell in the cytoplasmic matrix.

  37. What is a capsule?
    Topic: Microbial Diversity and Cell Structure

    Some species of bacteria have a third protective covering, a capsule made up of polysaccharides (complex carbohydrates). Capsules play a number of roles, but the most important are to keep the bacterium from drying out and to protect it from phagocytosis (engulfing) by larger microorganisms. The capsule is a major virulence factor in the major disease-causing bacteria, such as Escherichia coli and Streptococcus pneumoniae. Nonencapsulated mutants of these organisms are avirulent, i.e. they do not cause disease.

  38. Write the salient features of eukaryotic cells.
    Topic: Microbial Diversity and Cell Structure • 3 marks marks

    The DNA of eukaryotic cells, in the form of several linear chromosomes, is characteristically complexed with histone proteins and is housed in a double membrane bound nucleus. Eukaryotic ribosomes, apart from those located within certain organelles, are 80S. Eukaryotic cells divide by a complex process of mitosis and usually have a sexual lifecycle, involving meiosis (reduction division).

  39. Write a note on the nucleus.
    Topic: Microbial Diversity and Cell Structure • 3 marks marks

    The nucleus is the largest and most prominent organelle of the eukaryotic cell, bounded by a double membrane (the nuclear envelope) which is perforated by nuclear pores that control the exchange of materials with the cytoplasm. It contains the cell's genetic material: DNA complexed with histone proteins, organized as chromatin/chromosomes. The nucleus controls all cellular activities by directing protein synthesis, and it is essential for cell division (mitosis and meiosis). Inside it lies the nucleolus, the site of ribosomal RNA synthesis and ribosome assembly. Prokaryotic cells lack such a membrane-bound nucleus; their DNA lies free in the cytoplasm in the nucleoid region.

  40. Describe the structure of fungal hyphae.
    Topic: Microbial Diversity and Cell Structure • 3 marks marks

    A hypha is a long, tubular, branching filament that forms the body (mycelium) of a fungus. Its wall is rigid and made mainly of chitin (with glucans), which protects the cell and gives it shape. In many fungi the hypha is divided by cross-walls called septa into compartments, each containing one or more nuclei (septate hyphae); the septa have central pores through which cytoplasm and even organelles can pass. In lower fungi the hyphae are without septa (aseptate/coenocytic) and are multinucleate throughout. The cytoplasm contains a plasma membrane, mitochondria, vacuoles, endoplasmic reticulum and other organelles, and growth takes place at the hyphal tip, which extends by the action of vesicles in the apical region.

  41. Describe the sexual and asexual reproduction of Phycomycetes.
    Topic: Microbial Diversity and Cell Structure • 3 marks marks

    Asexual reproduction results in the formation of saclike sporangia at the tips of upright hyphae or sporangiophores. Hundreds of haploid spores are produced by mitosis within the sporangium, which are then wind dispersed. In sexual reproduction, mycelia of opposite mating types each form gametangia containing several haploid nuclei. Plasmogamy of + and - gametangia, and pairing of haploid nuclei, results in the formation of a dikaryotic zygosporangium. This structure is metabolically inactive and resistant to desiccation. When conditions become favorable, karyogamy occurs between paired nuclei and the resulting diploid nuclei undergo meiosis to produce haploid spores.

  42. What are imperfect fungi?
    Topic: Microbial Diversity and Cell Structure

    Imperfect fungi, also known as Deuteromycota or fungi imperfecti, are a group of fungi that do not exhibit a sexual reproductive stage in their life cycle. The term 'imperfect' reflects the incomplete understanding of their sexual reproduction. As a disadvantage, their lack of a known sexual phase limits genetic variability, hindering adaptability to environmental changes and making them challenging to study in terms of evolutionary processes.

  43. Write a brief note on yeast. [Also asked in the current-paper list]
    Topic: Microbial Diversity and Cell Structure • 3 marks marks

    The term 'yeast' refers to a unicellular form rather than to a phylogenetic classification. It can be used to describe a unicellular phase of the life-cycles of fungi that may be predominantly filamentous, but exhibit yeast-mould dimorphism. The term is used more commonly as a generic name for fungi that have only a unicellular phase. These true yeasts, and many others of industrial importance, are strains of Saccharomyces cerevisiae, a member of the ascomycetes. Yeasts are heterotrophic. They are found in a wide range of natural habitats, being particularly common on aerial plant organs, especially flowers, and plant debris in the surface layers of soil.

  44. Describe the sexual and asexual reproduction in yeast.
    Topic: Microbial Diversity and Cell Structure • 5 marks marks

    Asexual reproduction in yeast takes place mainly by budding: a small outgrowth (bud) appears on the parent cell, the nucleus divides mitotically, one daughter nucleus passes into the bud, and the bud separates to form a new cell (as in Saccharomyces cerevisiae). Some yeasts, e.g. Schizosaccharomyces, reproduce asexually by fission, dividing into two equal cells. In sexual reproduction, two haploid yeast cells of opposite mating types conjugate and fuse (plasmogamy followed by karyogamy) to form a diploid cell. The diploid cell may multiply by budding, but under suitable (often nutrient-poor) conditions it undergoes meiosis, producing haploid ascospores - typically four - enclosed within the original cell, which now acts as an ascus. On release, each ascospore germinates to give a new haploid yeast cell.

  45. How does bacitracin inhibit growth?
    Topic: Antibiotics and Inhibition of Microbial Growth • 3 marks marks

    It acts by inhibiting the release of peptidoglycan subunits from the lipid-carrier molecule that transports them across the cytoplasmic membrane.

  46. What is the role of quinolones and penicillin in the inhibition of microbial growth?
    Topic: Antibiotics and Inhibition of Microbial Growth

    Quinolones (such as ciprofloxacin and levofloxacin) inhibit bacterial DNA replication by targeting enzymes involved in DNA synthesis, specifically DNA gyrase and topoisomerase IV. By interfering with these enzymes, quinolones prevent the relaxation of supercoiled DNA during replication and transcription, ultimately leading to the inhibition of bacterial cell division and growth. They are broad-spectrum antibiotics, effective against both Gram-negative and some Gram-positive bacteria, and are commonly used for urinary tract, respiratory tract and skin infections. They act on actively dividing bacteria. Penicillins (such as amoxicillin and ampicillin) interfere with bacterial cell wall synthesis. They target enzymes called penicillin-binding proteins (PBPs), which are involved in the cross-linking of peptidoglycan in the bacterial cell wall. Inhibition of these enzymes weakens the cell wall structure, leading to osmotic instability, bacterial cell lysis and death. Penicillins are effective against a wide range of bacteria, particularly Gram-positive bacteria, and are used for respiratory tract, skin and soft tissue, and urinary tract infections. They are most effective against actively growing and dividing bacteria.

  47. Define broad and narrow spectrum antibiotics. [Asked in the 2022 paper and again in a current-paper list]
    Topic: Antibiotics and Inhibition of Microbial Growth • 2 marks marks

    Antibiotics effective against a wide range of Gram-positive and Gram-negative bacteria are said to be broad spectrum. If they are predominantly effective against Gram-positive or Gram-negative bacteria, they are narrow spectrum.

  48. What are the impacts of industrial biotechnology? [Asked in BOTH papers - the 2022 paper asked the environmental impact separately]
    Topic: Industrial Biotechnology and Metabolic Engineering

    Industrial biotechnology has a positive impact on the environment, the economy and society. Environment: in the near future, industrial and environmental biotechnology will likely benefit those intermediate developing countries that have already established some industrial enterprises, for example in leather or metal production, and have at least a rudimentary set of environmental regulations, because it is generally easier to modify existing industries than to create new ones, and even minimal pollution control measures can convince industrialists to adopt bio-based cleaner production processes. The environment benefits because very little waste is released into the environment, excessive materials are used in further processing, there is a significant reduction in carbon dioxide emission, and less energy is consumed. Economy: industrial biotechnology has a positive impact on the economy, as new products are increased and are cheaper, due to less energy consumption and more efficient processes. Fermentation and enzymatic processes are commonly used in the fine chemicals sector, to produce for example vitamins, pharmaceutical intermediates and flavors. They are also making their first inroads into larger volume segments such as polymers, bulk chemicals and bio-fuels, and many other industrial sectors. Society: regarding all these environmental and economic impacts, industrial biotechnology is most favorable for the sustainability of society.

  49. Write the challenges faced in industrial biotechnology.
    Topic: Industrial Biotechnology and Metabolic Engineering

    The main challenges faced in industrial biotechnology include: the high cost of research, development and production, so that bio-based products often struggle to compete in price with cheap petrochemical products; difficulties in scaling up laboratory processes to industrial scale while keeping the strain productive and stable; the risk of contamination during long fermentations; costly downstream processing and product purification, which can account for much of the total cost; maintaining the genetic stability and high yield of engineered strains over many generations; strict regulatory requirements and public/ethical concerns, especially for genetically modified organisms and their release into the environment; and the need for skilled manpower and suitable infrastructure, which is limited in many developing countries.

  50. Write a note on metabolic engineering.
    Topic: Industrial Biotechnology and Metabolic Engineering • 5 marks marks

    Metabolic engineering is the practice of optimizing genetic and regulatory processes within cells to increase the cells' production of a certain substance. Some of the common strategies used for metabolic engineering are: (1) over-expressing the gene encoding the rate-limiting enzyme of the biosynthetic pathway; (2) blocking the competing metabolic pathways; (3) heterologous gene expression; and (4) enzyme engineering. Metabolic engineering typically uses recombinant DNA technology to develop micro-organisms giving improved product yields or even having totally new pathways. Such 'designer organisms' can be seen as cell factories and form the cornerstone of industrial biotechnology. Goals: (1) Mathematical modelling of microbial metabolism, directed towards both steady state and dynamic models. (2) Design of new pathways and synthetic microorganisms focusing on the synthesis of molecules and products new to nature.

  51. Define TDT, D-value and del factor.
    Topic: Industrial Biotechnology and Metabolic Engineering • 3 marks marks

    TDT (thermal death time) is the time required, at a specified temperature, to kill a given population of microorganisms in a sample - i.e. to destroy all viable cells/spores under the stated conditions. D-value (decimal reduction time) is the time, at a given temperature, required to reduce the microbial population by 90%, i.e. by one log cycle (to one-tenth of its original number). Del factor is the total log reduction achieved during a heating process: del = log N0 - log N, where N0 is the initial number of organisms and N is the number surviving after the process; it expresses how many decimal reductions the process has delivered.
