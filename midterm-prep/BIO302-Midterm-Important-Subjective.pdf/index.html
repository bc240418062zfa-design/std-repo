  1. What was the significance of the Griffith, Avery, and Hershey–Chase experiments?
    Topic: DNA as the Genetic Material & Discovery of DNA Structure

    They demonstrated that DNA was the molecule of heredity.

  2. Explain Frederick Griffith's experiment.
    Topic: DNA as the Genetic Material & Discovery of DNA Structure

    Frederick Griffith (1928) worked with two strains of Streptococcus pneumoniae: the virulent smooth (S) strain, which has a capsule and kills mice, and the harmless rough (R) strain, which lacks a capsule. He found that live S bacteria killed mice, live R bacteria did not, and heat-killed S bacteria did not. But when heat-killed S bacteria were mixed with live R bacteria and injected, the mice died and live S bacteria were recovered from their blood. He concluded that some 'transforming principle' from the dead S cells had transformed the R cells into virulent S cells.

  3. In 1928, Frederick Griffith established that __________.
    Topic: DNA as the Genetic Material & Discovery of DNA Structure

    Heat-killed bacteria harbor the constituent(s) necessary to convey genetic properties to living bacteria.

  4. Define the S and R forms of bacterial colonies.
    Topic: DNA as the Genetic Material & Discovery of DNA Structure

    In Griffith's experiments with Streptococcus pneumoniae, the S (smooth) form of the bacterium produces a polysaccharide capsule, forms smooth colonies and is virulent (causes pneumonia and death in mice). The R (rough) form lacks the capsule, forms rough colonies and is non-virulent (avirulent).

  5. What results did Avery, MacLeod, and McCarty obtain in their experiments with virulent bacteria?
    Topic: DNA as the Genetic Material & Discovery of DNA Structure

    DNase destroyed the transforming activity (showing that the transforming principle was DNA).

  6. To be certain that the extract prepared from virulent cells still contained the transforming principle that was present prior to lysis, Avery __________.
    Topic: DNA as the Genetic Material & Discovery of DNA Structure

    Incubated nonvirulent cells with the complete extract.

  7. If Avery had observed transformation using only the extracts containing degraded DNA, degraded RNA, and degraded protein, but NOT the extract containing degraded polysaccharides, he would have concluded that __________.
    Topic: DNA as the Genetic Material & Discovery of DNA Structure

    Polysaccharides are the genetic material.

  8. The Hershey and Chase experiments involved the preparation of two different types of radioactively labeled phage. Which of the following best explains why two preparations were required?
    Topic: DNA as the Genetic Material & Discovery of DNA Structure

    It was necessary that each of the two phage components, DNA and protein, be identifiable upon recovery at the end of the experiment.

  9. How would Hershey and Chase learn whether genes were made of protein or DNA?
    Topic: DNA as the Genetic Material & Discovery of DNA Structure

    They grew viruses in cultures containing radioactive isotopes of phosphorus-32 (32P) and sulfur-35 (35S). Proteins contain almost no phosphorus and DNA contains almost no sulfur, so the two components could be followed separately.

  10. Which of the following outcomes would be most likely if the Hershey–Chase experiments were repeated without the step involving the blender?
    Topic: DNA as the Genetic Material & Discovery of DNA Structure

    Both preparations of infected bacteria would exhibit radioactivity.

  11. Write the contribution of Franklin and Wilkins to the discovery of DNA structure.
    Topic: DNA as the Genetic Material & Discovery of DNA Structure

    Maurice Wilkins and Rosalind Franklin, together with Ray Gosling, Alec Stokes, Herbert Wilson and other colleagues at the Randall Institute at King's, made crucial contributions to the discovery of DNA's structure in 1953. Wilkins began using optical spectroscopy to study DNA in the late 1940s. In 1950 he and Gosling obtained the first clearly crystalline X-ray diffraction patterns from DNA fibres. Alec Stokes suggested that the patterns indicated that DNA was helical in structure. The discovery of the structure of DNA in 1953 revealed the physical and chemical basis of how characteristics are passed down through the generations and how they are expressed in individual organisms.

  12. What did Matthew Meselson and Franklin Stahl prove?
    Topic: DNA as the Genetic Material & Discovery of DNA Structure

    They proved that DNA replicates in a semiconservative manner.

  13. Which of the following statements about DNA structure is true?
    Topic: DNA Structure, Nucleotides & Chromatin

    The nucleic acid strands in a DNA molecule are oriented antiparallel to each other, meaning they run in opposite directions.

  14. Identify three possible components of a DNA nucleotide.
    Topic: DNA Structure, Nucleotides & Chromatin

    Deoxyribose, a phosphate group, and a nitrogenous base (e.g., thymine).

  15. Differentiate between ribose and deoxyribose sugars.
    Topic: DNA Structure, Nucleotides & Chromatin

    Ribose and deoxyribose are both pentose (5-carbon) sugars. Ribose has a hydroxyl (–OH) group on its 2′carbon, whereas deoxyribose has only a hydrogen (–H) at the 2 ′position — it 'lacks' one oxygen, hence 'deoxy'. Ribose is the sugar of RNA and deoxyribose is the sugar of DNA; the missing 2 ′-OH makes DNA more chemically stable than RNA.

  16. Write the roles of nucleosomes.
    Topic: DNA Structure, Nucleotides & Chromatin

    1) Nucleosomes fold enormous lengths of DNA into the tiny space available in the cell nucleus. 2) They regulate how genes are 'read' to make proteins.

  17. Name five histone proteins.
    Topic: DNA Structure, Nucleotides & Chromatin

    The histones fall into five major classes, i.e., H1, H2A, H2B, H3 and H4. A typical human cell contains about 60 million copies of each kind of histone.

  18. Write a short note on the Zigzag model.
    Topic: DNA Structure, Nucleotides & Chromatin

    Zigzag model: the zigzag model predicts that the linker DNA forms a straight path between successive nucleosomes and the nucleosomes lie on opposite sides of the fiber. Solenoid model: the solenoid model predicts that the nucleosome chain forms a helical structure with about 6 nucleosomes per turn, and the linker DNA is bent to connect neighbouring nucleosomes. Some of the reconstitution experiments appear to support the zigzag model, while some others support the solenoid model.

  19. Write the characteristics of genetic material.
    Topic: DNA Structure, Nucleotides & Chromatin

    The genetic material must: (1) store all the information needed for the structure and functioning of the organism (information storage); (2) replicate faithfully, so that the information passes from generation to generation (replication); (3) be expressed — its information must be usable to make RNA and proteins (expression); and (4) be able to change occasionally by mutation, providing variation, while remaining chemically stable.

  20. What do you mean by DNA replication?
    Topic: DNA Replication

    DNA replication is the process of making an identical copy of a section of duplex (double-stranded) DNA, using existing DNA as a template for the synthesis of new DNA strands. In humans and other eukaryotes, replication occurs in the cell nucleus.

  21. Define conservative, semiconservative and dispersive replication in DNA.
    Topic: DNA Replication

    Semiconservative replication: the generally accepted method of DNA replication, in which the two strands of the DNA helix separate and free nucleotides pair with the exposed bases on the single chains to form two new DNA molecules, each containing one original and one newly synthesized strand of DNA. Conservative replication: a hypothetical form of replication in which a double-stranded DNA produces two daughter dsDNAs, one of which consists of the two original strands whereas the other daughter DNA consists of two newly synthesized chains. Dispersive replication: would produce two copies of the DNA, both containing distinct regions of DNA composed of either both original strands or both new strands (a mixture of old and new DNA).

  22. How does DNA replication initiate? [2 marks]
    Topic: DNA Replication

    The proteins that initiate DNA replication bind to DNA sequences at a replication origin to catalyze the formation of a replication bubble with two outward-moving replication forks. The process begins when an initiator protein–DNA complex is formed that subsequently loads a DNA helicase onto the DNA template.

  23. What is a replication fork?
    Topic: DNA Replication

    A replication fork is the Y-shaped structure formed when the DNA double helix is unwound at the origin of replication. It is the site where the parental strands are separated and new daughter strands are synthesized — the leading strand continuously and the lagging strand discontinuously as Okazaki fragments — and it moves along the DNA as replication proceeds.

  24. Write the salient features of DNA polymerase.
    Topic: DNA Replication

    The DNA polymerases are enzymes that create DNA molecules by assembling nucleotides, the building blocks of DNA. This region of DNA polymerase binds two divalent metal ions (Mg2+ or Zn2+). DNA polymerase is required to help duplicate the cell's DNA, so that a copy of the original DNA molecule can be passed to each of the daughter cells; in this way, genetic information is transmitted from generation to generation. DNA polymerase adds new free nucleotides to the 3 ′end of the newly-forming strand, elongating it in a 5′to 3′direction. The DNA is therefore 'proofread' by DNA polymerase after it has been copied, so that misplaced base pairs can be corrected.

  25. Differentiate between DNA polymerase I, II and III.
    Topic: DNA Replication

    DNA polymerase I: removes the RNA primers (by its 5 ′→ 3′exonuclease activity) and fills the gaps during replication; it is also used in DNA repair and has low processivity. DNA polymerase II: mainly involved in DNA repair and in restarting stalled replication forks; it has 3′→ 5′proofreading activity. DNA polymerase III: the main replicative polymerase in prokaryotes; as part of the holoenzyme it synthesizes the leading and lagging strands with very high processivity and has 3′→ 5′proofreading exonuclease activity.

  26. What is the polarity of DNA helicase?
    Topic: DNA Replication

    DNA helicases found at replication forks exhibit high processivity because they encircle the DNA. They associate with the DNA and unwind multiple base pairs of DNA. Release of the helicase from the DNA therefore requires the opening of the hexameric protein ring, which is a rare event; however, the helicase can dissociate when it reaches the end of the DNA strand. Of course, this arrangement of enzyme and DNA poses problems for the binding of the DNA helicase to the DNA strand in the first place; thus, there are specialized mechanisms that open the DNA helicase (hexameric) ring and place it around the DNA before re-forming the ring. Each DNA helicase moves along ssDNA in a defined direction; this property is referred to as the polarity of the DNA helicase. DNA helicases can have a polarity of either 5′→ 3′or 3′→ 5′. This direction is always defined according to the strand of DNA bound rather than the strand that is displaced.

  27. What are Okazaki fragments?
    Topic: DNA Replication

    Okazaki fragments are the short, discontinuous stretches of newly synthesized DNA (about 100–200 nucleotides in eukaryotes and 1000–2000 nucleotides in prokaryotes) formed on the lagging strand during DNA replication, because DNA polymerase can synthesize DNA only in the 5 ′→ 3′direction. Each fragment begins with an RNA primer; the fragments are later joined together (after primer removal and gap filling by DNA polymerase, and sealing by DNA ligase) to form a continuous strand.

  28. What is the end-replication problem of DNA?
    Topic: DNA Replication

    Because DNA polymerase can add nucleotides only to a 3 ′-OH group and needs a primer, the lagging strand at the very end of a linear chromosome cannot be completed: when the terminal RNA primer is removed there is no upstream 3 ′-OH to extend from, leaving a gap. As a result, linear chromosomes would shorten with every round of replication — the end-replication problem. Cells solve it through telomeres and the enzyme telomerase, which extends the chromosome ends.

  29. What is telomerase? Briefly write its functions.
    Topic: DNA Replication

    Telomerase is a ribonucleoprotein enzyme (a reverse transcriptase) that extends the telomeres — the repetitive DNA sequences (TTAGGG in vertebrates) at the ends of linear chromosomes. Using its own RNA component as a template, it adds telomeric repeats to the 3 ′end of the chromosome, thus solving the end-replication problem and preventing the progressive shortening of chromosomes at each cell division.

  30. What is decatenation?
    Topic: DNA Replication

    Decatenation is the separation (unlinking) of two interlocked (catenated) daughter DNA molecules produced after the replication of a circular DNA molecule (or of intertwined regions of linear chromosomes). It is carried out by a type II topoisomerase (DNA gyrase / topoisomerase IV in bacteria), which passes one duplex through a temporary break in the other so that the two molecules can segregate into the daughter cells.

  31. How many standard amino acids are there? Name any 5. [5 marks]
    Topic: Amino Acids, Proteins & Enzymes

    In eukaryotes, there are only 21 proteinogenic amino acids — the 20 of the standard genetic code, plus selenocysteine. Humans can synthesize 12 of these from each other or from other molecules of intermediary metabolism. Examples: Alanine, Tryptophan, Glutamine, Aspartic acid, Histidine.

  32. Define a peptide bond.
    Topic: Amino Acids, Proteins & Enzymes

    In a protein molecule, each amino acid residue is joined to its neighbour by a specific type of covalent bond, which is called a peptide bond.

  33. Why can't the C–N bond of a peptide bond rotate freely? [3 marks]
    Topic: Amino Acids, Proteins & Enzymes

    The stability of the peptide bond is due to the resonance of amides. With resonance, the nitrogen is able to donate its lone pair of electrons to the carbonyl carbon and push electrons from the carbonyl double bond towards the oxygen, forming the oxygen anion. This resonance effect is very stabilizing because the electrons can be delocalized over multiple atoms, with one especially stable resonance structure containing the highly electronegative oxygen as an anion. The double bond character of the C–N bond results in a relatively short bond. The double bond resonance form of the peptide bond helps to increase stability and decrease rotation about that bond. The partial double bond character is either strengthened or weakened depending upon the environment that it is in.

  34. Write any three functions of an enzyme.
    Topic: Amino Acids, Proteins & Enzymes

    Enzymes are biological catalysts. Three of their functions are: (1) they speed up biochemical reactions enormously by lowering the activation energy, without being consumed in the reaction; (2) they provide specificity — each enzyme acts on a particular substrate or reaction; and (3) they allow the regulation of metabolism, because enzyme activity can be switched on or off (e.g., by allosteric control or covalent modification) according to the needs of the cell.

  35. What does the suffix 'ase' mean? Enlist any three examples.
    Topic: Amino Acids, Proteins & Enzymes

    The suffix '-ase' denotes an enzyme. Examples: DNA polymerase (synthesizes DNA), helicase (unwinds DNA) and ligase (joins DNA fragments). Other examples are nuclease, protease, amylase and lipase.

  36. What is the central dogma of molecular biology?
    Topic: Transcription, RNA Processing & Gene Regulation

    The central dogma of molecular biology (proposed by Francis Crick) states that genetic information flows in one direction: DNA→ RNA → Protein. DNA is copied into RNA by transcription, and RNA is used to synthesize proteins by translation; DNA itself is copied by replication. Information does not normally flow back from protein to nucleic acid.

  37. Write the types of RNAs.
    Topic: Transcription, RNA Processing & Gene Regulation

    There are actually several types of ribonucleic acids or RNAs, but mainly three types of ribonucleic acids (RNAs) are present in the cells of living organisms: 1. Messenger RNA (mRNA); 2. Transfer RNA (tRNA); 3. Ribosomal RNA (rRNA).

  38. Write the characteristics of the bacterial core enzyme.
    Topic: Transcription, RNA Processing & Gene Regulation

    The bacterial RNA polymerase core enzyme has the subunit composition α2ββ′ω . It can carry out RNA synthesis (elongation) on a DNA template, but it cannot recognize promoters or initiate transcription specifically by itself; for promoter recognition and specific initiation it must combine with a sigma (σ) factor to form the holoenzyme. Thus, the core enzyme has the catalytic (polymerase) activity for RNA synthesis in the 5′→ 3′direction but lacks initiation specificity.

  39. Describe the RNA polymerase events (process of transcription).
    Topic: Transcription, RNA Processing & Gene Regulation

    Transcription by RNA polymerase occurs in three events/steps: (1) Initiation — the polymerase (holoenzyme) binds the promoter, forms first the closed and then the open complex by melting the DNA, and starts RNA synthesis (with abortive initiation of short oligonucleotides). (2) Elongation — the polymerase moves along the template, adding nucleotides to the growing RNA chain in the 5 ′→ 3′ direction. (3) Termination — at the terminator sequence the polymerase, the RNA transcript and the DNA separate, ending transcription.

  40. What is the transition to the open complex?
    Topic: Transcription, RNA Processing & Gene Regulation

    During transcription initiation, RNA polymerase first binds the promoter to form a closed complex. It then unwinds (melts) about 10–14 base pairs of DNA around the −10 region/start site to form the open complex, in which the template strand is positioned in the active site so that the first ribonucleotides can be incorporated. This isomerization from the closed to the open complex is an essential step for initiation.

  41. What is the function of the TATA box?
    Topic: Transcription, RNA Processing & Gene Regulation

    The TATA box helps position RNA polymerase by marking a point just before the point at which transcription begins.

  42. What is the elongation step in RNA (transcription)?
    Topic: Transcription, RNA Processing & Gene Regulation

    During elongation, RNA polymerase moves along the DNA template, unwinding the DNA ahead of it and rewinding it behind. It adds ribonucleotides complementary to the template strand at the 3′end of the growing RNA chain (synthesis in the 5′→ 3′direction), maintaining a short DNA–RNA hybrid of about 8–9 base pairs within the transcription bubble, until the terminator is reached.

  43. Write the function of elongation polymerases.
    Topic: Transcription, RNA Processing & Gene Regulation

    Elongation polymerases extend the growing nucleic acid chain during the elongation phase. In transcription, RNA polymerase in its elongation phase moves along the template, unwinds DNA ahead and rewinds it behind, and adds nucleotides in the 5′→ 3′direction at a high rate while maintaining the transcription bubble. In DNA replication, the replicative polymerase (e.g., DNA polymerase III in prokaryotes) continuously extends the leading strand and the Okazaki fragments of the lagging strand from the primer 3′-OH.

  44. Define the role of terminator sequences.
    Topic: Transcription, RNA Processing & Gene Regulation

    Terminator sequences signal the end of transcription. When RNA polymerase transcribes a terminator (in prokaryotes, a GC-rich inverted repeat that forms a hairpin in the RNA, followed by a run of U residues — Rho-independent termination — or termination with the help of the Rho factor), the polymerase pauses, the RNA transcript is released and the enzyme dissociates from the DNA, preventing transcription from continuing into neighbouring genes.

  45. How many CTD repeats are present in Drosophila, yeast and human?
    Topic: Transcription, RNA Processing & Gene Regulation

    The C-terminal domain (CTD) of the largest subunit of RNA polymerase II consists of repeats of the heptapeptide sequence Tyr-Ser-Pro-Thr-Ser-Pro-Ser (YSPTSPS). The number of repeats differs among organisms: about 26–27 repeats in yeast (Saccharomyces cerevisiae), about 45 repeats in Drosophila, and 52 repeats in humans (mammals).

  46. What are the activators? How do activators recruit additional factors?
    Topic: Transcription, RNA Processing & Gene Regulation

    Activator proteins bound to enhancer regions recruit additional factors needed for transcription: coactivators and the Mediator complex, the general transcription factors and RNA polymerase II (to form the pre-initiation complex), and chromatin-remodelling complexes and histone-modifying enzymes (e.g., histone acetyltransferases) that open the chromatin so that the transcription machinery can access the promoter.

  47. What are the requirements for the highly regulated level of transcription?
    Topic: Transcription, RNA Processing & Gene Regulation

    Highly regulated transcription requires: specific regulatory DNA sequences (promoters, enhancers, silencers and insulators); sequence-specific transcription factors (activators and repressors); coactivators and the Mediator complex; an accessible chromatin structure maintained by chromatin-remodelling complexes and histone modifications; and the general transcription factors that assemble RNA polymerase at the promoter. Signals from outside the cell can switch these factors on or off, so that a gene is transcribed only when and where it is needed.

  48. What is the operon model? Describe it with the lac operon.
    Topic: Transcription, RNA Processing & Gene Regulation

    An operon is a cluster of structural genes transcribed together as a single mRNA, regulated by one promoter and an operator; operons are common in prokaryotes. In the lac operon of E. coli, the genes lacZ (β-galactosidase), lacY (permease) and lacA (transacetylase) are required for lactose utilization. The repressor protein (product of the lacI gene) binds the operator and switches the operon OFF when lactose is absent. When lactose is present, its isomer allolactose acts as an inducer: it binds the repressor and inactivates it, so RNA polymerase can transcribe the genes and the operon is ON.

  49. Explain the RNA processing events.
    Topic: Transcription, RNA Processing & Gene Regulation

    In eukaryotes, the primary transcript (pre-mRNA) is processed in the nucleus into mature mRNA by three events: (1) 5′capping — a modified guanine nucleotide (7-methylguanosine cap) is added to the 5′end, which protects the mRNA and helps in ribosome binding; (2) splicing — the introns are removed by the spliceosome and the exons are joined together; (3) 3′polyadenylation — the transcript is cleaved at the poly-A signal and a tail of about 200 adenine nucleotides (poly-A tail) is added, which stabilizes the mRNA.

  50. Explain the classes of splicing.
    Topic: Transcription, RNA Processing & Gene Regulation

    There are four classes of intron splicing: (1) Group I introns — self-splicing, catalyzed by the intron RNA itself (a ribozyme) using an external guanosine cofactor; (2) Group II introns — self-splicing through a lariat intermediate, without an external cofactor; (3) Nuclear pre-mRNA (spliceosomal) introns — removed by the spliceosome (snRNPs) through a lariat intermediate; (4) tRNA introns — spliced by enzymes (an endonuclease and a ligase), not by transesterification.

  51. How do group I introns splice?
    Topic: Transcription, RNA Processing & Gene Regulation

    Group I introns are self-splicing (catalytic RNA, i.e., ribozymes). Splicing occurs by two successive transesterification reactions: first, an external guanosine (G/GTP) cofactor attacks the 5′splice site and cuts the RNA, attaching itself to the 5′end of the intron; second, the free 3′-OH of the upstream exon attacks the 3′splice site, joining the two exons together and releasing the intron as a linear molecule. No ATP or protein enzyme is required.

  52. Define the spliceosome.
    Topic: Transcription, RNA Processing & Gene Regulation

    The spliceosome is a large complex of small nuclear ribonucleoproteins (snRNPs — U1, U2, U4, U5 and U6) and many proteins that assembles on a pre-mRNA and removes (splices out) the introns, joining the exons together to form mature mRNA in the nucleus of eukaryotic cells.

  53. mRNA has a short life period. Write the name of the experiment which showed this, and its description.
    Topic: Transcription, RNA Processing & Gene Regulation

    The short life of mRNA was demonstrated by the experiment of Sydney Brenner, François Jacob and Matthew Meselson (1961). They infected E. coli with T2 bacteriophage and used density (isotope) labelling: after infection, the newly synthesized RNA was found associated with the pre-existing (old) ribosomes, showing that ribosomes are non-specific machines and that a short-lived messenger RNA carries the genetic instructions from DNA to the ribosomes. (Jacob and Monod had predicted such a messenger from the rapid on/off kinetics of enzyme induction in the lac operon.)

  54. What is an anticodon?
    Topic: Transcription, RNA Processing & Gene Regulation

    The anticodon is the three bases on the tRNA molecule, complementary to one of the mRNA codons.

  55. Where does translation occur?
    Topic: Transcription, RNA Processing & Gene Regulation

    Translation occurs in the cytoplasm (on the ribosomes).

  56. What are the types of UV rays? Also define the radiations which can damage DNA.
    Topic: DNA Damage, Mutations & Repair

    Cells are exposed to three types of high-energy electromagnetic radiations which can damage their DNA. These radiations include: ultraviolet light (wavelength 100–400 nm), X-rays (wavelength 0.01–100 nm) and gamma rays (wavelength <0.01 nm). The latter two are ionizing radiations. Ultraviolet light is divided into three bands: UV-A (321–400 nm), UV-B (296–320 nm) and UV-C (100–295 nm).

  57. How do different UV radiations reach the Earth?
    Topic: DNA Damage, Mutations & Repair

    Of the ultraviolet radiation emitted by the sun, UV-C (100–295 nm) is completely absorbed by the ozone layer and oxygen in the atmosphere and does not reach the Earth. Most of the UV-B (296–320 nm) is also absorbed by the ozone layer, and only a small fraction reaches the surface. UV-A (321–400 nm) is not absorbed by ozone, so nearly all of it reaches the Earth's surface.

  58. How and what cross-linkage is caused by alkylating agents? [2 marks]
    Topic: DNA Damage, Mutations & Repair

    Alkylating agents utilize selective alkylation by adding the desired aliphatic carbon chain to the previously chosen starting molecule; this is one of many known chemical syntheses. Alkylating agents are a highly reactive group of electrophiles which transfer methyl, ethyl or alkyl groups to the electron-rich atoms in the DNA and damage it. Bifunctional alkylating agents can join two positions in the DNA, forming cross-links.

  59. Why does a wrongly incorporated nucleotide during DNA replication result in a permanent mutation? [3 marks]
    Topic: DNA Damage, Mutations & Repair

    If DNA polymerase incorporates the wrong nucleotide into a newly synthesized DNA strand, a single base-pair substitution will occur. Another kind of error, called strand slippage, happens when DNA polymerase adds an extra nucleotide or skips a nucleotide in the newly synthesized DNA strand, which results respectively in an insertion or deletion. Strand slippage often occurs in regions of the genome with repetitive DNA sequence. If such an error escapes proofreading and repair, it is copied in the next round of replication and becomes a permanent mutation.

  60. Name the mutations which cause a one-base change.
    Topic: DNA Damage, Mutations & Repair

    A substitution is a mutation that exchanges one base for another (i.e., a change in a single 'chemical letter' such as switching an A to a G). Such a substitution could: 1) change a codon to one that encodes a different amino acid and cause a small change in the protein produced — for example, sickle cell anemia is caused by a substitution in the beta-hemoglobin gene, which alters a single amino acid in the protein produced; 2) change a codon to one that encodes the same amino acid and cause no change in the protein produced — these are called silent mutations; 3) change an amino-acid-coding codon to a single 'stop' codon and cause an incomplete protein, which can have serious effects since the incomplete protein probably won't function.

  61. Write the mutations caused by base analogs and intercalating agents.
    Topic: DNA Damage, Mutations & Repair

    Base analogs (e.g., 5-bromouracil, an analog of thymine) are incorporated into DNA in place of the normal bases during replication; because they pair ambiguously (5-bromouracil can pair with adenine or with guanine), they cause base-pair substitution (transition) mutations in the next round of replication. Intercalating agents (e.g., acridine dyes such as proflavin, and ethidium bromide) are flat molecules that insert (intercalate) between adjacent base pairs of DNA and distort the helix; during replication this makes the polymerase add or skip a nucleotide, producing insertion/deletion (frameshift) mutations.

  62. Differentiate between direct damage and indirect damage by ionizing radiation.
    Topic: DNA Damage, Mutations & Repair

    Direct damage occurs when a photon of ionizing radiation is absorbed directly by the DNA molecule itself, ionizing it and breaking its chemical bonds. Indirect damage occurs when the radiation ionizes the surrounding water molecules, producing free radicals (reactive oxygen species such as the hydroxyl radical •OH) which then diffuse and attack the DNA. About 65% of the DNA damage caused by these radiations is due to indirect effects, primarily due to the transfer of photons to water.

  63. How do reactive oxygen species damage the DNA?
    Topic: DNA Damage, Mutations & Repair

    Reactive oxygen species (ROS) such as superoxide, hydrogen peroxide and hydroxyl radicals — produced by normal metabolism and by ionizing radiation — oxidize DNA bases (e.g., converting guanine to 8-oxoguanine, which pairs with adenine and causes G → T transversions), attack the sugar-phosphate backbone causing single- and double-strand breaks, and cause loss of bases (AP sites). Such oxidative damage is repaired mainly by base excision repair.

  64. What is the effect of the 6-4 photoproduct on cell function?
    Topic: DNA Damage, Mutations & Repair

    The (6-4) photoproduct is formed when UV light joins the C-6 and C-4 positions of two adjacent pyrimidines. It bends and distorts the DNA helix more strongly than a cyclobutane dimer, so it blocks DNA replication and transcription and stalls the polymerase; if it is not removed (by nucleotide excision repair) it leads to mutations and cell death. It is therefore highly cytotoxic and mutagenic.

  65. Why does a DNA strand with one or more AP sites make a poor template?
    Topic: DNA Damage, Mutations & Repair

    An AP (apurinic/apyrimidinic) site is a position in DNA where the base has been lost, leaving only the sugar-phosphate backbone. Because there is no base at that position, the site carries no coding information: DNA polymerase cannot know which nucleotide to insert opposite it, so it either stalls (blocking replication) or inserts a random nucleotide (usually adenine), making the strand a poor and error-prone template.

  66. How is psoralen activated?
    Topic: DNA Damage, Mutations & Repair

    Psoralen is a flat, light-sensitive compound that intercalates into DNA without reacting in the dark. On exposure to ultraviolet A light (UV-A, 320–400 nm) it becomes activated (photoactivation) and forms covalent monoadducts with pyrimidines (mainly thymine); with a second photon it can form interstrand cross-links between the two DNA strands. This property is used in PUVA therapy for skin diseases such as psoriasis.

  67. Enlist any 3 ways in which PAHs are formed.
    Topic: DNA Damage, Mutations & Repair

    Polycyclic aromatic hydrocarbons (PAHs) are formed by the incomplete combustion of organic matter, for example: (1) burning of fossil fuels — coal, oil, and diesel/petrol exhaust from vehicles; (2) burning of wood, biomass and tobacco (tobacco smoke); (3) high-temperature cooking, grilling and charring of food, and industrial processes such as coke production and waste incineration.

  68. Which fungi produce aflatoxin?
    Topic: DNA Damage, Mutations & Repair

    Aflatoxins are produced mainly by the fungi Aspergillus flavus and Aspergillus parasiticus (also Aspergillus nomius). They contaminate stored grains, peanuts and corn, and aflatoxin B1 is a potent mutagen and liver carcinogen.

  69. What is methylation of N-3 in adenine, and how is it important for the treatment of cancer?
    Topic: DNA Damage, Mutations & Repair

    Methylation at the N-3 position of adenine produces 3-methyladenine, a cytotoxic lesion that blocks DNA polymerase and stops replication; it is removed by base excision repair (initiated by alkyladenine DNA glycosylase). This is important in cancer treatment because many chemotherapy drugs are alkylating agents that kill rapidly dividing cancer cells by producing such blocking lesions (e.g., 3-methyladenine and O6-methylguanine); the repair capacity of the tumour determines how well the cancer responds to, or becomes resistant to, these drugs.

  70. Write the mechanism of CPD photolyase in the repair of DNA. [5 marks]
    Topic: DNA Damage, Mutations & Repair

    CPD photolyase can bind to DNA in the dark by recognizing the altered DNA structure caused by a CPD formation rather than a specific nucleotide sequence. This binding is about 10^5 times tighter when a DNA segment contains a CPD than when it does not. Half of the binding energy appears to come from interactions between the enzyme and the DNA backbone, and the other half of the energy comes from interactions between the FADH at the active site and the CPD; however, the light-harvesting antenna pigment does not influence binding. Once the enzyme–DNA complex is formed, the CPD is flipped out of the DNA double helix and into the enzyme's active site. After the CPD flips into the enzyme's active site, the energy of an absorbed photon is transferred from the light-harvesting antenna pigment to the FADH; FADH then transfers an electron to the CPD to induce cyclobutane ring cleavage. The catalytic cycle is completed when the electron is transferred back from the repaired thymine to the FADH cofactor.

  71. What is the photoreactivation system?
    Topic: DNA Damage, Mutations & Repair

    Photoreactivation is a light-dependent direct-reversal repair system in which the enzyme photolyase (CPD photolyase) uses energy absorbed from visible/blue light to split (monomerize) the cyclobutane pyrimidine dimers (CPDs) in DNA, restoring the bases to their original state without removing any nucleotides. It is an error-free repair mechanism: the enzyme binds the dimer in the dark, flips it into its active site, and an electron transfer driven by the absorbed photon cleaves the cyclobutane ring.

  72. How was the first clue of an enzyme that repairs damaged DNA discovered? How did Albert Kelner report the direct reversal of DNA damage in 1949?
    Topic: DNA Damage, Mutations & Repair

    Albert Kelner (1949) obtained the first clue of an enzyme-catalyzed repair of damaged DNA while studying UV-irradiated bacteria (E. coli and Streptomyces griseus). He found that UV-inactivated cells recovered when they were exposed to visible light — a phenomenon called photoreactivation. This showed that DNA damage could be directly reversed by a light-dependent enzyme (later identified as photolyase); it was the first DNA repair mechanism to be discovered.

  73. Name three proteins that catalyse DNA repair (direct reversal).
    Topic: DNA Damage, Mutations & Repair

    1) O6-alkylguanine DNA alkyltransferase I; 2) O6-alkylguanine DNA alkyltransferase II; 3) O6-alkylguanine DNA alkyltransferase (the source lists type I again as the third entry). These enzymes directly reverse alkylation damage by removing the alkyl group from O6-alkylguanine in DNA.

  74. Write the functions of any three BER (base excision repair) enzymes.
    Topic: DNA Damage, Mutations & Repair

    In base excision repair (BER): (1) DNA glycosylase recognizes and removes the damaged or altered base, creating an AP site; (2) AP endonuclease cuts the sugar-phosphate backbone at the AP site; (3) DNA polymerase (Pol β in eukaryotes, Pol I in bacteria) fills the gap with the correct nucleotide using the undamaged strand as template, and DNA ligase seals the nick, restoring the DNA.

  75. Write the names of the E. coli mismatch repair proteins, and how the repair process begins.
    Topic: DNA Damage, Mutations & Repair

    The three E. coli proteins are MutS, MutL and MutH. The process of repair begins when MutS (a homodimer or homotetramer) binds to the mismatch. MutS recruits MutL (a homodimer) in an ATP-dependent fashion. Then the MutS–MutL complex activates MutH, which makes an incision at the nearest unmethylated GATC site, either 5 ′or 3′to the mismatch, in the newly synthesized strand.

  76. Write the advancements of molecular biology in agricultural science.
    Topic: Applications & Miscellaneous

    The advancements/applications of molecular biology in agricultural science include: cultivar identification and analysis of seed purity; evaluation of germplasm resources; construction of genetic maps; gene information describing the crop phenology; and prevention and control of agricultural diseases and pests.

  77. Describe the structure of the influenza virus.
    Topic: Applications & Miscellaneous

    The structure of the influenza virus is somewhat variable, but the virion particles are usually spherical or ovoid in shape and 80 to 120 nanometers in diameter. Sometimes filamentous forms of the virus occur as well, and are more common among some influenza strains than others.

  78. What does CRISPR stand for?
    Topic: Applications & Miscellaneous

    CRISPR stands for Clustered Regularly Interspaced Short Palindromic Repeats. These are families of DNA sequences found in the genomes of prokaryotes (bacteria and archaea), consisting of short repeated sequences separated by spacer DNA derived from invading viruses (bacteriophages); together with Cas proteins they form an adaptive immune system against foreign genetic elements.

  79. What are lysosomes?
    Topic: Applications & Miscellaneous

    Lysosomes are membrane-bound organelles found in animal cells that contain digestive (hydrolytic) enzymes such as proteases, lipases and nucleases. They digest worn-out organelles, macromolecules and engulfed particles, and are called the 'suicidal bags' of the cell.

  80. What are the three important discoveries in genetics?
    Topic: Applications & Miscellaneous

    Three important discoveries in genetics are: (1) Mendel's laws of inheritance (1866), which established the particulate nature of heredity; (2) the identification of DNA as the genetic material (Avery, MacLeod and McCarty, 1944; confirmed by Hershey and Chase, 1952); and (3) the double-helix model of DNA structure proposed by Watson and Crick (1953) on the basis of the X-ray diffraction data of Franklin and Wilkins, which explained replication and mutation.
