  1. What are the stages of micropropagation?
    Topic: Plant Tissue Culture & Micropropagation

    • Stage 0: Preparation of source plants. • Stage I: Explant establishment on nutrient medium. • Stage II: Multiplication of shoots via cytokinin-rich media. • Stage III: Rooting in auxin-rich medium. • Stage IV: Acclimatization to soil. (Micropropagation comprises mainly 4 stages — I, II, III and IV — but the initial Step 0 is also necessary.)

  2. What is tissue culture?
    Topic: Plant Tissue Culture & Micropropagation

    Tissue culture is a laboratory technique that involves the propagation of plant cells, tissues, or organs in a controlled environment to produce genetically identical and disease-free plants for various applications. It is in vitro propagation of plants, produces clones or disease-free plants, and is useful for rare or endangered species.

  3. What are the basic steps of tissue culture?
    Topic: Plant Tissue Culture & Micropropagation

    1) Selection of explant 2) Surface sterilization of the explant 3) Establishment of the explant / inoculation on nutrient media 4) Multiplication 5) Callus induction and morphogenesis 6) Root formation 7) Plantlet development and hardening

  4. What are the steps of callus formation in tissue culture?
    Topic: Plant Tissue Culture & Micropropagation

    • Explant inoculation on hormone-rich media. • Callus initiation via auxin/cytokinin balance. • Subculturing for multiplication. • Induction of organogenesis or embryos.

  5. What is the multiplication step of tissue culture?
    Topic: Plant Tissue Culture & Micropropagation

    The multiplication step in tissue culture involves the proliferation and growth of cells, tissues, or organs under controlled conditions to produce an increased number of viable and healthy plantlets.

  6. Write five types (names) of tissue culture in plants.
    Topic: Plant Tissue Culture & Micropropagation

    1) Shoot culture 2) Callus culture 3) Embryo culture 4) Root culture 5) Anther culture

  7. Which media are used in tissue culture?
    Topic: Plant Tissue Culture & Micropropagation

    The important medium used for all-purpose experiments is Murashige and Skoog medium (MS medium). Other commonly used media include Gamborg's B5 medium and Woody Plant Medium (WPM). MS medium contains salts, sugars, vitamins and hormones, and its pH is adjusted to ~5.8.

  8. What is surface sterilization of explants?
    Topic: Plant Tissue Culture & Micropropagation

    Surface sterilization of explants is a critical procedure in tissue culture. It involves removing potential contaminants from the surface of plant material to establish a sterile culture. Typically, the explants are washed with a sterilizing agent like sodium hypochlorite (bleach) or ethanol to eliminate bacteria, fungi, and other microorganisms. The concentration and duration of sterilization depend on the plant species and explant type. Proper surface sterilization ensures aseptic conditions, minimizing the risk of contamination and promoting successful establishment and growth of explants in culture.

  9. Define inoculation.
    Topic: Plant Tissue Culture & Micropropagation

    Inoculation is the process of introducing the explant into the culture medium. It is done in sterile conditions using a laminar flow hood.

  10. Define callus.
    Topic: Plant Tissue Culture & Micropropagation

    The callus is an unorganized mass of undifferentiated tissue. The mechanism of callus formation is that auxin induces cell elongation and cytokinin induces cell division, as a result of which masses of cells are formed.

  11. What is the role of callus?
    Topic: Plant Tissue Culture & Micropropagation

    • Source of totipotent cells. • Used for regeneration and transformation. • Basis for organogenesis and embryogenesis.

  12. What is morphogenesis? Write its types.
    Topic: Plant Tissue Culture & Micropropagation

    Formation of new organs from the callus under the influence of auxin and cytokinin is called morphogenesis; roots and shoots are differentiated from the callus. It is the process of organ development in tissue culture. Its types are: • Organogenesis: Shoots and roots form. • Embryogenesis: Formation of embryos from somatic cells.

  13. Explain indirect organogenesis.
    Topic: Plant Tissue Culture & Micropropagation

    Indirect organogenesis is a process in tissue culture where new plant organs, such as shoots or roots, are regenerated from undifferentiated callus tissue — it involves callus formation first, followed by differentiation into organs (or through intermediate stages such as somatic embryos) before developing into complete plantlets. It is less genetically stable than the direct method, and the indirect organogenesis process is more important for transgenic plant production.

  14. What is somatic embryogenesis?
    Topic: Plant Tissue Culture & Micropropagation

    Somatic embryogenesis is the formation of embryos from somatic cells. It involves callus induction and embryo maturation, and is used in artificial seed production.

  15. What are the advantages of somatic embryogenesis?
    Topic: Plant Tissue Culture & Micropropagation

    • High multiplication rate. • Suitable for artificial seeds. • Can be used for mass propagation.

  16. What is the principle of meristem culture?
    Topic: Plant Tissue Culture & Micropropagation

    • Meristem cells are virus-free. • They are capable of regenerating into whole plants. • Meristem culture is useful in virus elimination.

  17. What are the growth characteristics of cell suspension culture?
    Topic: Plant Tissue Culture & Micropropagation

    Cell suspension culture shows lag, log, and stationary phases, and includes single cells and small aggregates; growth is monitored by weight, cell count, and viability. Other characteristics include rapid cell proliferation, dependency on continuous agitation for uniform nutrient distribution, requirement for a balanced nutrient medium, need for adequate oxygen supply, and the potential for producing valuable secondary metabolites.

  18. What is the mechanism of somaclonal variations?
    Topic: Plant Tissue Culture & Micropropagation

    Genetic (heritable variations): 1) Pre-existing variations in the somatic cells of the explant; 2) Caused by mutations and other DNA changes; 3) Occur at high frequency. Epigenetic (non-heritable variations): 1) Variations generated during tissue culture; 2) Caused by temporary phenotypic changes; 3) Occur at low frequency.

  19. What is the effect of activated charcoal on anther and pollen culture?
    Topic: Plant Tissue Culture & Micropropagation

    The effects of activated charcoal on anther and pollen culture are due to its ability to adsorb inhibitory substances, promote callus induction, enhance shoot regeneration, and provide a suitable microenvironment for growth and development.

  20. What are the applications of embryo culture?
    Topic: Embryo Culture, Protoplasts, Haploids & Synthetic Seeds

    • Overcomes embryo abortion in hybrids. • Helps in overcoming seed dormancy. • Enables production of haploids. • Shortens breeding cycles. • Useful in sterile or non-viable seed germination.

  21. What are the conditions required for embryo culture?
    Topic: Embryo Culture, Protoplasts, Haploids & Synthetic Seeds

    • Sterile, nutrient-rich medium. • Controlled temperature and light. • Supplemented with hormones like auxins and cytokinins. • pH ~5.8; photoperiod 16/8 light/dark cycle.

  22. When is culture of mature embryo necessary?
    Topic: Embryo Culture, Protoplasts, Haploids & Synthetic Seeds

    • When seeds fail to germinate. • In cases of embryo dormancy. • To recover plants from sterile seeds.

  23. What is a protoplast?
    Topic: Embryo Culture, Protoplasts, Haploids & Synthetic Seeds

    A protoplast is a plant cell without a cell wall. Protoplasts are used in fusion, transformation, and culture, and are totipotent for regeneration.

  24. Write two methods of protoplast isolation.
    Topic: Embryo Culture, Protoplasts, Haploids & Synthetic Seeds

    • Mechanical method: Uses osmotic shock and cutting of tissues. • Enzymatic method: Uses cellulase and pectinase to digest cell walls.

  25. What are the optimal conditions for protoplast culture?
    Topic: Embryo Culture, Protoplasts, Haploids & Synthetic Seeds

    • Osmotic balance with mannitol/sorbitol; an osmoprotectant is maintained in the medium. • Optimal culture density. • Optimal auxin to cytokinin ratio (BAP and NAA), glucose and sucrose. • Temperature: 20–28°C. • pH: 5.5–5.9. • 0.25% casein hydrolysate.

  26. What are the advantages of synthetic seeds? Why do we use synthetic seeds?
    Topic: Embryo Culture, Protoplasts, Haploids & Synthetic Seeds

    • Easy storage and handling. • Useful for hybrid and GM propagation. • Bypass seed dormancy issues. • Convenient handling, mass production and disease-free propagation. • Long-term storage, and efficient transfer of desired traits. They offer a practical alternative to conventional seeds and facilitate clonal propagation and preservation of valuable plant genetic resources.

  27. What are the applications of synthetic seeds?
    Topic: Embryo Culture, Protoplasts, Haploids & Synthetic Seeds

    • Germplasm conservation. • Uniform plantlet production. • Used in large-scale propagation.

  28. What are haploid cells?
    Topic: Embryo Culture, Protoplasts, Haploids & Synthetic Seeds

    Haploid cells are cells that contain a single set of chromosomes (n), representing half the normal chromosome complement of a species. They are produced through processes such as meiosis or in vitro techniques (via anther/microspore culture) and are useful in developing homozygous lines; they have unique applications in genetics and plant breeding.

  29. What are the types of growth regulators on the basis of origin?
    Topic: Plant Growth Regulators

    • Natural hormones: Produced by plants (e.g., IAA). • Synthetic hormones: Artificially made (e.g., 2,4-D, NAA). Both control growth, development, and morphogenesis.

  30. Define growth-promoting hormones (plant growth regulators).
    Topic: Plant Growth Regulators

    Growth-promoting hormones, also known as plant growth regulators, are naturally occurring chemical substances that regulate and influence various aspects of plant growth and development. These hormones include auxins, cytokinins and gibberellins. They control processes such as cell division, elongation, differentiation, root and shoot development, flowering, fruiting, and response to environmental cues, playing critical roles in overall plant growth, morphogenesis, and physiological responses.

  31. What is the role of auxin in plants?
    Topic: Plant Growth Regulators

    Auxin plays a crucial role in plants, promoting cell elongation, root development, apical dominance, tropic responses, and differentiation of vascular tissues, as well as influencing fruit development and abscission.

  32. Define genetic markers. Also write their types and the mostly used genetic markers.
    Topic: Genetic & Molecular Markers

    A genetic marker is a gene or DNA sequence with a known location on a chromosome that can be used to identify individuals or species; it identifies specific genes or traits and tracks inheritance patterns, and is used in marker-assisted selection. Types of genetic markers: 1) Morphological markers: traits that are scored visually, whose inheritance can be followed with the naked eye. 2) Biochemical markers: based on proteins like isozymes. 3) Molecular markers: a sequence of DNA or protein that can be screened to reveal genetic variation; molecular markers include RFLP, SSR and RAPD. Mostly used genetic markers: Molecular markers are the most commonly used in agricultural research due to their versatility and precision.

  33. What is a molecular marker?
    Topic: Genetic & Molecular Markers

    A molecular marker is a DNA sequence associated with a trait. It is used for mapping and selection. Examples: SSR, RFLP, AFLP.

  34. What are morphological markers?
    Topic: Genetic & Molecular Markers

    Morphological markers are visible plant traits used for selection. Examples: flower color, leaf shape. They are less precise than molecular markers.

  35. What is a biochemical (molecular) marker?
    Topic: Genetic & Molecular Markers

    A biochemical marker is based on proteins like isozymes. Such markers were used before DNA markers, and they show variation in enzyme mobility.

  36. What are the advantages of biochemical markers?
    Topic: Genetic & Molecular Markers

    1) Simple 2) Inexpensive 3) Electrophoretically resolvable and detectable 4) Does not require DNA extraction or the availability of sequence information, primers or probes 5) Quick and easy to use

  37. What are the applications of DNA-based markers?
    Topic: Genetic & Molecular Markers

    • Genetic diversity analysis. • Marker-assisted breeding and marker-assisted selection. • Gene mapping and identification of desirable traits in crops. In medicine, they assist in diagnosing genetic disorders, determining drug responses, and predicting disease risks. In forensic science, DNA markers aid in criminal investigations and identifying individuals. They are also utilized in evolutionary studies, conservation genetics, studying population dynamics, biodiversity assessment, paternity testing, and tracking the spread of infectious diseases.

  38. How are DNA markers used in crop improvement?
    Topic: Genetic & Molecular Markers

    • Helps in selecting resistant or high-yield traits. • Speeds up plant breeding. • Used in QTL mapping.

  39. What are the uses of DNA markers in agriculture?
    Topic: Genetic & Molecular Markers

    • Track desirable traits. • Identify hybrids or pure lines. • Monitor genetic drift.

  40. What is the principle of electroporation?
    Topic: Gene Transfer & Plant Transformation

    • Electric pulses create temporary membrane pores. • This allows DNA uptake. • It is used in transformation of plant cells.

  41. What are the uses of CaMV?
    Topic: Gene Transfer & Plant Transformation

    • Source of the 35S promoter in plant vectors. • Drives strong gene expression in dicots. • Used in genetic transformation.

  42. How is CaMV used in gene transfer?
    Topic: Gene Transfer & Plant Transformation

    • The 35S promoter ensures constitutive expression. • It is used in GM crops for high expression. • It is effective in dicot transformation.

  43. How is the chloroplast used in gene transfer?
    Topic: Gene Transfer & Plant Transformation

    • Used to express transgenes maternally. • Prevents gene escape via pollen. • Suitable for biopharmaceuticals.

  44. What is plastid transformation?
    Topic: Gene Transfer & Plant Transformation

    Plastid transformation is the stable integration of genes into chloroplast DNA. It gives high expression and maternal inheritance, and is used in biopharmaceuticals and biofortification.

  45. Why are plastids used for transformation?
    Topic: Gene Transfer & Plant Transformation

    Plastids, particularly chloroplasts, are used for transformation in plants because they contain their own DNA and can be genetically modified to express desired traits. This allows for targeted modifications in photosynthesis, metabolic pathways, and other important cellular functions. (Bacteria can take up foreign DNA in a process called transformation, which is a key step in DNA cloning.)

  46. Enlist the methods of chloroplast transmission.
    Topic: Gene Transfer & Plant Transformation

    Chloroplast transmission is mostly maternal inheritance via the ovule, which is used in chloroplast transformation and prevents pollen-mediated gene flow. The methods of chloroplast transmission in plants include maternal inheritance, somatic fusion, genetic engineering, chloroplast transplantation, and grafting, facilitating the transfer and study of chloroplasts between plant cells and tissues.

  47. What is the name of the chloroplast method?
    Topic: Gene Transfer & Plant Transformation

    The name of the method used for isolating chloroplasts from plant cells is known as the "chloroplast isolation" or "chloroplast enrichment" method.

  48. What is crown gall disease?
    Topic: Gene Transfer & Plant Transformation

    Crown gall disease is a plant disease caused by the bacterium Agrobacterium tumefaciens. It induces tumor-like growths (galls) on the crown and roots of infected plants, negatively impacting their growth and productivity. It is used in genetic engineering as a vector.

  49. Name the tumor formed in plants and write its use in plants.
    Topic: Gene Transfer & Plant Transformation

    • Crown gall tumor, caused by Agrobacterium. • The Ti plasmid is used for gene transfer. • Genes are inserted into the T-DNA region.

  50. Write five transformation processes with examples.
    Topic: Gene Transfer & Plant Transformation

    • Agrobacterium-mediated (e.g., tomato). • Particle bombardment (e.g., rice). • Electroporation (e.g., protoplasts). • PEG-mediated (e.g., tobacco). • Microinjection (e.g., zygotes).

  51. Define the transformation process with an example.
    Topic: Gene Transfer & Plant Transformation

    Transformation is the introduction of foreign DNA into a plant cell. Example: the Bt gene introduced into cotton using Agrobacterium. It produces transgenic plants.

  52. By which methods can plant transformation be analyzed?
    Topic: Gene Transfer & Plant Transformation

    • PCR or Southern blotting. • Reporter genes like GUS or GFP. • Antibiotic or herbicide selection.

  53. Why do we need to check colonies in bacterial transformation?
    Topic: Gene Transfer & Plant Transformation

    Colonies are checked to confirm the presence of transformed cells and to identify one with the right plasmid (e.g., by PCR or restriction digest); non-transformed colonies are excluded. The bacteria that make colonies should all contain a plasmid (which provides antibiotic resistance); however, it is not necessarily the case that all of the plasmid-containing colonies will have the same plasmid, so several colonies are checked.

  54. What are the limitations of the particle bombardment method?
    Topic: Gene Transfer & Plant Transformation

    • Random integration of the transgene; insertion of multiple copies of the gene; integration of rearranged and/or truncated DNA sequences. • Lower efficiency in monocots; limited regeneration capacity of the tissue being bombarded; efficiency of stable integration of DNA. • Damage to the cellular tissue. • Specialized and expensive equipment is required.

  55. What are the disadvantages of the particle bombardment system?
    Topic: Gene Transfer & Plant Transformation

    • Tissue damage from high-velocity particles. • Low transformation efficiency. • Random gene integration and potential for DNA damage. • Limited target cell availability. • Requires expensive equipment; high cost and technical complexity.

  56. What are the limitations of PEG (polyethylene glycol)?
    Topic: Gene Transfer & Plant Transformation

    1) The DNA is susceptible to degradation and rearrangement. 2) Random integration of foreign DNA into the genome may result in undesirable traits. 3) Regeneration of plants from transformed protoplasts is a difficult task.

  57. What are the disadvantages of PEG (polyethylene glycol)?
    Topic: Gene Transfer & Plant Transformation

    • Toxic at high concentrations. • Low transformation efficiency. • Difficult to optimize for each species. Other disadvantages include potential interference with cellular processes, limited cell permeability, osmotic stress on cells, and variability in its effectiveness depending on the cell type and experimental conditions.

  58. What are herbicides?
    Topic: Herbicides

    A herbicide is a chemical substance used to control or manipulate undesirable vegetation, especially weeds. Herbicides are extensively used in gardening, farming, and landscape turf management.

  59. What are the uses of herbicides? Why are herbicides used?
    Topic: Herbicides

    • Kill or suppress weeds; efficient weed management. • Reduce crop-weed competition. • Increase crop yield / improve overall yield. • Reduce labor costs. • Clear areas before planting.

  60. What are the modes of action of herbicides?
    Topic: Herbicides

    • Inhibit photosynthesis or amino acid synthesis. • Act as hormone analogs. • Disrupt lipid or DNA synthesis. A herbicide's effectiveness is strongly influenced by its toxic mode of action and the application method. Herbicides can act by inhibiting a plant's amino acid production, growth, photosynthesis or cell division, or by mimicking natural auxin hormones to cause deformities. Most modern herbicides are synthetic mimics of natural plant hormones that obstruct the target plant's growth. Some plants, such as the tree of heaven and juglans (walnuts), produce their own natural herbicide. Organic herbicides are useful in organic gardens, but they are less effective and more costly than synthetic herbicides because they are based on natural materials.

  61. What are the advantages of herbicides?
    Topic: Herbicides

    • Cost-effective and effective weed control; broad spectrum of weeds controlled. • Time-saving and saves labor compared to manual weeding. • Enhances crop productivity. • Reduces competition for nutrients. • Reduced crop injury and reduced herbicide carryover. • New mode of action for resistance management. • Crop management flexibility and simplicity. • Use of herbicides that are more environmentally friendly.

  62. What are the disadvantages of herbicides?
    Topic: Herbicides

    1) Mammalian toxicity 2) Eco-toxicity 3) Weeds become super weeds 4) Reduced crop yield 5) Creates soil and air pollution; herbicides also damage the crop plants along with the weeds.

  63. How are herbicide-resistant crops produced?
    Topic: Herbicides

    • Insertion of herbicide resistance genes (e.g., EPSPS). • Selection of naturally resistant variants. • Use of recombinant DNA technology.

  64. Write six symptoms of herbicide poisoning.
    Topic: Herbicides

    • Nausea and vomiting. • Breathing difficulty. • Dizziness or confusion. • Skin or eye irritation. • Convulsions. • Headache.

  65. Define pesticides.
    Topic: Pesticides

    Pesticides are chemical substances used to control or eliminate pests, such as insects, weeds, and fungi, in order to protect crops, livestock, and public health from their detrimental effects. They include herbicides, insecticides and fungicides, and can harm the environment and health.

  66. What are the effects of pesticides on human health?
    Topic: Pesticides

    Experts broadly classify the effects of pesticides as topical or systemic. Topical reactions are usually limited to areas of the body that have come in direct contact with a pesticide; inflammation of the skin (dermatitis), such as a rash or blisters, is usually the most common topical symptom. Pesticides can have adverse effects on human health, including acute poisoning (acute toxicity in humans and animals), chronic exposure leading to long-term health issues, developmental and reproductive problems, neurological disorders, and an increased risk of certain cancers.

  67. What are the environmental effects of pesticides?
    Topic: Pesticides

    • Contaminates soil and water; soil degradation and air pollution. • Kills non-target organisms; ecotoxicity and disruption of natural ecosystems. • Leads to resistance and bioaccumulation. • Affects pollinators, beneficial insects, birds, and aquatic organisms and food chains.

  68. What are the drawbacks of pesticides?
    Topic: Pesticides

    • Development of resistance in pests. • Environmental pollution. • Harm to non-target organisms. • Health hazards in humans.

  69. What are the short-term effects of pesticides?
    Topic: Pesticides

    • Nausea and vomiting. • Dizziness; headaches or fatigue. • Eye or skin irritation; coughing and sore throat. • Respiratory distress. • Allergic reactions. • Diarrhea, loss of consciousness, seizures and extreme weakness.

  70. How do pesticides affect kids more than adults?
    Topic: Pesticides

    Kids' bodies are smaller and still growing, they take more breaths per minute, and they also eat and drink more relative to their weight. They can easily become exposed to pesticides (via inhalation or skin contact) in schools, daycare, playgrounds and hospitals. Their little kidneys and liver also cannot eliminate pesticides from their bloodstream as effectively as an adult's — all factors that make them more likely to absorb pesticides and residue.

  71. What are the steps involved in pesticide use?
    Topic: Pesticides

    The steps involved in pesticide use typically include: 1) identifying the pest problem, 2) selecting appropriate pesticides, 3) applying them according to label instructions, 4) monitoring effectiveness, and 5) taking necessary safety precautions to minimize environmental and human health impacts.

  72. What is the treatment if pesticides are inhaled?
    Topic: Pesticides

    • Move to fresh air immediately. • Seek medical attention. • Administer oxygen or antidote as needed.

  73. What are the symptoms of peripheral neuropathy?
    Topic: Pesticides

    • Tingling or numbness. • Muscle weakness; loss of coordination. • Burning or stabbing pain. • Sensitivity to touch or temperature changes in the affected areas.

  74. What is the mechanism of resistance in plants?
    Topic: Plant Breeding, Resistance & Miscellaneous

    • Structural barriers (e.g., waxy cuticle). • Production of defensive proteins, enzymes or toxic compounds (like alkaloids or terpenes) that deter pests or inhibit pathogen growth. • Expression of resistance genes (R genes); activation of defense responses, such as the production of antimicrobial proteins or phytohormones, to fight off invaders. • Antioxidant activity to counter toxins; modification of metabolic pathways to enhance resistance against specific threats.

  75. What are pre-zygotic barriers? How can they be overcome?
    Topic: Plant Breeding, Resistance & Miscellaneous

    Pre-zygotic barriers prevent fertilization from occurring. They include pollen incompatibility, pollen tube failure or arrest, and different flowering times or floral structures; in agriculture they also include incompatible pollinators or physical barriers. They can be overcome by: 1) In-vitro fertilization; 2) Protoplast fusion; 3) Embryo culture; 4) Use of growth hormones (IAA, NAA); 5) Adopting the bridging species technique.

  76. What is germplasm preservation?
    Topic: Plant Breeding, Resistance & Miscellaneous

    Germplasm is living tissue from which new plants can be grown — it can be a seed or another plant part, a leaf, a piece of stem, pollen, or even just a few cells that can be turned into a whole plant. Germplasm preservation refers to the collection, storage, and maintenance of plant or animal genetic resources to ensure their long-term availability for future use in breeding, research, and conservation purposes. Biochemically, it involves cryopreservation of tissues and the use of antioxidants and cryoprotectants, and it maintains genetic diversity.

  77. What is the process of plant development?
    Topic: Plant Breeding, Resistance & Miscellaneous

    The process of plant development involves cell division → differentiation → organogenesis, influenced by hormones (auxins, cytokinins) and requiring light, nutrients, and proper conditions. In the plant life cycle it includes germination of seeds, root and shoot formation, leaf growth, flower initiation, pollination, fertilization, fruit development, and ultimately seed dispersal, completing the life cycle.

  78. What do you know about flowering time?
    Topic: Plant Breeding, Resistance & Miscellaneous

    • Controlled by photoperiod and temperature. • Involves genes like FT and CO. • A key trait in breeding programs.

  79. What is a nullisomic?
    Topic: Plant Breeding, Resistance & Miscellaneous

    A nullisomic is an organism missing both copies of a chromosome (2n-2). Nullisomics are commonly studied in wheat genetics and aid in chromosomal mapping.

  80. What are multipartite viruses?
    Topic: Plant Breeding, Resistance & Miscellaneous

    Multipartite viruses are viruses with segmented genomes. Each segment is encapsidated separately, and multiple segments are required to infect.

  81. What are multi-allelic viruses? Give examples.
    Topic: Plant Breeding, Resistance & Miscellaneous

    Multi-allelic viruses include Influenza A virus, Hepatitis C virus, Human immunodeficiency virus (HIV), and Papillomavirus, among others.

  82. Why is lettuce preferred over tobacco for therapeutics?
    Topic: Plant Breeding, Resistance & Miscellaneous

    • Edible and safer for human consumption. • Lower alkaloid content. • Faster growth in controlled conditions.

  83. What is the treatment of skin burns?
    Topic: Plant Breeding, Resistance & Miscellaneous

    Plant extracts like aloe vera are used, and tissue-engineered skin from cultured cells can be applied; biotechnology aids in healing processes. In general, the treatment of skin burns primarily involves immediate first aid measures such as rinsing the affected area with cool water, applying sterile dressings, seeking medical attention, and following appropriate medical treatment protocols for burns.

  84. What are the benefits of agricultural biotechnology?
    Topic: Plant Breeding, Resistance & Miscellaneous

    1. Increased crop yields and productivity. 2. Enhanced resistance to pests, diseases, and environmental stresses. 3. Improved nutritional content of crops. 4. Efficient use of resources, such as water and fertilizers. 5. Reduction in chemical pesticide use. 6. Preservation of biodiversity through disease and pest management. 7. Development of crops with enhanced quality traits. 8. Accelerated breeding processes for crop improvement. 9. Contribution to sustainable agriculture and food security. 10. Potential for addressing global challenges like climate change and population growth.
