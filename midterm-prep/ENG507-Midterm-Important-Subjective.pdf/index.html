  1. Differentiate between phonetics and phonology.
    Topic: Phonetics and Phonology: Basic Concepts

    Phonetics, as a discipline, is the study of human speech sounds. It includes the understanding of how sounds are articulated using the mouth, nose, teeth and tongue, and how the ears hear those sounds and tell them apart. Phonology is the study of the sounds of a particular language (e.g., English). In phonology, it matters whether sounds are contrastive or not, that is, whether substituting one sound for another gives a different, or "contrastive", meaning. Phonetics studies sounds in general, while phonology studies how sounds are organized and patterned in a specific language.

  2. Define a phoneme and describe its characteristics.
    Topic: Phonetics and Phonology: Basic Concepts

    A phoneme is the smallest meaningful unit of sound (therefore, a smallest unit in phonology) in a language, and this meaningful unit of sound is one that will change one word into another word. For example, the difference in 'white' and 'right' (ignore spellings here, focus on sounds) is the difference of sounds (w – r), which are phonemes and have the ability to change meaning. Similarly, take the example of 'cat' vs. 'bat' (k – b). Linguists have also defined a phoneme as a group or class of sound events having common patterns of articulation.

  3. Define an allophone with suitable examples.
    Topic: Phonetics and Phonology: Basic Concepts

    An allophone is a definable systematic variant of a phoneme. Variants do not change meaning; we simply take them as alternate sounds. If a phoneme is a group, then allophones are the group members. Examples are the 's' sound in words like sill, still and spill; the 'k' sound in words like key and car; the 't' sound in words like true and tea; and the 'n' sound in words like tenth and ten.

  4. Name and explain the three branches of phonetics.
    Topic: Phonetics and Phonology: Basic Concepts

    Phonetics has three major branches: (1) Articulatory phonetics, which deals with studying the making of single sounds by the vocal tract — the way in which speech sounds are made ('articulated') by the vocal organs. (2) Acoustic phonetics, which is related to the study of the physical attributes of sounds produced by the vocal tract; it studies the physical properties of speech sound as transmitted between mouth and ear according to the principles of acoustics. (3) Auditory phonetics, which deals with understanding how the human ear perceives sound and how the brain recognizes different speech units; it studies the perceptual response to speech sounds as mediated by the ear, auditory nerve and brain.

  5. What are articulators? Name the principal articulators.
    Topic: Speech Production and Airstream Mechanisms

    We can only produce speech sounds by moving parts of our articulators (body parts), and this is done by the contraction of muscles. Most of the movements relevant to speech take place in the mouth and throat area (though we should not forget the activity in the chest for breath control). The parts of the mouth and throat area that we move when speaking are called articulators. The principal articulators are the tongue, lips, lower jaw and the teeth, velum or soft palate, uvula and larynx. Sounds are described by the specific properties of these articulators, including places and manners of articulation and phonation.

  6. Define the airstream mechanism. Explain its three types.
    Topic: Speech Production and Airstream Mechanisms

    'Airstream' is a term used in phonetics for a physiological process which provides a source of energy capable of being used in speech sound production. There are three mechanisms. (1) The pulmonic airstream mechanism, in which the air is moved inwards or outwards by the lungs; it is used for producing the majority of human speech sounds, and for speaking it is practically always egressive (air pushed out, compressed by the lungs). (2) The glottalic airstream mechanism, which uses the movement of the glottis — the aperture between the vocal folds — as the source of energy. (3) The velaric airstream mechanism, which involves an airflow produced by a movement of the back of the tongue against the velum.

  7. Write a note on the velaric airstream mechanism. What are click sounds?
    Topic: Speech Production and Airstream Mechanisms

    Under the velaric airstream mechanism, speech sounds are made by sucking the air. This sucking mechanism is used first by babies for feeding and by adult humans in later stages of life for such things as sucking liquid through a straw or drawing smoke from a cigarette (using the back of the tongue against the velum). The basic mechanism is the air-tight closure between the back of the tongue and the soft palate; as the tongue is then retracted, the pressure in the oral cavity is lowered and suction takes place. Consonants produced with this mechanism are called clicks. These sounds have a distinctive role in some languages such as Zulu. In English, they may be heard in the 'tut tut' (or tsk tsk) sounds and in a few other contexts.

  8. Write a note on the types of phonation.
    Topic: Speech Production and Airstream Mechanisms

    Phonation types are: (1) Voiceless – when the folds are open apart and the air passes through the glottis freely (e.g., /t/ or /p/). (2) Voiced – when the folds are tight together and there is vibration during the air passage through the glottis (e.g., /b/ or /d/). (3) Creaky voice – when there is a slight opening in the front and the arytenoid cartilages are tight together, so that the vocal folds can vibrate only at the anterior end. (4) Breathy or murmuring sound – when the vocal folds are apart but still they are vibrating; a breathy voice is like a whisper except with voice.

  9. Define Voice Onset Time (VOT). Explain its three types.
    Topic: Speech Production and Airstream Mechanisms

    Voice Onset Time (VOT) is a term used in phonetics referring to the point in time at which vocal fold vibration starts in relation to the release of a closure (during the production of plosive sounds). There are three possibilities: (1) Simple unaspirated voiceless stops have a voice onset time at or near zero — the voicing of a following vowel begins at or near to when the stop is released. (2) In aspirated stops followed by a vowel, voice onset time is greater than zero, called a positive VOT; the length of the VOT is based on the practical measure of aspiration — the longer the VOT, the stronger the aspiration. (3) In voiced stops, the VOT is noticeably less than zero, called a negative VOT; this means that the vocal cords start vibrating before the stop is released.

  10. Define aspiration. In which sounds does it occur in English?
    Topic: Consonants: Classification and Processes

    Aspiration is a puff of noise made when a consonantal constriction is released and air is allowed to escape relatively freely (e.g., in English /p t k/ at the beginning of a syllable are aspirated). Phonetically, aspiration is the result of the vocal cords being widely parted at the time of the articulatory release. In some languages (such as English) aspiration is allophonic, while in others (such as Urdu) it is phonemic.

  11. Write a note on stop consonants. How many stops are there in English?
    Topic: Consonants: Classification and Processes

    'Stop' is often used as if synonymous with plosive. However, some phoneticians use it to refer to the class of sounds in which there is complete closure specifically in the oral cavity. In this case, sounds such as m, n are also stops; more precisely, they are nasal stops. In English, there are nine stops (six oral and three nasal): the oral stops are bilabial p, b; alveolar t, d; and velar k, g, while the nasal stops are m (bilabial), n (alveolar) and ŋ (velar). Apart from these, in some varieties of English the glottal stop /ʔ/ is also found, as in beaten [ˈbɪʔn̩]. The term 'stop' is used in the phonetic classification of consonant sounds on the basis of their manner of articulation.

  12. Define sibilant fricatives. How do sibilants differ from strident fricatives?
    Topic: Consonants: Classification and Processes

    The fricatives [s, z, ʒ, ʃ] are called sibilant sounds. Sibilant fricatives (such as s, ʃ) are strong and clearly audible, whereas strident fricatives are weak and less audible (such as θ, f). A fricative consonant is made by forcing air through a narrow gap so that a hissing noise is generated. BBC pronunciation has nine fricative phonemes: f, θ, s, ʃ, h (voiceless) and v, ð, z, ʒ (voiced). Fricatives are also known as spirants.

  13. What are affricates? Give examples.
    Topic: Consonants: Classification and Processes

    An affricate sound is a type of consonant which is made of a plosive followed by a fricative with the same place of articulation (so, it is a mixture of two steps or gestures). For example, /tʃ/ (the voiceless affricate) has /t/ and /ʃ/ as a sound at the beginning and end of the English word church /tʃɜːtʃ/. The two affricate phonemes of English are [tʃ] and [dʒ], as at the beginning and end of the words 'church' and 'judge'.

  14. Differentiate between tap, flap and trill.
    Topic: Consonants: Classification and Processes

    Tap is the up and down movement of the top of the tip of the tongue. For example, pronouncing the middle sound in the word 'pity' with a typical American accent [ɾ]. It is very brief and is produced by a sharp upward throw of the tongue blade; in this sound, the tongue makes a single tap against the alveolar ridge. Flap is the front and back movement of the tongue tip at the underside of the tongue with curling behind. It is found in abundance in Indo-Aryan (IA) languages [ɽ]; typical flap sounds found in IA languages are retroflex sounds such as [ɽ], [ɖ] and [ɳ]. In the case of tap and flap, there is only one rapid contact. In the production of a trill, the articulator is set in motion by the current of air [r]; it is a typical sound of Scottish English, as in words like 'rye' and 'row'.

  15. Write a brief note on fortis and lenis consonants.
    Topic: Consonants: Classification and Processes

    In linguistics, fortis and lenis, also called tense and lax, refer to consonants pronounced with greater and lesser energy. Fortis refers to a sound made with a relatively strong degree of muscular effort and breath force compared with the other sound (known as lenis). English has fortis consonants, such as the p in pat, with a corresponding lenis consonant, such as the b in bat. Fortis and lenis consonants may be distinguished by tenseness or other characteristics, such as voicing, aspiration, glottalization, velarization, length, and length of nearby vowels. These are the terms used in the phonetic classification of consonantal sounds on the basis of their manner of articulation. The distinction between tense and lax is used for vowels on similar lines.

  16. Define nasalization. How does a nasal sound differ from a nasalized sound?
    Topic: Consonants: Classification and Processes

    Nasalization is an articulatory process whereby a sound is made 'nasal' (when the air is passing through the nasal cavity) due to its adjacent nasal sound; it is an articulatory influence of an adjacent nasal consonant, as in words like mat or hand. A vowel can also be nasalised in words like man (when /a/ may be articulated with the soft palate lowered throughout), because of the nasal consonants' influence (this is called anticipatory coarticulation). There is a difference between a 'nasal' and a 'nasalised' sound: a sound is nasalized when the nasality comes from other sounds, whereas the 'nasal' term suggests that the nasality is an essential identifying feature of a sound.

  17. Define velarisation with examples.
    Topic: Consonants: Classification and Processes

    In co-articulation, velarisation is a process whereby a constriction in the vocal tract is added to the primary constriction which gives a consonant its place of articulation. More specifically, velarisation is an example of secondary articulation. In the case of English "dark /l/", the /l/ phoneme is produced with its usual primary constriction in the alveolar region; it is like the back of the tongue is raised for an /u/ vowel sound, creating a secondary (articulation) constriction. More examples are life vs. file (/laɪf/ /faɪl̴/) and clap vs. talc (/klæp/ /tæl̴k/).

  18. How many vowels and consonants are there in English (RP)? Give the breakdown.
    Topic: Vowels and Diphthongs

    There are 44 sounds in the English RP (BBC) accent. Out of them, 20 are vowels, which are further divided into pure vowels and diphthongs. Pure vowels or monophthongs are 12, out of which 5 are long and 7 are short vowels. The remaining 24 sounds are consonants: 6 plosives, 3 nasals, 9 fricatives, 2 affricates and 4 approximants.

  19. Define a diphthong. List the diphthongs of BBC English with examples.
    Topic: Vowels and Diphthongs

    A diphthong is a single vowel consisting of the features of two vowels. Its most important feature is the glide from one vowel quality to another (so basically it is a glide). The BBC accent of English contains a large number (eight in total) of diphthongs, including three ending at /ɪ/ (eɪ, aɪ, ɔɪ – as in words bay, buy and boy), two ending at /ʊ/ (əʊ, aʊ – as in words no and now) and three ending at /ə/ (ɪə, eə, ʊə – as in words peer, pair and poor). English diphthongs are divided into two categories: centering (which end with the /ə/ sound) and closing (which end with either /ɪ/ or /ʊ/ sounds).

  20. Write a note on lax vowels. How do they differ from tense vowels?
    Topic: Vowels and Diphthongs

    Lax vowels are short. Lax sounds are produced with less muscular effort and movement, and are relatively short and indistinct vowel sounds (e.g., ɪ, e, ɒ, æ, ʌ, ʊ, ə — vowels articulated near the center of the vowel area) compared to tense sounds (e.g., uː, iː, ɜː, ɑː). In other words, a lax vowel is the one produced with relatively little articulatory energy, while tense vowels are produced with greater muscular effort.

  21. Define the relation of F1 and F2 in vowel sounds.
    Topic: Vowels and Diphthongs

    Acoustically, vowels are mainly distinguished by the first two formant frequencies F1 and F2. F1 is inversely related to the vowel height (which means that smaller F1 amplitude = higher vowels), and F2 is related to the front or back of the vowels (smaller F2 amplitude = more back vowels). On a spectrogram, the higher overtone F2 tells us about the position of the tongue, while the lower overtone F1 tells us about the level of the tongue (high/low).

  22. Describe the different forms of a vowel.
    Topic: Vowels and Diphthongs

    A vowel may take one out of three forms: stressed, unstressed and reduced. Most of the time a vowel is completely pronounced when it is in a stressed syllable, but the same vowel is different in quality (allophonic form) when it takes place in an unstressed syllable, and, of course, it is reduced to another form when it is in a reduced syllable. In most cases, various reduced vowels take the shape of the schwa vowel /ə/. The symbol /ə/ may be used to show many types of vowels with a central, reduced vowel quality. A vowel in an unstressed syllable does not necessarily have a completely reduced quality.

  23. What is phonetic transcription? Differentiate between broad (phonemic) and narrow transcription.
    Topic: Transcription and the IPA

    Transcription is an important tool in phonetics and phonology. Based on a specific set of symbols, transcription is the writing down of a spoken utterance; the implied word is converted from one representation (e.g., written text) into another (e.g., phonetic symbols). The most fundamental division is between phonemic (broad) and phonetic (narrow) transcription. In phonemic transcription, the only symbols that may be used are those which represent one of the phonemes of the language, and extra symbols are excluded; another name for phonemic transcription is broad transcription. In a phonetic transcription, the transcriber may use the full range of phonetic symbols if these are required; a narrow phonetic transcription is one which carries a lot of fine detail about the precise phonetic quality of sounds, while a broad phonetic transcription gives a more limited amount of phonetic information. In narrow transcription, the sounds are symbolized on the basis of their articulatory/auditory identity, regardless of their function in a language. Transcription is a useful technique for highlighting the phonemes of a language during documentation and description.

  24. Write a note on diacritics.
    Topic: Transcription and the IPA

    While transcribing (accurately and in detail), a small mark is added to a phonetic symbol to show the way it is spoken. Diacritics include various marks such as accent marks, the signs of devoicing and nasalization. The diacritic marks may be placed over a symbol, under it, before it, after it, or through it. The International Phonetic Association (IPA) recognizes a wide range of such marks (diacritics) for both vowels and consonants. In the case of vowels, diacritics indicate differences in frontness, backness, closeness or openness, and lip-rounding or unrounding, nasalization and centralization. In the case of consonants, diacritics are used for voicing or voicelessness, for advanced or retracted place of articulation, aspiration and many other aspects. Important diacritics include: voiceless (small circle below, e.g., /kw̥ɪk/), aspirated (small /h/ above, e.g., /kʰɪs/), dental (sign below, e.g., /həl̪θ/), nasalized (tilde above, e.g., /mæ̃n/), velarized (tilde through, e.g., /pʰɪl̴/), and syllabic (small vertical line below, e.g., /mɪʔn̩/).

  25. What is 'The North Wind and the Sun' story? Why is it used in phonetics?
    Topic: Transcription and the IPA

    The IPA provides various tools for the phonetic study of human languages. One such resource is 'The North Wind and the Sun' story (taken from Arne Thompson). In order to create a uniform system for the description of the sounds of languages, this is a recommended text for transcription (both narrow and broad), especially for publishing the IPA illustrations of languages in the IPA journal. It is a short folktale in which the North Wind and the Sun dispute which of them is the stronger; they agree that whoever first makes a traveler take off his cloak is stronger. The Wind blows as hard as it can, but the traveler only folds his cloak more closely; then the Sun shines warmly and the traveler immediately takes off his cloak, so the North Wind has to confess that the Sun is the stronger of the two.

  26. Define stress. Differentiate between lexical (word) stress and sentence stress.
    Topic: Stress, Tone, Intonation and Rhythm

    In phonetics, stress refers to the degree of force used in producing a syllable. The usual distinction is between stressed and unstressed syllables, the former being more prominent than the latter (and marked in transcription with a raised vertical line [ˈ]). Lexical stress is basically related to the primary stress applied at syllable level (when only one syllable is stressed) that has the ability to change the meaning and the grammatical category of a word, as in the case of 'IMport' (noun) and 'imPORT' (verb). On the other hand, stress on a word or certain words in a phrase or sentence is called sentence level or prosodic stress; this is a change or modification to word level stress in a sentence, basically a change of 'beat' on certain words in a sentence. We create 'rhythm' in spoken language on the basis of stress.

  27. Describe the degrees of stress and how they are marked.
    Topic: Stress, Tone, Intonation and Rhythm

    In the American structuralist tradition, four degrees of stress are usually distinguished and analyzed as stress phonemes, namely (from strongest to weakest): (1) primary, (2) secondary, (3) tertiary and (4) weak. These contrasts are, however, demonstrable only on words in isolation, as in the compound 'elevator operator'. In phonological analysis, most experts only distinguish among three degrees of stress, namely primary, secondary and weak or unstressed (e.g., /ɪɡ.ˌzæm.ɪ.ˈneɪ.ʃən/). Primary stress is marked with a raised vertical line [ˈ] before the syllable, and secondary stress with a lowered vertical line [ˌ] before the syllable.

  28. What are suprasegmental features? List the commonly mentioned ones.
    Topic: Stress, Tone, Intonation and Rhythm

    'Supra' means above (beyond) and 'segments' means sounds (such as vowels and consonants), so the term 'suprasegmental' means 'above sounds'. The term suprasegmental was initially invented to refer to aspects of sound such as intonation that did not seem to be the properties of individual segments (i.e., the vowels and consonants of which speech is composed). There has never been full agreement about how many suprasegmental features are to be found in speech, but pitch, loudness, tempo, juncture, syllable, rhythm and stress are the most commonly mentioned ones. The short symbols which are used for indicating suprasegmental features are called diacritics.

  29. Define intonation and explain its function.
    Topic: Stress, Tone, Intonation and Rhythm

    Intonation refers (very) simply to the variations in the pitch of a speaker's voice (f0). In order to describe intonation, we need to analyze the role of a 'stressed syllable'. In many languages, including English, intonation serves a grammatical function, distinguishing one type of phrase or sentence from another. Thus, "Your name is John," beginning with a medium pitch and ending with a lower one (falling intonation), is a simple assertion; "Your name is John?", with a rising intonation (high final pitch), indicates a question. One method of describing intonation is the 'tone and break indices' (ToBI) method, in which H and L represent High and Low pitches, showing pitch accent, phrase accent and boundary.

  30. Define rhythm. What is the stress-timed rhythm hypothesis?
    Topic: Stress, Tone, Intonation and Rhythm

    Speech is perceived as a sequence of events in time, and the word rhythm is used to refer to the way events are distributed in time (a noticeable event occurring at regular intervals). An obvious example of vocal rhythm is chanting as part of games (for example, children calling words while skipping, or cricket crowds calling their favorite team's name). In conversational speech, the sentence rhythm is a bit complicated, but it is clear that the timing of speech is not random. An extreme view (though a quite common one) is that English speech has a rhythm that allows us to divide it up into more or less equal intervals of time called feet, each of which begins with a stressed syllable: this is called the stress-timed rhythm hypothesis, and languages are divided on this basis into stress-timed and syllable-timed languages. English is a stress-timed language.

  31. Write a note on tone languages.
    Topic: Stress, Tone, Intonation and Rhythm

    In the case of tone languages, it is usual to identify tones as being a property of individual syllables, whereas an intonational tone may be spread over many syllables. In typical 'tone' languages, the linguistic function of tone is to change the meaning of a word. For example, in Mandarin (Chinese), /má/ said with high pitch means 'mother', while /mǎ/ spoken on a low rising tone means 'hemp'. In the analysis of English intonation, tone refers to one of the pitch possibilities for the tonic (or nuclear) syllable, a set usually including fall, rise, fall–rise and rise–fall. The three variables of pitch are range, height and direction.

  32. What is connected speech? List its features.
    Topic: Connected Speech and Accent Variation

    Connected speech is spoken language that is used in a continuous sequence, as in normal conversations; it is also called connected discourse. There is often a significant difference between the way words are pronounced in isolation and the way they are pronounced in the context of connected speech. For example, words or syllables can be clipped or run together, or their stresses can change. The features of connected speech are: assimilation, rhythm, stress, elision, linking, tone and intonation. Sets of phonemes and sound patterns are dynamic in connected speech, whereas they are static in isolation within a human language.

  33. Define a rhotic accent.
    Topic: Connected Speech and Accent Variation

    A rhotic speaker pronounces the letter "R" in words like hard and water. In other words, rhotic speakers pronounce /r/ in all positions. The term 'rhotic' is used to describe some varieties of English (e.g., American) pronunciation in which the /r/ phoneme is found in all its phonological contexts, unlike non-rhotic accents (such as RP), where /r/ is pronounced only before a vowel.

  34. Describe the four types of accent variation.
    Topic: Connected Speech and Accent Variation

    Accent differences must be realized while transcribing for a specific accent (e.g., British vs. US accent), as one language can have many possible transcriptions. There are four types of variations among various accents of a language: (a) Difference in phonological inventories, e.g., compare /strʌt/ vs. /strʊt/. (b) Difference in phonetic features, such as pronouncing /t/ as [ʔ]. (c) Phonological distribution, e.g., rhotic vs. non-rhotic accents. (d) Lexical distribution, e.g., /θ/ and /ð/ differences (as England and Wales have /ð/ and Scottish accents have /θ/).

  35. What are homographs? Explain with examples.
    Topic: Connected Speech and Accent Variation

    It is a group (usually a pair) of words that are spelled the same way, and may or may not be pronounced the same way, although the difference in pronunciation is often just a shift in the accented syllable. There is a whole class of homographs that end in -ate, usually with one being a verb and the other being a noun or an adjective related to it. For example, "advocate" can be pronounced with a long "a" sound and mean "to speak or write in support of"; "advocate" can also be pronounced with a short "a" sound and refer to a person who supports or pleads the cause of another.
