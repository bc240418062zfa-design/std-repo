  1. Define literature. Why do we study literature?
    Topic: Introduction to Literature

    Literature means written works — fiction, poetry, drama and criticism — that are recognized as having important or permanent artistic value; in a broader sense it is the body of written works of a culture, language, people or period. Ezra Pound calls great literature "language charged with meaning to the utmost possible degree." We study literature because it reflects human ideas, beliefs and societies; it acquaints us with the human psyche and with perspectives separated from us by time and social barriers; it cultivates wisdom by showing us good and evil and the injustices of the world; it gives us a mature worldview; and it entertains us while introducing us to literary figures, movements and memorable characters.

  2. What is the role of language in literature? Can literature exist without language?
    Topic: Introduction to Literature

    Language is the medium through which all feelings, emotions and opinions in a piece of literature are transferred to the reader; literature cannot be segregated from language. In the creation of a work of art three things count: the writer, the reader and language. Writers play with language to create unusual new meanings, and imagery created through language breaks the monotony of a text and spares the writer long, boring descriptions. A separate branch of linguistics, stylistics, studies the relationship of language and literature and explains the means by which language becomes a work of art. Hence literature cannot exist without language, because language is the only widely accepted form of communication through which a literary work reaches its readers.

  3. What is a short story? How is it different from a novel?
    Topic: Introduction to the Short Story

    A short story is a brief fictional work of prose, shorter in length than a novel. In contemporary fiction it ranges from about 1,000 to 20,000 words, and according to Poe it should be read in one sitting, anywhere from half an hour to two hours. It usually focuses on one incident, has a single plot, a single setting, a small number of characters and covers a short period of time. It differs from the novel in that it has a simpler plot and setting; instead of developing character through many incidents, it tends to reveal character at one crucial moment; and every word in it must contribute to a single preconceived effect, whereas a novel may ramble and develop its themes at length.

  4. What are the key elements (components) of a short story? Discuss briefly.
    Topic: Introduction to the Short Story

    The basic components of a short story are setting, plot, conflict, character, point of view and theme. Setting is the time and place of the action, including weather, social conditions and mood. Plot is the planned sequence of events — introduction, rising action, climax, falling action and denouement. Conflict is the opposition of forces (external or internal) without which there is no plot. Character covers the persons of the story, chiefly the protagonist and the antagonist, revealed through appearance, speech, thoughts and actions. Point of view is the angle from which the story is told, such as first person or omniscient. Theme is the controlling idea or central insight of the story. A successful short story also shows integrity, economy, an epiphany (a moment of revelation or change) and an ending that gives a feeling of closure.

  5. What role does conflict play in weaving the plot of a short story?
    Topic: Introduction to the Short Story

    Conflict is essential to plot; without conflict there is no plot at all. It is the opposition of forces which ties one incident to another and makes the plot move forward. The rising action consists of the complications produced by the conflict, the climax is the point where the conflict reaches its highest intensity, and the falling action and denouement resolve it. Conflict may be external — a struggle with a force outside one's self — or internal — a struggle within one's self. Its four dimensions are Man vs. Man, Man vs. Circumstances, Man vs. Society and Man vs. Himself. A short story usually has one central struggle, or one dominant struggle with many minor ones growing out of it.

  6. Why did Della sell her hair and Jim sell his gold watch in "The Gift of the Magi"? What is the irony of the story?
    Topic: O. Henry — The Gift of the Magi

    Della had saved only one dollar and eighty-seven cents to buy Jim a Christmas present worthy of him. Her one treasure was her beautiful long hair, so she sold it for twenty dollars and bought Jim a platinum fob chain for the gold watch he was so proud of. Unknown to her, Jim had sold that very gold watch to buy Della the set of combs she had long worshipped for her beautiful hair. The irony is that each sacrificed his or her greatest treasure to buy a gift that the other's sacrifice has made useless: the chain has no watch, and the combs have no hair. Yet the narrator concludes that of all who give and receive gifts, these two foolish children are the wisest — they are the magi — because the true gift was their selfless love.

  7. What was Mr. Behrman's masterpiece in "The Last Leaf"? Was the last leaf real?
    Topic: O. Henry — The Last Leaf

    Mr. Behrman was an old, unsuccessful painter who had always talked of painting a masterpiece one day. When Johnsy, ill with pneumonia, became convinced that she would die when the last leaf fell from the ivy vine outside her window, Behrman went out on the stormy night and painted a leaf on the brick wall so perfectly that it looked real and never fluttered or moved. The last leaf was not real — it was Behrman's painting. Seeing it still clinging to the vine, Johnsy regained her will to live and recovered; but Behrman caught pneumonia from the cold, wet night and died. The painted leaf was therefore truly his masterpiece: it saved a life at the cost of his own, and the story becomes a tale of hope, courage and selfless sacrifice.

  8. Why does the Happy Prince weep, and how does he help the poor of his city?
    Topic: Oscar Wilde — The Happy Prince

    When alive, the Prince lived in the Palace of Sans-Souci, where sorrow was not allowed to enter, so he never knew what tears were. After his death his statue is set up high above the city, from where he can see all the ugliness and misery of his people, and though his heart is made of lead he cannot choose but weep. With the help of the little Swallow, who stays behind to be his messenger, he gives away everything he has: the ruby from his sword-hilt goes to the poor seamstress whose little boy is ill with fever; one sapphire eye to the young playwright who is too cold and hungry to finish his play; the other sapphire eye to the little match-girl; and finally the fine gold leaves covering his body, leaf by leaf, to the poor and the starving children of the city.

  9. How did the Nightingale build a red rose out of music in "The Nightingale and the Rose"? What moral lesson do you get from the story?
    Topic: Oscar Wilde — The Nightingale and the Rose

    The Rose-tree told the Nightingale that a red rose could be built only out of music by moonlight and stained with her own heart's-blood: she must sing all night with her breast pressed against a thorn, so that the thorn pierces her heart and her life-blood flows into the veins of the tree. Believing that Love is better than Life, the Nightingale sings all night — of the birth of love, of passion, and of the love that is perfected by death — and as the rose turns from pale to pink to crimson, she dies. The moral lesson is that true love is selfless sacrifice, while human beings are often selfish and ungrateful: the Student, who understood nothing of her song, throws the rose into the gutter when the Professor's daughter prefers jewels, and returns to his books, calling love impractical.

  10. Describe the major theme of "The Necklace". What is meant by "irony of fate" in the story?
    Topic: Guy de Maupassant — The Necklace

    The major theme of "The Necklace" is the hollowness of appearances. Mathilde, dissatisfied with her modest life, longs to appear rich and admired; the borrowed necklace that seems to be made of diamonds is actually an imitation, just as her one night of glamour is an unreal guise — and even Madame Forestier, the embodiment of wealth in Mathilde's eyes, owns only fake diamonds. Irony of fate means the cruel twist by which events turn against a person's expectations. Mathilde loses the necklace, and she and her husband ruin ten years of their lives — she loses even her beauty, her only true possession — to replace it with a real necklace worth 36,000 francs, only to learn at the end that the original was paste, worth at most five hundred francs. In trying to appear more than she was, she loses what she genuinely had.

  11. Compare and contrast the life of Mathilde before and after the loss of the necklace.
    Topic: Guy de Maupassant — The Necklace

    Before the loss, Mathilde is a pretty, charming but deeply dissatisfied young woman who suffers ceaselessly over the poverty of her dwelling, dresses plainly yet dreams of luxuries, and values herself only through appearances; she is a round and dynamic character drawn in depth. After the loss, she dismisses the maid, moves to a garret, does the heavy housework herself, haggles over every sou and works for ten years alongside her husband to repay the debt of 36,000 francs. She loses her beauty and charm and becomes a strong, hard-working, genuine woman who endures hardship with grace for the honour of her family name. Just as the fake necklace is replaced by a real one, the superficial Mathilde is replaced by a real, stronger human being.

  12. What are the important themes of "The Devil"? What is the significance of its title?
    Topic: Guy de Maupassant — The Devil

    The important themes of "The Devil" are greed, selfishness, impatience, death and the worldly cares that show no compassion even for the dying. Honore Bontemps cannot sit beside his dying ninety-two-year-old mother because he must get in his wheat, and La Rapet, hired for a fixed sum of six francs, wants the old woman to die quickly so that her bargain remains profitable. The title is significant because the "devil" of the story is not a supernatural being but the greed within human beings: La Rapet dresses up as the Devil — with a sheet, a saucepan on her head and a broom in her hand — and frightens the old woman to death. The story is a commentary on how money can become more important than human relations, and on the helplessness of a dying person.

  13. What is the basic motive behind the narrator's intention to kill the old man in "The Tell-Tale Heart"? What does the sound of the heartbeat symbolize?
    Topic: Edgar Allan Poe — The Tell-Tale Heart

    The narrator himself insists there was no ordinary motive: he loved the old man, who had never wronged or insulted him, and he had no desire for his gold. His motive was the old man's eye — a pale blue "vulture eye" with a film over it — which made his blood run cold whenever it fell upon him; he resolved to take the old man's life and so rid himself of the eye forever. This motiveless obsession shows the narrator's madness, which he denies throughout. The sound of the heartbeat that he believes he hears after the murder — growing louder and louder until he confesses — symbolizes his guilty conscience: the heart that will not stay buried is his own sense of guilt, and it proves the truth that no sin goes unpunished.

  14. What is the major theme of "Cannibalism in the Cars"? Does cannibalism actually take place in the story?
    Topic: Mark Twain — Cannibalism in the Cars

    The major themes of the story are the corruption and selfishness widespread in the politics of Twain's time. The snowbound passengers, facing starvation, hold a mock congressional session — with motions, nominations and votes conducted in full parliamentary form — to choose who shall be eaten, and they describe their fellow passengers as stringy, tough or tender like items on a menu. Through this horrible comedy Twain ridicules the congressmen and politicians who, by unfair means and unfair taxation, were metaphorically eating human flesh and taking pleasure in it. In fact the cannibalism is only a tale within a tale: at the end the narrator learns that his companion is a former Congressman who has lost his reason after his terrible experience, so the gruesome feast is the product of the madman's imagination, told deadpan as sober fact — which makes the satire sharper.

  15. "A Dog's Tale" is a comic story with a serious underlying message. Comment.
    Topic: Mark Twain — A Dog's Tale

    "A Dog's Tale" is narrated by a dog whose father was a St. Bernard and whose mother was a collie — a mother fond of fine large words she does not understand, through whom Twain satirizes the pretentious manners of the ladies of his time. The surface of the tale is comic: the dog innocently misunderstands human words and customs, and repeats learned phrases without grasping them. Beneath the comedy lies a serious message. The dog saves the baby from a fire and is beaten by the master who misunderstands her action; later her own puppy is killed in a cruel scientific experiment performed by Mr. Grey and his friends to settle a point of knowledge. The story thus exposes human selfishness, cruelty and ingratitude towards faithful animals, and shows that dumb creatures possess love, loyalty and feeling which "superior" human beings often lack.

  16. Write a plot summary of "The Garden Party" and describe its central theme.
    Topic: Katherine Mansfield — The Garden Party

    On a perfect summer day the wealthy Sheridan family prepares a garden party. Laura, the daughter put in charge of the arrangements, learns that a poor carter, Scott, who lives in the lane below, has been killed in an accident. She wants to stop the party out of sympathy, but her mother and sisters dismiss the idea, and her mother distracts her with a beautiful black hat. The party is a great success; afterwards Laura is sent with a basket of leftovers to the dead man's cottage. In the dark little room she sees the dead carter lying peaceful and beautiful, is overwhelmed, and hurries out; meeting her brother Laurie she stammers, "Isn't life — " but cannot finish, and he answers, "Isn't it, darling?" The central theme is the contrast between life and death — the bright, careless world of the rich against the dark world of the poor — together with class consciousness and Laura's growth into emotional and moral maturity through her moment of epiphany before the corpse.

  17. Describe the major themes of "Miss Brill". In what way is Miss Brill's fur analogous to her?
    Topic: Katherine Mansfield — Miss Brill

    The major themes of "Miss Brill" are loneliness, illusion versus reality, and the cruelty of the world towards the old and insignificant. Miss Brill, an elderly, lonely woman, spends her Sundays in the Jardins Publiques imagining that she and everyone around her are actors in a play, and that she herself has an important part in it. Her fur is analogous to her: it is old, faded and a little damaged, kept shut in a dark box all week and taken out only on Sundays — just as Miss Brill herself is brought out of her dark little room for these weekly outings; she talks to it and treats it as a companion ("Little rogue!") because it shares her loneliness. At the end, when the young couple mock her as "that stupid old thing", she puts the fur back into its box without looking at it and thinks she hears something crying — the fur, and her own heart, weeping at the collapse of her illusion.

  18. Define poetry, giving at least two definitions of poetry by famous poets.
    Topic: Introduction to Poetry

    Poetry is a literary art in which language is used for its aesthetic qualities; it suggests alternative meanings in words and arouses emotional and sensuous responses through devices such as rhythm, rhyme, alliteration, assonance, symbolism and irony. Wordsworth defines poetry as "the spontaneous overflow of powerful feelings" (Preface to the Lyrical Ballads). Matthew Arnold says, "Poetry is at bottom a criticism of life." Coleridge calls poetry "the best words in their best order," Sir Philip Sidney calls it "a speaking picture, with this end: to teach and delight," and Keats writes, "If poetry comes not as naturally as the leaves to a tree, it had better not come at all."

  19. What is epic poetry? Give examples. Is rhyming scheme necessary for all types of poetry?
    Topic: Introduction to Poetry

    Epic poetry is a major form of narrative literature which recounts, in a continuous manner, the life and works of a heroic or mythological person or group of persons. Notable examples are Homer's Iliad and Odyssey, Virgil's Aeneid, the Mahabharata, Valmiki's Ramayana and Milton's Paradise Lost. As for rhyme scheme: a rhyme scheme is a regular pattern of rhyme consistent throughout a poem, but it is not necessary for all types of poetry. Many forms — free verse, prose poetry and several fixed forms such as the sestina and tanka — are unrhymed, and even rhymed poetry may use irregular patterns. Rhyme is an ornamental and musical device that aids memory and pleasure, but poetry is defined by its imaginative use of language, rhythm and feeling, not by rhyme alone.

  20. How does the chimney-sweeper's cry "blacken" the Church in Blake's "London"? What does the term "Marriage hearse" symbolize?
    Topic: William Blake — London

    In "London" Blake attacks the institutions that should protect the weak but instead share in their oppression. The chimney-sweeper's cry "every blackning Church appalls" — the cry of the exploited child labourer appals (and, in the pun on "blackening", morally blackens) a Church that preaches charity yet allows little children to be sold into the soot and danger of the chimneys; the Church is blackened by its own failure, just as the sweeps are blackened by soot. "Marriage hearse" in the last stanza is a powerful oxymoron: the youthful harlot's curse blights the new-born infant's tear and turns marriage — the symbol of love, life and new birth — into a hearse, a vehicle of death, because disease and moral corruption infect even the marriage bed and are passed to innocent children.

  21. What does the apple symbolize in "A Poison Tree", and why does it kill the foe? What moral lesson does the poem teach?
    Topic: William Blake — A Poison Tree

    The apple symbolizes the speaker's wrath against his foe, which, because it was never expressed, was nursed in secret — watered with fears and tears and sunned with smiles and soft deceitful wiles — until suppressed anger grew into a poisonous tree bearing a bright, attractive fruit of hatred. The apple kills the foe because it embodies that ripened, hidden malice: the foe, knowing it belongs to his enemy, steals into the garden at night and eats the poisoned fruit of the speaker's deceit, and is found outstretched dead beneath the tree in the morning. The moral lesson is that anger openly expressed towards a friend dissolves and ends, whereas anger suppressed and hypocritically nourished grows into hatred that destroys the enemy — and morally poisons the one who nurses it.

  22. What is the implied concern of the poet in the question "Did he who made the Lamb make thee?" in "The Tyger"?
    Topic: William Blake — The Tyger

    The Tyger is a lyric about the nature of God and His creation. Throughout the poem the speaker wonders what immortal hand or eye could frame the tiger's fearful symmetry — its fire, strength and deadly terrors. The implied concern of the question "Did he who made the Lamb make thee?" is the problem of evil: could the same God who created the Lamb — the symbol of innocence, meekness and Christ-like gentleness — also have created the tiger, the symbol of ferocity and destructive power? The question expresses awe and bewilderment that one Creator is the author of both good and evil, innocence and experience; significantly, the poem offers no answer, and the speaker's repeated questioning shows man confronting a mystery beyond his understanding.

  23. What is the reason behind the poet's anguish in "The World is Too Much with Us"? Why does he wish to be a pagan?
    Topic: William Wordsworth — The World is Too Much with Us

    The poet's anguish is caused by the materialism of the modern world: "Getting and spending, we lay waste our powers" — in the pursuit of wealth and possessions people have given their hearts away, so that they see little in Nature that is theirs and are "out of tune" with the sea, the winds and all natural beauty. The Industrial Revolution has cut man off from the spiritual consolation of Nature. The poet exclaims that he would rather be "a Pagan suckled in a creed outworn" because the old pagan, though his religion is outdated, at least lived in living communion with Nature and could see gods in the sea and the winds — Proteus rising from the sea and Triton blowing his wreathed horn. Such glimpses, the poet feels, would make him less forlorn than the spiritually empty wealth-worship of his own age.

  24. What does Wordsworth mean by "The Child is father of the Man" in "The Rainbow"? What is "natural piety"?
    Topic: William Wordsworth — The Rainbow

    "The Child is father of the Man" is a paradox meaning that childhood is not merely the beginning of life but its shaping source: the feelings, joys and perceptions of the child form the character of the adult, so that the child, in a sense, begets the man he will become. The poet's heart leaps up at a rainbow in childhood, in manhood, and — he prays — in old age too, or he would rather die. "Natural piety" is the reverent bond of gratitude and love towards Nature that links these stages together: he wishes his days to be "bound each to each by natural piety", each day of his life connected to the next by the same pure, childlike reverence for Nature, so that the innocence and joy of childhood are never lost.

  25. "Sensuousness is the highest quality of Keats's poetical genius." Discuss with reference to "Ode to Autumn".
    Topic: John Keats — Ode to Autumn

    Sensuousness means Keats's power of appealing to all five senses — sight, hearing, smell, taste and touch — through concrete imagery, and "Ode to Autumn" is its finest example. Autumn is personified as a season of "mists and mellow fruitfulness", conspiring with the maturing sun to load vines, bend apple trees and fill fruit "with ripeness to the core" — images we can see and almost taste in the swollen gourd, the plumped hazel shells with a sweet kernel and the oozing cider-press watched "hours by hours". We feel the winnowing wind lifting Autumn's hair, hear the music of the season — the gnats' choir, lambs bleating, crickets singing, the red-breast whistling and swallows twittering — and touch the clammy cells of the bees. Keats does not preach or philosophize about autumn; he makes the reader see, hear, taste and feel it, which is why sensuousness is called the highest quality of his genius.

  26. Discuss the conflicts found in Keats's "Ode to a Nightingale".
    Topic: John Keats — Ode to a Nightingale

    The central conflicts of the ode are those that run through all Keats's poetry: mortality and immortality, pain and pleasure, life and death, transience and permanence, dream and reality. The nightingale, singing in full-throated ease, is a symbol of immortal art and joy — it is no "born for death" creature, and its song is the same voice heard by emperors and clowns and by Ruth amid the alien corn. The poet, by contrast, lives in a world "where men sit and hear each other groan; where palsy shakes a few, sad, last gray hairs, where youth grows pale, and spectre-thin, and dies". He longs to escape on the viewless wings of Poesy into the bird's timeless world, yet knows the imagination's flight cannot last: the word "forlorn" tolls him back to his sole self, and he is left asking whether the vision was a waking dream or a deception — the conflict between dream and reality unresolved.

  27. Describe the thematic aspects of Shelley's "Ode to the West Wind".
    Topic: P. B. Shelley — Ode to the West Wind

    The great theme of the ode is change — destruction that prepares the way for rebirth. The West Wind, the breath of Autumn's being, is both "Destroyer and Preserver": it drives the dead leaves like ghosts fleeing an enchanter and carries the winged seeds to their dark wintry bed, where they lie like corpses until the Spring, the wind's azure sister, wakes them into life. It stirs the clouds and the sea in the same spirit of upheaval. In the last section the theme becomes personal and prophetic: the poet, fallen upon the thorns of life, prays to the Wind to lift him as a wave, a leaf, a cloud, to make him its lyre, and to drive his dead thoughts and words over the universe "like withered leaves to quicken a new birth". The poem thus moves from nature to revolution and regeneration, ending in hope: "If Winter comes, can Spring be far behind?"

  28. Identify the speaker of "My Last Duchess" and discuss what his monologue reveals about his character.
    Topic: Robert Browning — My Last Duchess

    The speaker is the Duke of Ferrara, who is showing the portrait of his late Duchess to an envoy sent to arrange the Duke's next marriage. The poem is a dramatic monologue: while the Duke speaks, he unconsciously reveals his own character. He is proud, arrogant and possessive: the Duchess had "a heart too soon made glad" — she smiled at everyone and ranked his gift of a nine-hundred-years-old name with anybody's gift, and this equality of favour enraged him. He was too proud to stoop to correct her; instead, "I gave commands; then all smiles stopped together" — the chilling hint that he had her killed. Even her portrait he keeps behind a curtain that none draws but he, so that in death she is wholly his possession. The monologue ends with him coolly discussing dowry and pointing out his bronze Neptune — a symbol of his desire to tame and dominate — revealing a man of refined taste and utterly ruthless, jealous pride.

  29. What does the "road" symbolize in Frost's "The Road Not Taken"? Write the theme of the poem.
    Topic: Robert Frost — The Road Not Taken

    The road in the poem symbolizes the choices and decisions of life. The two roads that diverge in the yellow wood are the alternative paths that present themselves at every stage of human life; the traveller cannot travel both, just as a man cannot live two lives or undo a decision, for "way leads on to way" and he doubts he will ever come back. The theme of the poem is the universal dilemma of making the correct decision at the correct time, and the courage of individual choice: the speaker takes the road "less traveled by" and declares that this "has made all the difference". The poem teaches us not to follow the crowd blindly, not to fear taking chances and trying new things, and to stand steadfastly by the choice we truly believe in, accepting that its consequences will shape the rest of our lives.
