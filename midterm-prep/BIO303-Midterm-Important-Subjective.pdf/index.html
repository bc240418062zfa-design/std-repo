  1. What are ncRNAs?
    Topic: Nucleic Acids & RNA

    ncRNAs are the large and small noncoding regulatory RNAs. One of the most exciting discoveries in the last decade of eukaryotic regulatory biology has been the identification and characterization of regulatory non-protein-coding RNAs (ncRNAs). ncRNAs exist in two general size classes: small, consisting of microRNAs (miRNAs) and silencing RNAs (siRNAs); and large, consisting of long noncoding RNAs (lncRNAs). The small ncRNAs (miRNAs and siRNAs) typically inhibit gene expression at the level of specific protein production by targeting mRNAs through one of several distinct mechanisms. Both siRNAs and miRNAs typically hybridize, via the formation of RNA–RNA hybridization, to their targeted mRNAs.

  2. Differentiate between DNA and RNA.
    Topic: Nucleic Acids & RNA

    Full name: DNA = Deoxyribonucleic Acid; RNA = Ribonucleic Acid. Function: DNA replicates and stores genetic information — it is a blueprint for all the genetic information contained within an organism. RNA converts the genetic information contained within DNA into a format used to build proteins, and then moves it to ribosomal protein factories. Structure: DNA consists of two strands arranged in a double helix; the strands are made up of subunits called nucleotides, and each nucleotide contains a phosphate, a 5-carbon sugar molecule and a nitrogenous base. RNA has only one strand, but like DNA it is made up of nucleotides; RNA strands are shorter than DNA strands, and RNA sometimes forms a secondary double-helix structure, but only intermittently. Length: DNA is a much longer polymer than RNA — a chromosome, for example, is a single, long DNA molecule that would be several centimetres in length when unravelled. RNA molecules are variable in length, but much shorter than long DNA polymers; a large RNA molecule might only be a few thousand base pairs long. Sugar: The sugar in DNA is deoxyribose, which contains one less hydroxyl group than RNA's ribose. RNA contains ribose sugar molecules, without the hydroxyl modifications of deoxyribose. Bases: The bases in DNA are Adenine (A), Thymine (T), Guanine (G) and Cytosine (C). RNA shares Adenine (A), Guanine (G) and Cytosine (C) with DNA, but contains Uracil (U) rather than Thymine. Base pairs: In DNA, Adenine pairs with Thymine (A–T) and Cytosine with Guanine (C–G). In RNA, Adenine pairs with Uracil (A–U) and Cytosine with Guanine (C–G). Location: DNA is found in the nucleus, with a small amount of DNA also present in mitochondria. RNA forms in the nucleolus, and then moves to specialised regions of the cytoplasm depending on the type of RNA formed. Reactivity: Due to its deoxyribose sugar, which contains one less oxygen-containing hydroxyl group, DNA is a more stable molecule than RNA, which is useful for a molecule whose task is keeping genetic information safe. RNA, containing a ribose sugar, is more reactive than DNA and is not stable in alkaline conditions; RNA's larger helical grooves mean it is more easily subject to attack by enzymes. Ultraviolet (UV) sensitivity: DNA is vulnerable to damage by ultraviolet light; RNA is more resistant to damage from UV light than DNA.

  3. How are ribonucleotides held together?
    Topic: Nucleic Acids & RNA

    The ribonucleotides are held together by 3′–5′ phosphodiester bonds.

  4. Define the first law of thermodynamics.
    Topic: Thermodynamics & Bioenergetics

    The first law is the principle of the conservation of energy: it states that for any physical or chemical change, the total amount of energy in the universe remains constant; energy may change form or it may be transported from one region to another, but it cannot be created or destroyed.

  5. Write about the thermodynamically unstable condition of a reaction.
    Topic: Thermodynamics & Bioenergetics

    Thermodynamic instability means a system exists that is not at equilibrium. That means that some kind of chemical reaction is thermodynamically possible. One of the results of thermodynamics is that a system at equilibrium at a given temperature and pressure is at a minimum of its Gibbs free energy. An unstable system has the potential to reach another state in such a way that lowers its Gibbs free energy.

  6. Define high-energy and low-energy compounds.
    Topic: Thermodynamics & Bioenergetics

    High-energy compounds: Compounds that contain phosphate are called or described as high-energy ones when they have a large value of −ΔG (the change in Gibbs free energy), of the order of −25 or −30 kJ/mol. The high-energy phosphate compounds represent temporary forms of stored energy, transporting energy from one reaction to another. Examples: ATP, GTP, ADP, etc. Low-energy compounds: Hydrolysis of low-energy phosphate compounds releases Pi, which has an even lower group transfer potential. The phosphate group transfer potential of phosphoenolpyruvate is very high, that of ATP is high, and that of glucose-6-phosphate is low. Example: Glucose 6-phosphate.

  7. Write the phenomenon of phosphoryl group transfer, by example of phosphocreatine (PCr).
    Topic: Thermodynamics & Bioenergetics

    In phosphocreatine the P–N bond can be hydrolysed to generate free creatine and Pi. The release of Pi and the resonance stabilization of creatine favour the forward reaction. Pi is also resonance stabilized. The standard free energy change of phosphocreatine hydrolysis is large and negative (ΔG°′ = −49.3 kJ/mol, as given in the source). Phosphoryl group transfer is the attachment of a good leaving group (a phosphoryl group) to a metabolic intermediate to activate the intermediate for subsequent reaction. A phosphoryl group (PO3²⁻) can be transferred to an alcohol or to a carboxylic acid.

  8. What are thioesters?
    Topic: Thermodynamics & Bioenergetics

    In thioesters a sulfur atom replaces the usual oxygen in the ester bond. Thioesters have a large, negative standard free energy change of hydrolysis. Acetyl coenzyme A is one of many thioesters important in metabolism and has a large, negative standard free energy of hydrolysis. The acetyl group in these compounds is activated for trans-acylation, condensation or oxidation-reduction reactions. Hydrolysis of the ester bond generates a carboxylic acid, which can ionize and assume several resonance forms. ΔG°′ = −31.4 kJ/mol for acetyl-CoA hydrolysis.

  9. What are lyases? Give an example.
    Topic: Enzymes

    Lyases are enzymes that catalyze the addition of NH3, H2O or CO2 to double bonds, or the removal of these groups, leaving behind double bonds. Examples: Decarboxylases, Aldolases.

  10. What is the Cori cycle?
    Topic: Glycolysis, Gluconeogenesis & Carbohydrate Metabolism

    The pathway through which lactate produced by anaerobic glycolysis in skeletal muscle returns to the liver and is converted to glucose, which moves back to muscle and is converted to glycogen, is called the Cori cycle. Site: Liver. Substrate: Lactate.

  11. Write the 5th step of glycolysis and name the enzyme(s).
    Topic: Glycolysis, Gluconeogenesis & Carbohydrate Metabolism

    (The source's answer works through the first five steps of glycolysis.) Step 1: The first step in glycolysis is phosphorylation of glucose at the hydroxyl group on C-6 by a family of enzymes called hexokinases, to form glucose-6-phosphate (G6P). Step 2: The G6P is converted to D-fructose 6-phosphate, which is again phosphorylated, this time at C-1, to yield D-fructose 1,6-bisphosphate (Step 3). For both phosphorylations, ATP is the phosphoryl group donor. Step 4: Fructose 1,6-bisphosphate is split to yield two three-carbon molecules, dihydroxyacetone phosphate and glyceraldehyde 3-phosphate; this is the lysis step that gives the pathway its name. Step 5: The dihydroxyacetone phosphate is isomerized to a second molecule of glyceraldehyde 3-phosphate.

  12. How does ATP synthase work in the inner mitochondrial membrane?
    Topic: Electron Transport & ATP Synthesis

    Electrons carried by NADH and FADH2 are funneled into a chain of mitochondrial (or, in bacteria, plasma membrane-bound) electron carriers — the respiratory chain — ultimately reducing O2 to H2O. This electron flow drives the production of ATP.

  13. What is transketolase?
    Topic: Pentose Phosphate Pathway

    Transketolase catalyzes the transfer of a two-carbon fragment from a ketose donor to an aldose acceptor. In its first appearance in the pentose phosphate pathway, transketolase transfers C-1 and C-2 of xylulose 5-phosphate to ribose 5-phosphate, forming the seven-carbon product sedoheptulose 7-phosphate.

  14. Write two ketone bodies in the cell.
    Topic: Fatty Acid & Ketone Body Metabolism

    1. Acetone. 2. Acetoacetate.

  15. What are the three main stages (steps) of an industrial fermentation operation?
    Topic: Fermentation & Industrial Biotechnology

    Any industrial fermentation operation can be broken down into three main stages, viz: 1. Upstream processing, 2. The fermentation process, 3. Downstream processing.

  16. How are industrial products formed?
    Topic: Fermentation & Industrial Biotechnology

    Industrial products of biotechnology are formed by fermentation: selected microorganisms (bacteria, yeasts or fungi) are grown on a large scale in fermenters (bioreactors) on a suitable nutrient medium. In upstream processing, the culture medium is prepared and sterilized and a pure, active inoculum of the production organism is developed. During the fermentation process, the microorganisms grow under controlled conditions of temperature, pH, aeration and agitation and convert the substrate into the desired product, such as an enzyme, antibiotic, organic acid or ethanol. In downstream processing, the product is separated from the culture broth and cells, then purified, concentrated and formulated into the final industrial product.

  17. Name the six classes of enzymes in the IUB classification, with one example of each.
    Topic: Enzymes

    Oxidoreductases — catalyze oxidation-reduction reactions (e.g. lactate dehydrogenase). Transferases — transfer a functional group from one substrate to another (e.g. hexokinase, kinases). Hydrolases — break bonds by the addition of water (e.g. lipases, peptidases). Lyases — remove groups leaving double bonds, or add groups to double bonds (e.g. aldolases, decarboxylases). Isomerases — catalyze intramolecular rearrangements (e.g. triose phosphate isomerase). Ligases — join two molecules together, coupled with ATP hydrolysis (e.g. pyruvate carboxylase).

  18. Differentiate between competitive and non-competitive enzyme inhibition.
    Topic: Enzymes

    Competitive inhibition: the inhibitor resembles the substrate and competes with it for the active site of the enzyme. Vmax remains unchanged, but Km increases (more substrate is needed). The inhibition can be overcome by increasing the substrate concentration. Non-competitive inhibition: the inhibitor binds to a site other than the active site (an allosteric site) and changes the shape of the enzyme. Vmax decreases, while Km remains unchanged. It cannot be overcome by increasing the substrate concentration.

  19. What is a coenzyme? How does it differ from a cofactor and a prosthetic group?
    Topic: Enzymes

    A cofactor is a non-protein chemical component required for an enzyme's activity; it may be a metal ion (such as Zn²⁺ or Fe²⁺) or an organic molecule. An organic cofactor is called a coenzyme (e.g. NAD⁺, FAD, coenzyme A); coenzymes act as carriers of electrons or functional groups and are only loosely associated with the enzyme. A prosthetic group is a cofactor that is tightly (often covalently) bound to the enzyme, e.g. the flavin nucleotides FMN and FAD, which serve as prosthetic groups of flavoproteins.

  20. Define Km and Vmax. What does a low Km value indicate?
    Topic: Enzymes

    Vmax is the maximum initial velocity (Vo) of an enzyme-catalyzed reaction, reached when all the enzyme's active sites are saturated with substrate. Km (the Michaelis constant) is the substrate concentration at which the reaction velocity is half of Vmax. A low Km indicates a high affinity of the enzyme for its substrate (only a little substrate is needed to reach half-maximal velocity); a high Km indicates a low affinity.

  21. Differentiate between exergonic and endergonic reactions.
    Topic: Thermodynamics & Bioenergetics

    Exergonic reactions release free energy (ΔG is negative) and proceed spontaneously, e.g. ATP hydrolysis and the oxidation of glucose. Endergonic reactions proceed with a gain of free energy (ΔG is positive) and are non-spontaneous; in the cell they occur only when coupled to an exergonic reaction, e.g. the phosphorylation of glucose coupled to ATP hydrolysis.

  22. What is ΔG°′, and how is it related to the equilibrium constant Keq?
    Topic: Thermodynamics & Bioenergetics

    ΔG°′ is the standard free-energy change of a reaction under the standard conditions used in biochemistry (including pH 7). It is related to the equilibrium constant by the equation ΔG°′ = −RT ln Keq. If Keq is greater than 1.0, ΔG°′ is negative and the reaction favours the products; if Keq is less than 1.0, ΔG°′ is positive; if Keq equals 1.0, ΔG°′ is zero.

  23. Why is ATP called the energy currency of the cell?
    Topic: Thermodynamics & Bioenergetics

    ATP links energy-releasing (catabolic) processes with energy-requiring (anabolic) processes: energy obtained from the oxidation of fuels is captured in ATP, and hydrolysis of ATP (ΔG°′ about −30.5 kJ/mol) then drives unfavourable reactions by coupling. ATP provides energy mainly by group (phosphoryl) transfers. Its phosphoryl group transfer potential is intermediate among phosphorylated compounds — lower than that of phosphoenolpyruvate and phosphocreatine, but higher than that of glucose-6-phosphate — so ATP can accept phosphoryl groups from high-energy donors and donate them to lower-energy acceptors. ATP is continuously recycled between ATP and ADP + Pi, which makes it the common energy currency of the cell.

  24. Write the three irreversible steps of glycolysis and the enzymes that catalyze them.
    Topic: Glycolysis & Gluconeogenesis

    Step 1: Glucose → Glucose-6-phosphate, catalyzed by hexokinase (glucokinase in the liver). Step 3: Fructose-6-phosphate → Fructose-1,6-bisphosphate, catalyzed by phosphofructokinase-1 (PFK-1) — the committed and main regulatory step of glycolysis; PFK-1 is an allosteric enzyme. Step 10: Phosphoenolpyruvate → Pyruvate, catalyzed by pyruvate kinase. The remaining seven steps of glycolysis are reversible and are shared with gluconeogenesis.

  25. Differentiate between glycolysis and gluconeogenesis.
    Topic: Glycolysis & Gluconeogenesis

    Glycolysis is the catabolic breakdown of glucose to pyruvate; it takes place in the cytosol of all cells, gives a net gain of 2 ATP, and is favoured in the fed state. Gluconeogenesis is the anabolic synthesis of glucose from non-carbohydrate precursors (lactate, glycerol and most amino acids); it takes place mainly in the liver (cytosol and mitochondria), is accelerated during starvation/fasting, and is energetically expensive, consuming six high-energy phosphate bonds per glucose formed. The two pathways share the seven reversible steps of glycolysis, but gluconeogenesis bypasses the three irreversible glycolytic steps using four bypass enzymes: pyruvate carboxylase, phosphoenolpyruvate carboxykinase, fructose-1,6-bisphosphatase and glucose-6-phosphatase.

  26. Name the four bypass enzymes of gluconeogenesis.
    Topic: Glycolysis & Gluconeogenesis

    1. Pyruvate carboxylase — converts pyruvate to oxaloacetate; this is the first step of gluconeogenesis. 2. Phosphoenolpyruvate carboxykinase (PEPCK) — converts oxaloacetate to phosphoenolpyruvate. 3. Fructose-1,6-bisphosphatase — converts fructose-1,6-bisphosphate to fructose-6-phosphate. 4. Glucose-6-phosphatase — converts glucose-6-phosphate to free glucose (found in the liver).

  27. What is the net gain of ATP in glycolysis? Describe its preparatory and payoff phases.
    Topic: Glycolysis & Gluconeogenesis

    In the preparatory (investment) phase, glucose is phosphorylated and cleaved into two molecules of glyceraldehyde-3-phosphate, consuming 2 ATP. In the payoff phase, each glyceraldehyde-3-phosphate is oxidized and converted to pyruvate, producing 2 ATP by substrate-level phosphorylation and 1 NADH — a total of 4 ATP and 2 NADH per glucose. Net gain of glycolysis = 4 − 2 = 2 ATP (plus 2 NADH) per glucose molecule.

  28. What is a ligand? What is meant by a binding site?
    Topic: Proteins, Ligands & Binding

    A ligand is a signal-triggering molecule that binds reversibly to a specific site on a target protein (e.g. a substrate, a hormone, or oxygen binding to myoglobin/hemoglobin). The binding site is the specific region of the protein that is complementary to the ligand in shape, size and charge, where the ligand binds through weak, non-covalent interactions.

  29. Describe the four levels of protein structure.
    Topic: Proteins, Ligands & Binding

    Primary structure: the linear sequence of amino acids joined by peptide bonds. Secondary structure: local folding of the polypeptide chain into α-helices and β-pleated sheets, stabilized by hydrogen bonds. Tertiary structure: the overall three-dimensional folding of a single polypeptide chain, stabilized by hydrophobic interactions, hydrogen bonds, ionic bonds and disulfide bonds. Quaternary structure: the association of two or more polypeptide subunits into one functional protein, e.g. hemoglobin.

  30. Name the main types of RNA and give the function of each.
    Topic: Nucleic Acids & RNA

    mRNA (messenger RNA): carries the genetic code from DNA to the ribosome for protein synthesis; it is complementary to one of the two strands of a gene. tRNA (transfer RNA): a small, single-stranded globular molecule that carries amino acids to the ribosome and matches them to the mRNA codons. rRNA (ribosomal RNA): forms the structural and catalytic core of the ribosomes. In addition, eukaryotes contain small RNAs such as snRNA (involved in splicing) and the regulatory miRNAs and siRNAs (gene silencing).

  31. What is the pentose phosphate pathway? Write its two phases and its main products.
    Topic: Pentose Phosphate Pathway

    The pentose phosphate pathway (PPP, also called the hexose monophosphate shunt) is an alternative pathway of glucose oxidation in the cytosol that produces no ATP directly. Its oxidative phase oxidizes glucose-6-phosphate to ribulose-5-phosphate, producing 2 NADPH and CO2. Its non-oxidative phase reversibly interconverts sugars through the enzymes transketolase and transaldolase and links the pathway with glycolysis. Main products: NADPH, needed for reductive biosynthesis and protection against oxidative damage, and ribose-5-phosphate, needed for the synthesis of nucleotides and nucleic acids.

  32. What is fermentation? Differentiate between surface and submerged fermentation.
    Topic: Fermentation & Industrial Biotechnology

    Fermentation is the process in which microorganisms convert organic substrates into useful products such as ethanol, organic acids, enzymes and antibiotics; the study of fermentation is called fermentology. In the surface technique, the microorganisms are cultivated on the surface of a liquid or solid substrate. In submerged fermentation, the microorganisms grow suspended throughout a liquid medium in a fermenter under controlled aeration and agitation; this is the technique used for most large-scale industrial production.

  33. What are ketone bodies? When and where are they formed?
    Topic: Fatty Acid & Ketone Body Metabolism

    Ketone bodies are acetone, acetoacetate and β-hydroxybutyrate. They are formed in the liver (in the mitochondria of hepatocytes) from acetyl-CoA when fatty-acid oxidation is high and oxaloacetate is being used for gluconeogenesis — as happens during starvation, prolonged fasting and uncontrolled diabetes mellitus. Being water-soluble, they are exported in the blood to extrahepatic tissues such as muscle and brain, where they are oxidized for energy; their excessive production leads to ketosis and ketoacidosis.

  34. Name the complexes of the electron transport chain and its mobile electron carriers.
    Topic: Electron Transport & ATP Synthesis

    The respiratory (electron transport) chain in the inner mitochondrial membrane consists of Complex I (NADH dehydrogenase), Complex II (succinate dehydrogenase), Complex III (cytochrome bc1 complex) and Complex IV (cytochrome c oxidase), together with two mobile carriers: coenzyme Q (ubiquinone), which moves within the membrane, and cytochrome c, which moves on the membrane surface. Electrons from NADH enter at Complex I and electrons from FADH2 at Complex II; all electrons pass through coenzyme Q, Complex III and cytochrome c to Complex IV, which reduces O2 to H2O. The proton gradient generated drives ATP synthase (Complex V) to produce ATP.

  35. What are the types of noteworthy chemical transformations during glycolysis?
    Topic: Glycolysis & Gluconeogenesis

    Three types of noteworthy chemical transformations take place during glycolysis: (1) degradation of the carbon skeleton of glucose to pyruvate; (2) phosphorylation of ADP to ATP by compounds with high phosphoryl-transfer potential; and (3) transfer of a hydride ion to NAD+, forming NADH.

  36. Write down the importance (biomedical importance) of gluconeogenesis.
    Topic: Glycolysis & Gluconeogenesis

    Gluconeogenesis maintains the blood glucose concentration during prolonged fasting or starvation, when sufficient carbohydrate is not available from the diet or from glycogen reserves. It supplies glucose to the glucose-dependent tissues such as the brain, erythrocytes, testes, renal medulla and embryonic tissues. It maintains the level of intermediates of the TCA cycle even when fatty acids are the main source of acetyl-CoA in the tissues, and it clears the lactate produced by muscle and erythrocytes and the glycerol produced by adipose tissue.

  37. Define gluconeogenesis.
    Topic: Glycolysis & Gluconeogenesis

    Gluconeogenesis ('new formation of sugar') is the synthesis of glucose from non-carbohydrate sources. It is the pathway that converts pyruvate and related three- and four-carbon compounds to glucose. In mammals it takes place mainly in the liver and, to a lesser extent, in the renal cortex.

  38. Write down the overall equation of gluconeogenesis.
    Topic: Glycolysis & Gluconeogenesis

    2 Pyruvate + 4 ATP + 2 GTP + 2 NADH + 2 H+ + 4 H2O → Glucose + 4 ADP + 2 GDP + 6 Pi + 2 NAD+. The synthesis of glucose from pyruvate is an energy-expensive process: it costs six nucleoside triphosphates, whereas glycolysis nets only two ATP.

  39. Write the steps of the synthesis of phosphoenolpyruvate from pyruvate.
    Topic: Glycolysis & Gluconeogenesis

    Pyruvate is first transported into the mitochondria, where pyruvate carboxylase converts it to oxaloacetate using ATP and CO2. Oxaloacetate is reduced to malate by mitochondrial malate dehydrogenase, and malate leaves the mitochondrion. In the cytosol, malate is re-oxidized to oxaloacetate, which is then converted to phosphoenolpyruvate by PEP carboxykinase, using GTP and releasing CO2.

  40. Write the full (overall) equation of glycolysis.
    Topic: Glycolysis & Gluconeogenesis

    Glucose + 2 NAD+ + 2 ADP + 2 Pi → 2 Pyruvate + 2 NADH + 2 H+ + 2 ATP + 2 H2O. Overall the process is exergonic: the oxidation of glucose to pyruvate releases free energy, part of which is conserved in the formation of ATP.

  41. Why is malate formed during gluconeogenesis?
    Topic: Glycolysis & Gluconeogenesis

    Oxaloacetate formed inside the mitochondria cannot cross the inner mitochondrial membrane, because there is no transporter for it. It is therefore reduced to malate by mitochondrial malate dehydrogenase; malate is exported to the cytosol, where it is re-oxidized to oxaloacetate, which is then converted to phosphoenolpyruvate. This also carries reducing equivalents out of the mitochondrion.

  42. Write down the effects of the glucagon–cAMP cascade in the liver.
    Topic: Glycolysis & Gluconeogenesis

    In the liver, the glucagon–cAMP cascade: (1) stimulates gluconeogenesis; (2) inhibits glycolysis; (3) stimulates glycogen breakdown (glycogenolysis); (4) inhibits glycogen synthesis; and (5) results in the formation of free glucose, which is released into the blood.

  43. What are the three major fates of glucose in animals and vascular plants?
    Topic: Glycolysis & Gluconeogenesis

    Glucose has three major fates in animals and vascular plants: (1) it may be stored as a polysaccharide (glycogen in animals, starch in plants) or as sucrose; (2) it may be oxidized to pyruvate via glycolysis to provide ATP and metabolic intermediates; or (3) it may be oxidized via the pentose phosphate pathway to yield ribose 5-phosphate for nucleic acid synthesis and NADPH for reductive biosynthesis.

  44. Which form of pyruvate can immediately tautomerize, and what does it form?
    Topic: Glycolysis & Gluconeogenesis

    The enol form of pyruvate, as first released from phosphoenolpyruvate, immediately tautomerizes (non-enzymatically) to the more stable keto form of pyruvate.

  45. Name the organ in which glucose transporters are mainly found, and write the names of three glucose transporters (GLUT family).
    Topic: Glycolysis & Gluconeogenesis

    Glucose is taken up from the blood into cells by a family of glucose transporters (GLUT). Three important members are: GLUT1 — present in most tissues and responsible for basal glucose uptake (e.g., erythrocytes, blood–brain barrier); GLUT2 — found in liver, pancreatic β-cells, intestine and kidney, a low-affinity, high-capacity transporter that also acts as a glucose sensor; and GLUT3 — found mainly in neurons, a high-affinity transporter that ensures glucose supply to the brain.

  46. Define glycogenosis.
    Topic: Glycolysis & Gluconeogenesis

    Glycogenosis (glycogen storage disease) is a group of inherited disorders caused by defects in the enzymes of glycogen synthesis or glycogen degradation. Glycogen of normal or abnormal structure accumulates in tissues such as the liver and muscle, e.g., von Gierke disease, caused by glucose-6-phosphatase deficiency.

  47. Write down the laws of thermodynamics.
    Topic: Thermodynamics & Bioenergetics

    First law of thermodynamics (law of conservation of energy): in any physical or chemical change, the total amount of energy in the universe remains constant, although the form of the energy may change. Second law of thermodynamics: in all natural processes, the entropy of the universe increases; that is, all physical and chemical processes proceed in such a direction that the randomness or disorder (entropy) of the universe increases.

  48. Define Gibbs free energy.
    Topic: Thermodynamics & Bioenergetics

    Gibbs free energy (G) is the amount of energy capable of doing work during a reaction at constant temperature and pressure. When a reaction proceeds with the release of free energy (the system loses free energy, ΔG is negative), the reaction is said to be exergonic; if the system gains free energy (ΔG is positive), the reaction is endergonic.

  49. Define bioenergetics.
    Topic: Thermodynamics & Bioenergetics

    Bioenergetics is the quantitative study of the energy transductions — the changes of one form of energy into another — that occur in living cells, and of the nature and function of the chemical processes underlying these transductions.

  50. What is standard reduction potential (E°′)?
    Topic: Thermodynamics & Bioenergetics

    The standard reduction potential (E°′) is a quantitative measure, in volts, of the tendency of a conjugate redox pair to lose or gain electrons, measured under standard conditions (25°C, 1 M concentrations, pH 7) relative to the standard hydrogen electrode. The more negative the E°′, the greater the tendency of the pair to lose electrons (to act as a reducing agent); the more positive the E°′, the greater its tendency to gain electrons.

  51. Differentiate between standard free-energy change and actual free-energy change.
    Topic: Thermodynamics & Bioenergetics

    The standard free-energy change (ΔG°′) is an alternative way of expressing the equilibrium constant: if Keq = 1, ΔG°′ is zero; if Keq > 1, ΔG°′ is negative; and if Keq < 1, ΔG°′ is positive. The actual free-energy change (ΔG), on the other hand, depends on ΔG°′ and on the actual concentrations of reactants and products, and it is ΔG that determines the direction in which a reaction proceeds in the cell.

  52. Define oxidation and reduction reactions. Also define reductant and oxidant.
    Topic: Thermodynamics & Bioenergetics

    Oxidation is the loss of electrons (or the loss of hydrogen atoms, or the gain of oxygen) by a molecule, atom or ion — an increase in oxidation state. Reduction is the gain of electrons (or the gain of hydrogen atoms, or the loss of oxygen) — a decrease in oxidation state. The electron-donating molecule in an oxidation–reduction reaction is called the reducing agent or reductant, and the electron-accepting molecule is called the oxidizing agent or oxidant; together they form a conjugate redox pair.

  53. Write down the three major classes of reactions in which C–C bonds are formed or broken.
    Topic: Thermodynamics & Bioenergetics

    The three major classes of reactions in which carbon–carbon bonds are formed or broken in biological systems are: (1) aldol condensation, (2) Claisen condensation, and (3) decarboxylation (of a β-keto acid).

  54. Briefly describe the 1906 experiment of Arthur Harden and William Young on yeast extract.
    Topic: Thermodynamics & Bioenergetics

    In 1906, Arthur Harden and William Young were experimenting with yeast extract to test their hypothesis that inhibitors of proteolytic enzymes would stabilize the glucose-fermenting enzymes in the extract. They found that boiled serum also stimulated fermentation; the stimulating factor proved to be inorganic phosphate (Pi). They further discovered that glucose added to the yeast extract was converted to a hexose bisphosphate — the 'Harden–Young ester' — which was eventually identified as fructose 1,6-bisphosphate.

  55. What do you mean by the 'energy hill' and the transition state of a chemical reaction? How does activation energy affect the rate of a reaction?
    Topic: Thermodynamics & Bioenergetics

    The transition state is the fleeting, highest-energy state of the reacting molecules at the top of the 'energy hill' between substrates and products. The activation energy is the amount of energy that must be supplied to bring the reacting molecules up to the transition state. The higher the activation energy, the slower the reaction; enzymes increase the rate of a reaction by lowering the activation energy, without changing the free-energy difference between substrates and products.

  56. What is a hydrolase? Give examples.
    Topic: Enzymes

    A hydrolase is an enzyme that catalyzes a hydrolysis reaction — the cleavage of bonds such as C–O, C–N and C–C by the addition of water. Examples include the peptidases (digestive enzymes), lipases, phosphatases and nucleases.

  57. What is an isozyme?
    Topic: Enzymes

    Two or more enzymes that catalyze the same reaction but are encoded by different genes are called isozymes, e.g., the isozymes of hexokinase (hexokinases I–III and glucokinase/hexokinase IV).

  58. Explain the ping-pong mechanism of nucleoside diphosphate kinase.
    Topic: Enzymes

    In the ping-pong mechanism of nucleoside diphosphate kinase, the enzyme binds its first substrate (ATP in this case), and a phosphoryl group is transferred to the side chain of a histidine (His) residue of the enzyme. ADP departs, and another nucleoside (or deoxynucleoside) diphosphate replaces it; this is converted to the corresponding triphosphate by transfer of the phosphoryl group from the phosphohistidine residue.

  59. Write the full names of NAD and NADP.
    Topic: Enzymes

    NAD = nicotinamide adenine dinucleotide. NADP = nicotinamide adenine dinucleotide phosphate.

  60. How does enzyme concentration affect the rate of an enzyme-catalyzed reaction?
    Topic: Enzymes

    When substrate is present in excess, the rate of an enzyme-catalyzed reaction increases in direct proportion to the enzyme concentration: doubling the amount of enzyme doubles the number of active sites available, so about twice as much substrate is converted to product per unit time. The rate therefore rises linearly with enzyme concentration until the substrate becomes limiting.

  61. Write three steps in an enzyme-catalyzed reaction.
    Topic: Enzymes

    (1) The substrate binds to the active site of the enzyme, forming an enzyme–substrate (ES) complex. (2) The bound substrate is converted to product while attached to the enzyme (enzyme–product complex); the enzyme stabilizes the transition state and lowers the activation energy. (3) The product is released and the enzyme is regenerated unchanged, ready to catalyze another reaction cycle.

  62. Define nucleophiles and electrophiles.
    Topic: Proteins, Ligands & Functional Groups

    Nucleophiles are electron-rich functional groups that seek a positive (electron-poor) center and donate electrons to form a bond. Electrophiles are electron-deficient functional groups that seek electrons and accept an electron pair from a nucleophile. Carbon–carbon bond formation in metabolism generally results from the reaction of a nucleophilic carbon with an electrophilic carbon.

  63. What are thioalcohols (thiols)?
    Topic: Proteins, Ligands & Functional Groups

    Thioalcohols (thiols) are organic compounds in which the oxygen atom of an alcohol is replaced by sulfur, i.e., they contain an –SH (thiol/sulfhydryl) group instead of –OH. The thiol group of coenzyme A is chemically important because it forms high-energy thioester bonds, as in acetyl-CoA.

  64. What is transaldolase? Write its function.
    Topic: Pentose Phosphate Pathway

    Transaldolase is an enzyme of the non-oxidative phase of the pentose phosphate pathway. It transfers a three-carbon fragment (a dihydroxyacetone group) from sedoheptulose 7-phosphate to glyceraldehyde 3-phosphate, forming fructose 6-phosphate and erythrose 4-phosphate.

  65. Write the reactions (steps) of the oxidative phase of the pentose phosphate pathway.
    Topic: Pentose Phosphate Pathway

    In the oxidative phase, glucose 6-phosphate is oxidized and decarboxylated to ribulose 5-phosphate in three reactions: (1) glucose 6-phosphate dehydrogenase oxidizes glucose 6-phosphate to 6-phosphoglucono-δ-lactone, producing NADPH; (2) lactonase hydrolyzes the lactone to 6-phosphogluconate; (3) 6-phosphogluconate dehydrogenase oxidatively decarboxylates 6-phosphogluconate at C-1 to ribulose 5-phosphate, producing a second NADPH and CO2. Net result: glucose 6-phosphate + 2 NADP+ + H2O → ribulose 5-phosphate + 2 NADPH + 2 H+ + CO2.

  66. What are the components (requirements) of a fermenter / fermentation process?
    Topic: Fermentation & Industrial Biotechnology

    The main components (requirements) of a fermentation process are: (i) formulation of the fermentation medium; (ii) sterilization of the medium; (iii) production of an active, pure culture in sufficient quantity to inoculate the production vessel; (iv) growth of the organism in the fermenter under optimum conditions; (v) extraction of the product and its purification; and (vi) disposal of the effluents produced by the process.

  67. Write six types of fermenters.
    Topic: Fermentation & Industrial Biotechnology

    The different types of fermenters include: simple fermenters (used for batch and continuous fermentation), fed-batch fermenters, air-lift fermenters, cyclone column fermenters, tower fermenters, fluidized bed fermenters, packed bed fermenters and photobioreactors.

  68. What is continuous fermentation?
    Topic: Fermentation & Industrial Biotechnology

    Continuous fermentation is a process in which fresh medium (substrate) is continuously added into the bioreactor, and biomass or products containing leftover nutrients and microorganisms are continuously removed at the same rate, keeping the culture volume constant and the cells in the exponential (log) phase of growth.

  69. What is the stationary phase in batch fermentation?
    Topic: Fermentation & Industrial Biotechnology

    In a batch culture, the stationary phase is the phase in which the growth rate of the cells equals their death rate, so the total number of viable cells remains constant. It is reached when an essential nutrient is exhausted or waste products accumulate to inhibitory levels; secondary metabolites (such as many antibiotics) are typically produced during this phase.

  70. Why is sterilization essential in fermentation?
    Topic: Fermentation & Industrial Biotechnology

    Sterilization is essential in fermentation to prevent contamination of the culture by undesired microorganisms, which would compete for the nutrients and reduce the yield of the desired product. The air is sterilized by membrane filtration, the medium is sterilized by heat, and heat-labile components are sterilized by filtration.

  71. How are yogurt and Swiss cheese produced by fermentation?
    Topic: Fermentation & Industrial Biotechnology

    Yogurt is produced when the bacterium Lactobacillus bulgaricus ferments the carbohydrates in milk, producing lactic acid; the resulting drop in pH causes the milk proteins to precipitate, producing the thick texture and sour taste of unsweetened yogurt. Swiss cheese is produced using Propionibacterium freudenreichii, which ferments milk to produce propionic acid and CO2; the propionic acid precipitates the milk proteins, and bubbles of CO2 cause the holes characteristic of Swiss cheese.

  72. Write the advantages of fermentation.
    Topic: Fermentation & Industrial Biotechnology

    Fermentation preserves and enriches food and improves its digestibility, taste and flavour. It has the potential of enhancing food safety by controlling the growth and multiplication of a number of pathogens in foods. It also makes an important contribution to human nutrition, particularly in developing countries, where economic problems pose a major barrier to ensuring food safety.

  73. Describe briefly the meaning of fermentation in biochemistry and in industrial microbiology.
    Topic: Fermentation & Industrial Biotechnology

    For the biochemist, the meaning of fermentation relates to the generation of energy by the catabolism of organic compounds. For the industrial microbiologist, the meaning tends to be much broader: industrial microbiologists have extended the term 'fermentation' to describe any process for the production of a product by the mass culture of microorganisms.

  74. Enlist the five major groups of commercially important fermentation products.
    Topic: Fermentation & Industrial Biotechnology

    Commercially important products of fermentation can be described in five major groups: (1) biomass, (2) primary metabolites, (3) bioconversion or biotransformation products, (4) enzymes, and (5) recombinant products.

  75. Which factors are considered when designing a fermenter?
    Topic: Fermentation & Industrial Biotechnology

    Factors to be considered when designing a fermenter include: aseptic and regulatory capability and long-term reliability; adequate aeration and agitation; low power consumption; temperature and pH controls; and sampling facilities.

  76. What are the fates of pyruvate under anaerobic conditions?
    Topic: Fermentation & Industrial Biotechnology

    Under anaerobic conditions, pyruvate is the terminal electron acceptor of glycolysis, and the NAD+ consumed in the glyceraldehyde 3-phosphate dehydrogenase step must be regenerated. In skeletal muscle and some microorganisms, pyruvate is reduced to lactate (lactic acid fermentation). In yeast and several other microorganisms, pyruvate is converted to ethanol and CO2 (alcoholic/ethanol fermentation). Both fates regenerate NAD+ so that glycolysis can continue.

  77. What do you know about snRNA?
    Topic: Nucleic Acids & RNA

    Small nuclear RNAs (snRNAs) are a large number of small, stable RNA species found in eukaryotic cells. Most of them are complexed with proteins to form ribonucleoproteins, and they are distributed in the nucleus and the cytoplasm. They are involved in the processing of rRNA and mRNA and in gene regulation, and they play a pivotal role in mRNA processing.

  78. What is the acceptor arm of tRNA?
    Topic: Nucleic Acids & RNA

    The acceptor arm of tRNA is made up of an unpaired sequence of cytosine–cytosine–adenine (CCA) at the 3′ end. The 3′-OH group of the adenine binds with the carboxyl group of a specific amino acid and carries it to the ribosomes for protein synthesis.

  79. Which nitrogen bases are present in the TΨC arm of tRNA, and what is the function of this arm?
    Topic: Nucleic Acids & RNA

    The TΨC arm of tRNA is named after the sequence it contains: thymine, pseudouridine (Ψ) and cytosine — including the unusual modified bases thymine and pseudouridine. This arm helps in the binding of the tRNA to the ribosome during protein synthesis.

  80. What is hyperchromicity (in the denaturation of DNA)?
    Topic: Nucleic Acids & RNA

    Hyperchromicity is the increase in absorbance at 260 nm that occurs when double-stranded DNA is denatured (melted). Double-stranded DNA absorbs less ultraviolet light than single-stranded DNA or free bases, so separation of the strands increases the absorbance; this effect is used to follow the melting of DNA.

  81. What is denaturation of DNA?
    Topic: Nucleic Acids & RNA

    Denaturation of DNA is the separation of the two strands of the double helix when the hydrogen bonds between the paired bases are disrupted, for example by heating above about 80°C or by changes in pH or salt concentration. No covalent bonds are broken during denaturation.

  82. Under which conditions does renaturation of DNA take place?
    Topic: Nucleic Acids & RNA

    Renaturation (reannealing) of denatured DNA takes place when the separated single strands are kept under appropriate conditions of temperature and salt concentration, so that complementary strands reassociate by base pairing and the double helix reforms. This reassociation of complementary nucleic acid strands is also called hybridization.

  83. Which factors affect the melting temperature of DNA?
    Topic: Nucleic Acids & RNA

    The melting temperature (Tm) of DNA is affected by: (1) the G–C content — the higher the proportion of G≡C base pairs (held by three hydrogen bonds), the higher the Tm; (2) the length of the DNA molecule — longer molecules have higher Tm values; (3) the ionic strength (salt concentration) of the solution — higher salt shields the negatively charged phosphates and raises the Tm; and (4) the pH of the solution — extremes of pH disrupt hydrogen bonding and lower the Tm.

  84. Write the names of the components of eukaryotic ribosomes. Write about any two of them.
    Topic: Nucleic Acids & RNA

    The eukaryotic ribosome (80S) is made of two nucleoprotein subunits. The larger 60S subunit contains 28S, 5.8S and 5S rRNAs together with about 49 proteins; it carries the sites at which peptide bonds are formed during protein synthesis. The smaller 40S subunit contains 18S rRNA and about 33 proteins; it binds the mRNA and ensures the correct matching of codons with tRNA anticodons.

  85. What is atomic number and mass number?
    Topic: Introduction & Basic Concepts

    The atomic number (Z) of an element is the number of protons in the nucleus of its atoms; it determines the identity of the element. The mass number (A) is the total number of protons and neutrons in the nucleus. Atoms of the same element having different mass numbers (different numbers of neutrons) are called isotopes.

  86. Name five diseases caused by ion-channel defects.
    Topic: Introduction & Basic Concepts

    Diseases caused by defects (mutations) in ion channels — the channelopathies — include: (1) cystic fibrosis (defective CFTR chloride channel); (2) long QT syndrome (cardiac potassium/sodium channels); (3) myotonia congenita (skeletal-muscle chloride channel); (4) hypokalemic periodic paralysis (calcium/sodium channels); and (5) certain forms of epilepsy (neuronal ion channels).
