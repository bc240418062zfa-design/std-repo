  1. What is homeostasis? (Marks: 2)
    Topic: Introduction, Homeostasis and Feedback

    Homeostasis is the maintenance of a stable internal environment of the body despite changes in the external environment. Through feedback control systems, the body keeps variables such as temperature, pH, and water and ion balance within narrow limits. It is one of the central themes of physiology and provides insight into the patterns of physiological evolution and the adaptive value of physiological processes.

  2. What is negative feedback?
    Topic: Introduction, Homeostasis and Feedback

    Negative feedback is a reaction that causes a decrease in function. It occurs in response to some kind of stimulus. Often it causes the output of a system to be lessened, so the feedback tends to stabilize the system. This can be referred to as homeostasis in biology, or equilibrium in mechanics.

  3. Differentiate between positive and negative feedback.
    Topic: Introduction, Homeostasis and Feedback

    A positive feedback mechanism is the exact opposite of a negative feedback mechanism. With negative feedback, the output reduces the original effect of the stimulus, stabilizing the system. In a positive feedback system, the output enhances the original stimulus, amplifying the response. A good example of a positive feedback mechanism is blood clotting.

  4. What are the five major themes of physiology?
    Topic: Introduction, Homeostasis and Feedback

    The five major themes of physiology are: 1. Structure-function relationships. 2. Adaptation, acclimatization and acclimation. 3. Principle of homeostasis. 4. Feedback control systems. 5. Conformity and regulation. The major goals of the study of physiology are to explore the basic physiological processes, their evolution by selective forces, the patterns of physiological evolution, and the adaptive value of physiological processes.

  5. What is acclimatization?
    Topic: Introduction, Homeostasis and Feedback

    Acclimatization (acclimation) is the process in which an individual organism adjusts to a change in its environment, allowing it to maintain performance across a range of environmental conditions. Acclimatization occurs in a short period of time and within the organism's own lifetime.

  6. What are hormones? State their functions.
    Topic: Introduction, Homeostasis and Feedback

    Hormones are special chemical messengers in the body that are created in the endocrine glands. These messengers control most major bodily functions, from simple basic needs like hunger to complex systems like reproduction, and even the emotions and mood. Hormones are chemical substances that affect the activity of another part of the body (the target site). In essence, hormones serve as messengers, controlling and coordinating activities throughout the body.

  7. Is water an ion? Explain.
    Topic: Membrane and Nerve Physiology

    Water is not an ionic compound; it is a polar compound, having partial positive charges on the hydrogen atoms and a partial negative charge on oxygen. Due to these partial charges, water in certain respects behaves like ionic compounds, e.g. it splits into positive and negative ions, particularly in solutions.

  8. What is Donnan equilibrium?
    Topic: Membrane and Nerve Physiology

    Donnan equilibrium (Gibbs-Donnan equilibrium) is the behaviour of charged particles near a semipermeable membrane, which sometimes fail to distribute evenly across the two sides of the membrane because large charged particles (such as proteins) cannot cross it.

  9. Write a brief note on the signaling system of cAMP.
    Topic: Membrane and Nerve Physiology

    Many hormones use the adenylate cyclase-cAMP second messenger signaling system to stimulate their target tissues. The hormone binds to a receptor which is coupled to a G protein. The G protein stimulates adenylate cyclase in the membrane. Activated adenylate cyclase catalyzes the conversion of ATP into cAMP in the cytoplasm. cAMP then activates an enzyme called cAMP-dependent protein kinase. Protein kinase phosphorylates specific proteins in the cell, triggering a cascade of biochemical reactions that ultimately leads to the cell's response to the hormone.

  10. Differentiate between cGMP and cAMP.
    Topic: Membrane and Nerve Physiology

    Production of cGMP is catalyzed by the enzyme guanylate cyclase from GTP. Guanylate cyclase occurs in two forms: one bound to the membrane and one free in the cytoplasm. In contrast, adenylate cyclase is always bound to the membrane. Guanylate cyclase becomes active as the Ca2+ concentration is increased within the cell, while adenylate cyclase activity is increased when Ca2+ concentration is low. cGMP activates a specific protein kinase G instead of protein kinase A.

  11. What is an axon? State its importance.
    Topic: Membrane and Nerve Physiology

    An axon (nerve fiber) is a long, slender projection of a nerve cell (neuron) in vertebrates that typically conducts electrical impulses known as action potentials away from the nerve cell body. It is important because it transmits the nerve impulse from the cell body to the synapse, from where the signal is passed to the next neuron or effector, making long-distance communication in the nervous system possible.

  12. What are the phases of an action potential?
    Topic: Membrane and Nerve Physiology

    An action potential occurs when the membrane potential of a specific cell location rapidly rises and falls; this depolarization then causes adjacent locations to similarly depolarize. Its phases are: 1. Resting phase - the membrane is at its resting potential (about -70 mV). 2. Depolarization - voltage-gated Na+ channels open and Na+ rushes in, making the inside positive. 3. Repolarization - Na+ channels close and K+ channels open, so K+ flows out and the potential falls back. 4. Hyperpolarization (undershoot) - the potential briefly becomes more negative than resting before the Na+/K+ pump restores the resting state.

  13. Differentiate between neurogenic and myogenic pacemakers.
    Topic: Membrane and Nerve Physiology

    In myogenic hearts, the heartbeat is generated by specialized muscle cells within the heart itself. Two, three or four chambered hearts with muscular ventricles pump blood through the body with pressure and are known as pressure pumps; the specialized muscle cells later become myogenic or neurogenic. In neurogenic hearts, the rhythm of the heartbeat is imposed by the nervous system (cardiac ganglion cells) rather than by the heart muscle itself.

  14. What is a sensory receptor?
    Topic: Sensory Physiology

    A sensory receptor is a structure that reacts to a physical stimulus in the environment, whether internal or external. It is a sensory nerve ending that receives information and conducts a process of generating nerve impulses to be transmitted to the brain for interpretation and perception.

  15. What is sensory transduction?
    Topic: Sensory Physiology

    The physical or chemical energy of a stimulus is converted into electrical signals, i.e. nerve impulses, by sensory receptor cells. This conversion is called sensory transduction. All sensory transduction systems contain related molecules and operate through similar cellular mechanisms. They perform three basic functions: detection of the stimulus, amplification of the stimulus, and encoding of the stimulus.

  16. Define sensory adaptation. Give an example.
    Topic: Sensory Physiology

    Sensory adaptation is the process in which changes in the sensitivity of sensory receptors occur in relation to the stimulus. All senses are believed to experience sensory adaptation; however, some experimental psychologists say that the sense of pain does not experience this phenomenon. Example: when a person enters a room with a strong smell, the odor is very noticeable at first, but after some time it is no longer perceived because the olfactory receptors have adapted to the constant stimulus.

  17. What is labeled line coding?
    Topic: Sensory Physiology

    Each receptor subtype for taste sensations is connected to a particular set of axons in the nerve. In such an arrangement, information about one taste, e.g. sweetness, would be carried by some specific subset of axons. Such a pattern is called labeled line coding.

  18. Define range fractionation. Give its importance and two examples.
    Topic: Sensory Physiology

    In a sense organ, sensory receptors are arranged in an order of increasing sensitivity to different ranges of intensities of the stimulus. This hierarchical arrangement of receptors is known as range fractionation. Importance: it results in increasing the range of a multi-neuronal sensory system beyond the range of any individual receptor; it increases the overall precision of the sense organ; and it enables the CNS to discriminate stimulus intensities. Examples: (1) rods and cones of the retina cover different ranges of light intensity, extending the range of vision from dim to bright light; (2) touch and pressure receptors of different thresholds together allow a wide range of pressures to be discriminated.

  19. What is the function of the olfactory epithelium?
    Topic: Sensory Physiology

    The olfactory epithelium is a specialized epithelial tissue inside the nasal cavity that is involved in smell. In humans, it measures about 1 square centimetre (on each side) and lies on the roof of the nasal cavity about 7 cm above and behind the nostrils. The olfactory epithelium is the part of the olfactory system directly responsible for detecting odors.

  20. Write a note on stigma and ocelli.
    Topic: Sensory Physiology

    Stigma (eyespot): the simplest photoreceptive structure, found in some protozoa e.g. Euglena. It is a bright red colored organelle having carotenoid pigments. It gives a sense of light and dark and helps in phototaxis. Ocelli (eyecups): multicellular photoreceptive structures that consist of a cuplike depression containing photoreceptor cells. They are found in cnidarians and flatworms e.g. Planaria. They cannot form an image and provide the animal a sense of direction only.

  21. What are photoreceptors?
    Topic: Sensory Physiology

    A photoreceptor cell is a specialized type of neuroepithelial cell found in the retina that is capable of visual phototransduction. Photoreceptors are the cells in the retina that respond to light; their distinguishing feature is the presence of large amounts of a tightly packed membrane that contains the photopigment rhodopsin or a related molecule. The great biological importance of photoreceptors is that they convert light (visible electromagnetic radiation) into signals that can stimulate biological processes. There are two types of photoreceptor cells: rods and cones.

  22. What is color blindness? What causes it?
    Topic: Sensory Physiology

    The three types of opsins in color pigments are encoded by three genes. The gene encoding the opsin in the blue-cone pigment is located on an autosomal chromosome. The genes for the red and green-cone pigments are closely linked on the X chromosome. Color blindness is caused by a mutation in one of the cone opsin genes that results in the absence of one type of pigmented cones. A person missing a single type of color receptive cones is unable to distinguish some colors.

  23. Name the two types of stimuli.
    Topic: Sensory Physiology

    Stimuli are of two types: 1. External stimuli - stimuli of odor, touch, light, sound and gravitation. 2. Internal stimuli - stimuli of pain, homeostatic imbalances and blood pressure.

  24. Describe the role of the fibers of zonula and ciliary muscles.
    Topic: Sensory Physiology

    The fibers of zonula (suspensory ligaments) hold the lens in place. They exert an outward tension on the perimeter of the lens, keeping it flattened. Attached with the fibers of zonula are the ciliary muscles. When the ciliary muscles contract, the tension of the zonular fibers is released, so the elastic lens becomes more rounded (convex) and near objects are focused on the retina. When the ciliary muscles relax, the zonular tension increases and flattens the lens, focusing distant objects on the retina.

  25. Name the parts of the inner ear.
    Topic: Sensory Physiology

    The inner ear consists of a bony labyrinth which is filled with perilymph and has an oval window and a round window. The oval window receives sound vibrations through the stapes. In the bony labyrinth lies the membranous labyrinth, which is filled with endolymph. The inner ear includes the cochlea (for hearing) and the vestibular apparatus - utricle, saccule and three semicircular canals (for balance).

  26. What is the vestibular apparatus?
    Topic: Sensory Physiology

    Sensory information about motion, equilibrium and spatial orientation is provided by the vestibular apparatus, which in each ear includes the utricle, saccule and three semicircular canals. The utricle and saccule detect gravity (information in a vertical orientation) and linear movement.

  27. Define taste buds and taste receptors.
    Topic: Sensory Physiology

    Taste buds contain the taste receptor cells, which are also known as gustatory cells. A taste receptor is a type of receptor which facilitates the sensation of taste; these receptors are of four types (for the basic tastes). When food or other substances enter the mouth, molecules interact with saliva and, via small openings in the tongue epithelium called taste pores, come into contact with the taste receptors in the oral cavity and other locations.

  28. What are olfactory receptors?
    Topic: Sensory Physiology

    The olfactory receptor, also called the smell receptor, is a protein capable of binding odor molecules that plays a central role in the sense of smell (olfaction). The outer and inner ends of the receptor proteins involved in smell are connected by a chain of amino acids.

  29. Name and describe the two types of cilia found on hair cells.
    Topic: Sensory Physiology

    Hair cells of the inner ear bear two types of cilia: 1. Kinocilium - a single true cilium with the typical 9+2 microtubule arrangement, present on each hair cell. 2. Stereocilia - stiff, actin-filled, microvilli-like projections arranged in rows of increasing height next to the kinocilium. Bending of the stereocilia toward the kinocilium depolarizes the hair cell and increases neurotransmitter release, while bending away from it hyperpolarizes the cell.

  30. Write a note on Meissner's corpuscles and Pacinian corpuscles.
    Topic: Sensory Physiology

    Meissner's corpuscles are encapsulated nerve endings located in the dermal papillae of hairless skin, especially the fingertips and lips. They are rapidly adapting mechanoreceptors that detect light touch and low-frequency vibration. Pacinian (lamellar) corpuscles are large, onion-like layered receptors lying deep in the dermis and subcutaneous tissue. They are also rapidly adapting and respond to deep pressure and high-frequency vibration.

  31. Name the cells of the eye which are involved in vision.
    Topic: Sensory Physiology

    Cone cells (cones) are photoreceptor cells in the retinas of vertebrate eyes (e.g. the human eye). They respond differently to light of different wavelengths and are thus responsible for color vision; they function best in relatively bright light, as opposed to rod cells, which work better in dim light and give achromatic vision. Location: retina of mammals.

  32. What are the types of cells of the organ of Corti?
    Topic: Sensory Physiology

    The cells of the organ of Corti are of two groups: 1. Sensory (hair) cells - a single row of inner hair cells, which are the true sensory receptors for hearing, and three rows of outer hair cells. 2. Supporting cells - pillar cells, Deiters' cells, Hensen's cells and Claudius' cells, which support the hair cells on the basilar membrane, beneath the tectorial membrane. (Marks: 5)

  33. Differentiate between phasic and tonic receptors.
    Topic: Sensory Physiology

    Tonic receptors are slowly adapting receptors that keep firing as long as the stimulus is present, e.g. muscle spindles, baroreceptors and pain receptors; they inform the CNS about the duration and steady intensity of a stimulus. Phasic receptors are rapidly adapting receptors that respond mainly at the onset (and sometimes the offset) of a stimulus and then stop firing, e.g. Pacinian corpuscles and light touch receptors; they inform the CNS about changes in stimulus intensity. (Marks: 3)

  34. What is the mechanism of mechanoreception?
    Topic: Sensory Physiology

    Bending or stretching of the external structure of a mechanoreceptor generates tension that alters the permeability of the ion channels. This change in ion permeability alters the membrane potential, resulting in a depolarization or hyperpolarization, which is then transmitted as a nerve impulse.

  35. Which hormones are involved in salt and water maintenance?
    Topic: Endocrine Physiology

    Three major hormones are involved in regulating sodium and water balance in the body. 1. Mineralocorticoids - these have effects on mineral metabolism and act principally in maintaining salt and water balance in the body; the primary mineralocorticoid hormone is aldosterone. 2. ADH (antidiuretic hormone) from the posterior pituitary acts on the kidney to promote water reabsorption, thus preventing its loss in the urine.

  36. Name the glandular cells of the adenohypophysis and the hormones they produce.
    Topic: Endocrine Physiology

    The glandular (endocrine) cells of the adenohypophysis (anterior pituitary) are of five types: 1. Somatotropes - produce growth hormone (GH). 2. Corticotropes - produce adrenocorticotropic hormone (ACTH). 3. Thyrotropes - produce thyroid-stimulating hormone (TSH). 4. Gonadotropes - produce the gonadotropic hormones, luteinizing hormone (LH) and follicle stimulating hormone (FSH). 5. Lactotropes - produce prolactin (PRL).

  37. What are gonadotropins?
    Topic: Endocrine Physiology

    Luteinizing hormone (LH) and follicle-stimulating hormone (FSH) are called gonadotropins because they stimulate the gonads - in males, the testes, and in females, the ovaries. They are not necessary for life, but are essential for reproduction.

  38. What is the function of LH in males and females?
    Topic: Endocrine Physiology

    In females, an acute rise of LH (the LH surge) triggers ovulation and development of the corpus luteum. In males, where LH is also called interstitial cell-stimulating hormone (ICSH), it stimulates Leydig cell production of testosterone. It acts synergistically with FSH.

  39. What is the function of estrogen during pregnancy?
    Topic: Endocrine Physiology

    During pregnancy, estrogens exert mainly a proliferative function on the reproductive and associated organs of the mother.

  40. What are androgens? Which androgens are secreted by the adrenal cortex?
    Topic: Endocrine Physiology

    An androgen is any natural or synthetic steroid hormone that regulates the development and maintenance of male characteristics by binding to androgen receptors. The adrenal cortex also secretes small amounts of sex hormones, especially androgenic hormones. These androgens exhibit about the same effects in the body as the male sex hormone testosterone.

  41. Name the different types of cells of the islets of Langerhans.
    Topic: Endocrine Physiology

    The islets of Langerhans are endocrine. The islets contain four types of cells: 1. Beta cells (secrete insulin). 2. Alpha cells (secrete glucagon). 3. Delta cells (secrete somatostatin). 4. PP cells (secrete pancreatic polypeptide).

  42. Name the hormones of the thyroid gland.
    Topic: Endocrine Physiology

    The thyroid gland produces: 1. Thyroxine (T4) and 2. Triiodothyronine (T3), secreted by the follicular cells, which regulate the basal metabolic rate of the body; and 3. Calcitonin, secreted by the parafollicular (C) cells, which lowers blood calcium levels.

  43. Which disease is due to hypothyroidism?
    Topic: Endocrine Physiology

    Hypothyroidism results when the thyroid gland fails to produce enough hormones. Hypothyroidism may be due to a number of factors, including autoimmune disease. The most common cause of hypothyroidism is an autoimmune disorder known as Hashimoto's thyroiditis.

  44. Describe at least three developmental abnormalities that arise due to growth hormone.
    Topic: Endocrine Physiology

    Abnormalities due to growth hormone (GH) are: 1. Dwarfism - deficiency of GH in childhood reduces the growth of the body, producing a proportionate short stature. 2. Gigantism - excessive secretion of GH in childhood, before the epiphyseal plates close, causes excessive growth of the whole body. 3. Acromegaly - excessive GH secretion in adults, after epiphyseal closure, causes enlargement of the hands, feet, jaw and internal organs, though height does not increase. (Growth hormone deficiency itself may be congenital - present from birth due to genetic mutations or structural defects in the brain - or acquired later in life from trauma, infection, radiation therapy or tumor growth within the brain.)

  45. What are placental hormones?
    Topic: Endocrine Physiology

    The placenta is an endocrine gland that is only present during pregnancy. The hormones it produces include human chorionic gonadotropin (hCG), progesterone, estrogen, and human placental lactogen (hPL).

  46. Differentiate between exocrine and endocrine glands. Give examples.
    Topic: Endocrine Physiology

    Exocrine glands are glands that produce and secrete substances onto an epithelial surface by way of a duct, out of the body. Examples of exocrine glands include sweat, salivary, mammary, ceruminous, lacrimal, sebaceous and mucous glands. Endocrine glands are glands of the endocrine system that secrete their products, hormones, directly into the blood rather than through a duct. The major glands of the endocrine system include the pineal gland, pituitary gland, pancreas, ovaries, testes, thyroid gland, parathyroid gland, hypothalamus and adrenal glands.

  47. Compare the adrenal cortex and adrenal medulla.
    Topic: Endocrine Physiology

    The two portions of the mammalian adrenals have different cell types, functions and embryonic origins. The cells of the cortex are true endocrine cells, derived from non-neural, mesodermal tissue. The cells of the medulla are derived from neural tissue during embryonic development and are functionally related to the sympathetic nervous system. Thus, like the pituitary gland, each adrenal gland is a fused endocrine and neuroendocrine gland.

  48. Write a note on epinephrine and norepinephrine.
    Topic: Endocrine Physiology

    Epinephrine (adrenaline) and norepinephrine (noradrenaline) are the catecholamine hormones secreted by the adrenal medulla; norepinephrine also acts as a neurotransmitter of sympathetic nerve endings. Epinephrine increases heart rate and force of contraction, raises blood pressure, dilates the airways and increases blood glucose by promoting glycogen breakdown - preparing the body for fight or flight. Norepinephrine mainly causes vasoconstriction and thereby raises arterial blood pressure. Both are released during stress under sympathetic stimulation.

  49. Write a note on the structure of a sarcomere.
    Topic: Muscle Physiology

    Sarcomeres are composed of long, fibrous proteins as filaments that slide past each other when a muscle contracts or relaxes. Two of the important proteins are myosin, which forms the thick filament, and actin, which forms the thin filament. A sarcomere is the functional unit of a myofibril, lying between two Z lines; other bands (A band, I band, H zone) appear when the sarcomere is relaxed.

  50. Describe the structure of the sarcoplasmic reticulum.
    Topic: Muscle Physiology

    The sarcoplasmic reticulum (SR) is a membrane-bound structure found within muscle cells that is similar to the endoplasmic reticulum in other cells. Its main function is to store calcium ions. It is therefore vital that calcium ion levels are controlled tightly: Ca2+ can be released into the cell when necessary (for contraction) and then removed from the cell (for relaxation).

  51. Differentiate between concentric and eccentric contractions.
    Topic: Muscle Physiology

    In a concentric contraction, the muscle shortens while producing force, e.g. lifting a load by flexing the elbow. In an eccentric contraction, the muscle lengthens while it is under tension, e.g. lowering a load slowly or walking downhill; eccentric contractions resist the lengthening and control the movement. (Marks: 3)

  52. Differentiate between cardiac muscle and skeletal muscle.
    Topic: Muscle Physiology

    Cardiac and skeletal muscle are both striated in appearance, while smooth muscle is not. Cardiac muscle is an involuntary muscle, is more akin in structure to skeletal muscle, and is found only in the heart.

  53. What is the cardiac conduction system? Name its components.
    Topic: Cardiovascular Physiology

    The cardiac conduction system is a group of specialized cardiac muscle cells in the walls of the heart that generate rhythmic electrical impulses and send signals to the heart muscle causing it to contract. Its components are: 1. The SA node (pacemaker), which generates the impulse. 2. Atrial internodal pathways, which conduct the impulse from the SA node to the AV node. 3. The AV node, which conducts impulses into the ventricles. 4. The A-V bundle (bundle of His), with its left and right bundle branches, which conducts impulses from the atria into the ventricles. 5. Purkinje fibers, which branch off from the bundle branches and conduct the impulse to all parts of the ventricles.

  54. Name the types of blood vessels.
    Topic: Cardiovascular Physiology

    Three types of blood vessels carry blood during circulation: 1. Arteries, which carry blood away from the heart to organs and tissues. 2. Veins, which return blood to the heart. 3. Capillaries, which have thin walls formed of only a single layer of endothelial cells and permit exchange of materials between blood and tissues. (Marks: 5)

  55. What is the Fahraeus-Lindqvist effect?
    Topic: Cardiovascular Physiology

    The Fahraeus-Lindqvist effect is the phenomenon of plasma skimming and the resultant increased blood flow velocity in small vessels. It reduces the energy required to drive blood through the microcirculation. (Marks: 2)

  56. What are baroreceptors?
    Topic: Cardiovascular Physiology

    The baroreceptor system is a simple and rapidly acting control mechanism that contributes to the regulation of arterial blood pressure. Baroreceptors are widely distributed in the arterial system of vertebrates. The central cardiovascular system of amphibians, reptiles and mammals has unmyelinated baroreceptors, which respond only to pressures above normal, initiating reflexes that reduce arterial blood pressure. Mammals also have myelinated baroreceptors, which respond to blood pressures below normal; they are present in the mammalian carotid sinus, aortic arch, subclavian artery, pulmonary arteries and the wall of the right atrium. (Marks: 2)

  57. Describe the role of the nervous system in maintaining blood pressure.
    Topic: Cardiovascular Physiology

    Neural mechanisms respond to changes in arterial pressure or blood gas levels sensed at specific sites. When those changes occur, the cardiovascular centers in the medulla oblongata and pons adjust cardiac output and peripheral resistance to maintain blood pressure and ensure adequate blood flow.

  58. Name the local vasodilators.
    Topic: Cardiovascular Physiology

    Local vasodilators include: decreased tissue oxygen levels or increased CO2 levels; lactic acid or other acids generated by tissue cells; nitric oxide (NO) released from endothelial cells; rising concentrations of potassium ions or hydrogen ions in the interstitial fluid; and elevated local temperature.

  59. Name the mechanoreceptor fibers present in the atrial wall.
    Topic: Cardiovascular Physiology

    The atrial walls contain many mechanoreceptive afferent fibers. These include: myelinated A-type afferent fibers; myelinated B-type afferent fibers; unmyelinated C-type afferent fibers; and stretch-sensitive secretory cells.

  60. What are the types of vascular resistance?
    Topic: Cardiovascular Physiology

    Vascular resistance is the resistance that must be overcome to push blood through the circulatory system and create flow. It is of two types: 1. Systemic vascular resistance (SVR). 2. Pulmonary vascular resistance (PVR).

  61. Which factors affect systemic vascular resistance?
    Topic: Cardiovascular Physiology

    Factors affecting systemic vascular resistance (SVR) include: 1. Elasticity of the vessel wall. 2. Diameter of the vessel - decreasing vessel diameter (vasoconstriction) increases SVR, while increasing vessel diameter (vasodilation) decreases SVR. (Marks: 3)

  62. Describe the effect of gravity on arterial blood pressure.
    Topic: Cardiovascular Physiology

    Gravity affects the column of blood in the larger arteries, so pressure in the limbs and head region varies in different positions of the body. In the lying down position, the heart is at the same level as the limbs and head with respect to gravity, so blood pressure in the arteries of the head and limbs is equal. In a standing position, the heart is below the head and about a meter above the lower limbs, so the arterial pressure in the lower limbs increases while in the head region it decreases.

  63. Trace the pathway of systemic (peripheral) circulation.
    Topic: Cardiovascular Physiology

    The pathway followed by blood pumped from the left ventricle of the mammalian heart until its return to the right atrium is known as peripheral or systemic circulation. The oxygenated blood is pumped by the left ventricle into the aorta, the largest artery in the peripheral circulation. Branches from the aorta form the arterial system and supply blood to the organs and major body regions. Arteries branch to form capillary beds in the tissues, where oxygen is exchanged for carbon dioxide. The deoxygenated blood returns through the venous system to the superior and inferior vena cavae, which open into the right atrium.

  64. What are vasodilators?
    Topic: Cardiovascular Physiology

    Vasodilators are agents that open (dilate) blood vessels. They affect the muscles in the walls of arteries and veins, preventing the muscles from tightening and the walls from narrowing. As a result, blood flows more easily through the vessels.

  65. How is alveolar collapse prevented in the lungs?
    Topic: Respiration

    Alveoli are very small bubble-like structures that are inflated and deflated alternately. Continuous inflation and deflation may cause them to collapse (alveolar collapse), reducing the surface area available for gas exchange. Alveolar collapse normally does not occur for two reasons: 1. The surrounding tissue helps prevent overexpansion of alveoli. 2. The presence of pulmonary surfactant: due to surfactant on the surface of the alveoli, the wall tension increases when the surface film is expanded and decreases when it is compressed. When alveoli deflate and fold, the layer of surfactant between the folds becomes thick; this thick layer reduces surface tension and permits easy inflation of the folded alveoli. This effect also minimizes pressure differences between large and small alveoli, reducing the chance of collapse. (Marks: 2)

  66. What is hypoxia? How does it affect aquatic animals?
    Topic: Respiration

    Hypoxia is a condition in which the body or a region of the body is deprived of adequate oxygen supply. The oxygen level in the aquatic environment varies frequently, so aquatic animals are subjected to more frequent and rapid changes in oxygen levels. Many aquatic animals can withstand very long periods of hypoxia: many utilize a variety of anaerobic metabolic pathways to survive the period of reduced oxygen availability, and many adjust the respiratory and cardiovascular systems to maintain oxygen delivery. For example, many fishes increase gill ventilation rate in hypoxic conditions, and ram ventilators (e.g. tuna), which swim forward with their mouth open, increase the size of the gape during hypoxia to increase water flow over the gills. (Marks: 3)

  67. What is the function of gills in fishes?
    Topic: Respiration

    Gills are effective respiratory organs for gas exchange in water. They have a thin, moist and highly vascularized layer of epidermis that permits gas exchange through the water medium. Fishes live in an environment that contains less than 2.5% of the oxygen present in air. To maintain adequate levels of oxygen in their bloodstream, fishes must pass large quantities of water across the gill surfaces and extract the small amount of oxygen present in the water.

  68. What are the steps of gas transfer in animals?
    Topic: Respiration

    A gas-transfer system in animals takes in oxygen from the environment, takes it to the tissues through blood, brings CO2 from the tissues to the gas exchange surface and removes it by diffusion. It involves four basic steps: 1. Breathing movements, which assure a continual supply of air or water to the respiratory surface (e.g. lungs or gills). 2. Diffusion of O2 and CO2 across the respiratory epithelium. 3. Bulk transport of gases by the blood. 4. Diffusion of O2 and CO2 across capillary walls between blood and mitochondria in tissue cells.

  69. What is the composition of bile?
    Topic: Digestion and Excretion

    Bile consists of water and a weakly basic mixture of cholesterol, lecithin, inorganic salts, organic salts and bile pigments. The vertebrate liver secretes bile that does not contain any digestive enzymes; however, it contains many salts which are essential for the digestion of fats. The bile organic salts are manufactured by the liver from cholesterol and amino acids complexed with sodium. The bile pigments are derived from biliverdin and bilirubin, which are products of the breakdown of hemoglobin coming from ruptured red blood cells.

  70. Which hormones control the secretion of pancreatic juice?
    Topic: Digestion and Excretion

    Pancreatic secretions are controlled by a variety of hormones including peptide hormones, gastrin, cholecystokinin, somatostatin and enkephalins. Acid chyme reaching the small intestine stimulates the release of the peptide hormones secretin and vasoactive intestinal peptide (VIP), which stimulate secretion of pancreatic enzymes. Gastrin from the stomach lining elicits a small flow of pancreatic juice. Cholecystokinin, secreted in response to fatty acids and amino acids in the intestinal chyme, elicits secretion of pancreatic enzymes and also stimulates contraction of the gallbladder, forcing bile into the duodenum. Somatostatin inhibits gastric and pancreatic secretions, while enkephalins inhibit gastric acid secretion but stimulate pancreatic enzyme secretion.

  71. What are proenzymes (zymogens)? Give an example.
    Topic: Digestion and Excretion

    Certain digestive enzymes, in particular proteolytic enzymes, are synthesized, stored and released in an inactive molecular form known as a proenzyme or zymogen. Packaging of the enzyme in an inactive form prevents self-digestion of the releasing tissues/cells by the enzyme. The proenzyme is activated by the removal of a portion of the molecule, either by the action of another enzyme specific for this purpose or through a rise in ambient acidity. Example: pepsinogen is converted into pepsin.

  72. What are the types of stomach in vertebrates?
    Topic: Digestion and Excretion

    Based on the number of chambers, stomachs in vertebrates are classified as: 1. Monogastric stomachs - a single strong muscular tube or sac, found in carnivorous and omnivorous vertebrates. 2. Digastric stomachs - multichambered stomachs found in ruminant mammals of the order Artiodactyla, e.g. deer, giraffe, sheep, cattle, camel and llama. These stomachs have four chambers in two divisions: the first division consists of the fermentation chambers, rumen and reticulum; the second division comprises the omasum and abomasum (the true stomach). (Marks: 5)

  73. What are the uses of the crop?
    Topic: Digestion and Excretion

    In some animals, the esophagus contains a sac-like expanded section, the crop, which is used to store food before digestion. The crop is generally found in animals that feed infrequently; it allows quantities of food to be stored for digestion at a later time. Examples: many birds e.g. chicken, and leeches, which feed very infrequently with weeks or months between feeding periods. In some animals, crops are also used to ferment or digest foods for purposes other than the normal digestive process; for example, some parent birds prepare food in this way to be regurgitated for their nestlings, e.g. pigeon's milk.

  74. Describe the parts and functions of the colon.
    Topic: Digestion and Excretion

    The colon is about 1.5 m long and consists of four parts: ascending colon, transverse colon, descending colon and sigmoid colon. The colon receives the undigested part of the food along with inorganic ions and excess water. Ions and water are absorbed by the colon; as water is absorbed, indigestible wastes become increasingly solid and are converted into feces as they move along the colon by peristalsis. It takes approximately 12-24 hours for material to travel the length of the colon and reach the rectum for elimination through the anus.

  75. What is the function of the appendix?
    Topic: Digestion and Excretion

    Humans have a relatively small cecum with a finger-like extension, the appendix, which is about 9 cm long. The appendix has no role in digestion or absorption, but has an undefined role in immunity, as it has many lymph nodes.

  76. What is peristalsis?
    Topic: Digestion and Excretion

    The esophagus conducts the bolus by peristaltic movement. Peristaltic movements are the waves of contraction and relaxation in the smooth muscles of the alimentary canal walls. These movements start from the oral cavity and end at the distal end of the rectum.

  77. Name the accessory glands of the alimentary canal.
    Topic: Digestion and Excretion

    Accessory glands secrete digestive juices through ducts. The accessory glands of the mammalian digestive system are: three pairs of salivary glands, gastric glands, the pancreas, the liver, and the gallbladder.

  78. Describe the ornithine-urea cycle.
    Topic: Digestion and Excretion

    All vertebrates, except most teleost fishes, synthesize urea primarily in the liver via the ornithine-urea cycle. In this cycle, two ammonia and one CO2 molecule are added to ornithine to form arginine. Arginine is cleaved by the enzyme arginase to remove a molecule of urea and regenerate ornithine.

  79. What is the role of hepatocytes in nutrient transfer?
    Topic: Digestion and Excretion

    In the liver, much of the glucose is taken up by hepatocytes and, under the influence of insulin, is converted into glycogen granules for storage. Only a controlled quantity is released into the circulation to be utilized by the tissues.

  80. What is torpor? Give an example.
    Topic: Thermoregulation

    Small endotherms cannot maintain their high metabolic rates when they are not feeding during periods of inactivity. During those periods, some of these animals enter a state of torpor, in which body temperature and metabolic rate decrease. The lower basal metabolism lowers the rate of conversion of energy stores into body heat; thus it is advantageous to allow body temperature to decrease during periods of nonfeeding and inactivity. As the animal comes out of torpor and becomes active, it undergoes a burst of metabolic activity, through shivering or oxidation of brown fat, and its body temperature rises. Daily torpor is practiced by many terrestrial birds; the hummingbird is a classic example, allowing its body temperature to fall from a daytime level of about 40 °C to a nighttime level as low as 13 °C. Several species of small mammals also undergo torpor, e.g. shrews.

  81. What is panting?
    Topic: Thermoregulation

    Panting involves the use of the respiratory system to lose heat by evaporative cooling. It occurs in mammals, birds and some reptiles. During panting, mammals inhale through the nose and exhale through the mouth, exposing the tongue and other mouth structures to encourage further water evaporation and therefore heat loss. It also involves an increase in breathing rate and depth (hyperventilation).

  82. What is blubber? State its roles.
    Topic: Thermoregulation

    Blubber is a thick layer of fat (adipose tissue) directly under the skin of all marine mammals, e.g. seals, walruses, sirenians and cetaceans (whales). As fatty tissues are little metabolically active and have little blood vascularization, blubber is a good insulator because of its low thermal conductivity, and it prevents heat loss through the body surface.

  83. What is rete mirabile? Mention its function in the tuna fish.
    Topic: Thermoregulation

    The rete mirabile is a highly evolved system of countercurrent heat exchange, found widely in homeothermic and heterothermic vertebrates. The artery carrying warm blood toward an extremity is completely encased in a circlet of veins carrying back cold blood. In the tuna fish, the large number of rete mirabile form an extensive heat exchange surface between inflowing and outflowing blood and are used to regulate the temperature of the brain, muscles and eyes. Swim bladders of some fishes (e.g. eels) are associated with rete mirabile and function as CO2 exchangers. (Marks: 5)

  84. State the principle of countercurrent exchangers.
    Topic: Thermoregulation

    Countercurrent exchangers work by: 1. Regulating the extent of blood flow near the body surface. 2. Trapping heat within the body core.

  85. What are the three methods of surviving freezing?
    Topic: Thermoregulation

    Animals survive freezing by three methods: 1. Ice-nucleating agents (INAs) - substances in the extracellular fluid (inorganic substances, microorganisms, proteins, organic residues) that accelerate ice crystal formation outside the cells; as ice forms outside, water is drawn out of the cells, lowering the intracellular freezing point and protecting the cells, e.g. larvae of the midge Chironomus survive freezing at -32 °C. 2. Cryoprotectants (antifreeze substances) - e.g. glycerol, sorbitol and antifreeze proteins, which lower the freezing point of the body fluids. 3. Supercooling - the body fluids are cooled below the freezing point yet remain unfrozen because ice-nucleating agents are kept out, e.g. larvae of the parasitic wasp Bracon cephi can supercool to -47 °C.

  86. Describe the physiological adaptations of ectotherms in a hot environment.
    Topic: Thermoregulation

    Most ectotherms have high heat conductance that allows them to radiate heat rapidly on moving to less warm places. Above 40 °C, many reptiles start panting, just like birds and mammals, to increase heat loss through respiratory evaporation, which causes cooling. The marine iguanas can regulate heart rate and flow of blood to the surface tissues to either absorb or eliminate heat. The Gila monster (Heloderma suspectum) evaporates water from its cloaca to cool its internal body temperature - a process similar to sweating in mammals. (Marks: 5)

  87. What are the objectives of ethology?
    Topic: Animal Behavior

    Ethology is the scientific study of animal behavior in the natural environment. Its objectives are: 1. Understanding the stimuli that elicit behavior. 2. Understanding the physiological mechanisms that mediate the response. 3. Understanding how an animal's experience during growth and development influences the response. 4. Understanding how the behavior aids in survival and reproduction. 5. Understanding the behavior's evolutionary history.

  88. What is animal orientation? Describe its types with examples.
    Topic: Animal Behavior

    Orientation is the ability of an animal to determine its position in space and move predictably with respect to specific stimuli. Its types are: 1. Kinesis - a change in activity or turning rate in response to a stimulus, without direction; e.g. sow bugs become more active in dry areas and less active in humid areas, so they tend to leave dry areas and encounter moist areas. 2. Taxis - an oriented movement toward (positive taxis) or away from (negative taxis) a stimulus; e.g. river fishes swim upstream against the current, and cockroaches move away from light (negative phototaxis), while an animal turning toward light shows positive phototaxis.

  89. What is kin selection? Give an example.
    Topic: Animal Behavior

    Kin selection is an altruistic evolutionary strategy that favors the reproductive success of an organism's close relatives, even at a cost to the organism's own survival and reproduction. It is an instance of inclusive fitness, which combines the number of offspring produced by an individual itself and the number it can ensure by supporting close relatives (siblings). William Hamilton proposed the idea of kin selection. For kin selection to be effective, individuals must be able to identify their relatives, so it is found in animals with advanced social structures, i.e. primates and social insects. Example: parents sacrificing their own well-being to produce and aid offspring increases the parents' fitness because it maximizes their genetic representation in the population.

  90. What is an aggregation? How does it differ from an animal society?
    Topic: Animal Behavior

    An aggregation is any form of gathering of animals, or the process of coming together, with minimal interaction between its members, e.g. several Drosophila flies living on a piece of rotting fruit. In contrast, a stable group of individuals of the same species that maintains a close-knit cooperative social relationship is called an animal society; this association typically has complex social organization and extends beyond the level of mating and taking care of the young. Such societies are observable both in invertebrates and vertebrates.

  91. What are cognitive maps? Give an example.
    Topic: Animal Behavior

    Some animals guide their activity by a cognitive map, a representation in the nervous system of the spatial relationships between objects in the animal's surroundings. Rather than relying solely on moving from landmark to landmark, animals using cognitive maps can navigate more flexibly and efficiently by relating landmark positions to one another. Example: the Clark's nutcracker birds store thousands of pine seeds in fall at thousands of hiding places (caches) distributed over an area as large as 35 km2, and during the winter they relocate many of their caches.

  92. What are pheromones? Describe their roles.
    Topic: Animal Behavior

    Pheromones are chemical messengers released by many animals into the environment to communicate; they spread through air, affect the behavior of individuals of the same species, are effective at remarkably low concentrations, have particular odors, and are detected by the olfactory receptors of the receiving animal. Their roles are: 1. Recognition of members of the species, e.g. insects identifying members of their colony. 2. Reproductive roles - attracting the opposite sex for breeding, inducing courtship and spawning behaviors. 3. Territorial behavior - many male mammals mark their territories with pheromones to warn other males. 4. Insect social order - in a honeybee colony, pheromones produced by the queen maintain the hive's complex social order. 5. Alarm - pheromones produced on injury warn other members of danger. 6. Repelling predators - e.g. skunks produce foul-smelling musk that makes them unpalatable to enemies. (Marks: 10)

  93. What is parental care?
    Topic: Animal Behavior

    Parental care caters to the needs of the newborn and young offspring. It is more evolved in animals whose newborns and young cannot care for themselves. It has therefore been an important factor in the evolution of mating systems.

  94. What are chemoreceptors?
    Topic: Animal Behavior

    Chemoreceptors are receptor cells that are specialized for acquiring information about the chemical environment and transmitting it to other neurons. They can be divided into two categories: gustatory (taste) receptors and olfactory (smell) receptors. Three types of chemoreceptors monitor changes in O2, CO2 and pH levels in arterial blood: peripheral chemoreceptors, central chemoreceptors, and type-J chemoreceptors.
