  1. What is the common thread that runs through the functions, roles, skills, and activities approaches to management? (3)
    Topic: Core Subjective Topics

    The common thread is the manager himself. Whether management is studied through its functions (planning, organizing, leading, controlling), through the roles managers play, through the skills they need, or through the activities they actually perform during the day, every approach is describing the same reality from a different angle: what managers do and how they get things done through other people. All four approaches agree that a manager's job is to achieve organizational goals effectively and efficiently by working with and through people and other organizational resources.

  2. What are the common skills exhibited by an effective manager? (10)
    Topic: Core Subjective Topics

    A skill is an ability to act in a way that allows a person to perform highly in his or her role. Managers need three types of skills: 1. Technical skills: job-specific knowledge and techniques required to perform an organizational role. 2. Human skills: the ability to understand, work with, lead, and control the behavior of other individuals and groups.

  3. Conceptual skills: the ability to analyze and diagnose a situation and to distinguish between cause and effect. Effective managers need all three types of skill, although their relative importance changes with the managerial level: technical skills matter most at the first line, human skills are important at every level, and conceptual skills become most critical at the top.

  4. How does globalization affect a manager's skills? (3)
    Topic: Core Subjective Topics

    Globalization means a manager now works with people from different cultures, values, and communication styles, often across different countries and time zones. A manager therefore needs stronger human and conceptual skills: cultural sensitivity and adaptability, the ability to communicate across cultures and languages, an understanding of different laws, business practices, and management styles, and the ability to manage a diverse workforce. Managers who cannot adjust their style to different cultural contexts are likely to fail in global assignments.

  5. Which biographical characteristics best predict productivity, absenteeism, turnover, and satisfaction? (10)
    Topic: Core Subjective Topics

    The biographical characteristics studied in OB are age, gender, marital status, and tenure. - Productivity: Age has little effect on productivity; older workers are not less productive. Tenure is a better predictor: employees with longer service generally show higher productivity because of experience. - Absenteeism: Tenure is negatively related to absenteeism; long-service employees are absent less (except unavoidable absence). Gender differences are small, though women historically showed slightly higher rates. - Turnover: Tenure is the strongest predictor; the longer an employee has stayed, the less likely he or she is to quit. Married employees also show lower turnover. - Satisfaction: Tenure is positively related to job satisfaction; satisfaction tends to increase with length of service. Age also shows a generally positive relationship with satisfaction.

  6. What are skill-based pay plans? (3)
    Topic: Core Subjective Topics

    In a skill-based pay plan, employees are paid not for the job they hold but for the number of skills they have mastered and the number of jobs they are capable of performing. Pay increases as the employee learns new skills and becomes able to perform more tasks. Such plans encourage learning, flexibility, and cross-training, increase the workforce's ability to fill in for absent employees, and can raise satisfaction, although employees may become frustrated once they have learned all the skills the plan rewards.

  7. What are values? (3)
    Topic: Core Subjective Topics

    Values represent basic convictions that a specific mode of conduct or end-state of existence is personally or socially preferable to an opposite or converse mode of conduct or end-state of existence. Values contain a judgmental element because they carry an individual's ideas of what is right, good, or desirable. They are relatively stable, are largely formed early in life through family, culture, religion, and education, and they form the foundation for attitudes and behavior. Rokeach classified values into terminal values (desirable end-states, e.g., a comfortable life, social recognition) and instrumental values (preferable modes of behavior, e.g., honesty, ambition).

  8. Discuss the importance of values. (3)
    Topic: Core Subjective Topics

    Values are important to the study of organizational behavior for several reasons. First, they lay the foundation for the understanding of attitudes and motivation, because what people value influences what they perceive and how they behave. Second, values cloud objectivity and rationality: people enter organizations with preconceived notions of what ought and ought not to be, and these affect their decisions. Third, values influence managers' behavior toward employees, their choice of leadership style, and their ethical choices. Finally, matching employee values with organizational values improves satisfaction, commitment, and retention.

  9. Compare Hofstede's cultural dimensions with the GLOBE framework. (10)
    Topic: Core Subjective Topics

    Hofstede's framework assesses cultures on five dimensions: 1. Power distance: the degree to which people accept that power is distributed unequally. 2. Individualism versus collectivism: whether people prefer to act as individuals or remain in tight-knit groups. 3. Quantity of life versus quality of life: valuing assertiveness, money, and acquisition versus valuing relationships and concern for the welfare of others. 4. Uncertainty avoidance: the degree to which people prefer structured over unstructured situations. 5. Long-term versus short-term orientation: valuing thrift and persistence versus tradition and the present. The GLOBE framework extends this research with nine dimensions, including future orientation, assertiveness, uncertainty avoidance, power distance, performance orientation, and humane orientation. The two frameworks overlap considerably: GLOBE's future orientation corresponds to Hofstede's long-term orientation, GLOBE's assertiveness corresponds to Hofstede's quantity of life, and uncertainty avoidance and power distance appear in both. GLOBE goes further by also measuring performance orientation and humane orientation, and by distinguishing cultural practices ('as is') from cultural values ('should be').

  10. Social recognition is a terminal value of a person. What are the possible instrumental values to achieve this terminal value? (5)
    Topic: Core Subjective Topics

    Terminal values are the end-states a person wants to achieve, while instrumental values are the modes of behavior used to reach them. To achieve social recognition, a person would rely on instrumental values such as: being ambitious (working hard and aspiring), being capable (competent and effective), being courageous (standing up for one's beliefs), being helpful (working for the welfare of others), being honest (sincere and truthful), being responsible (dependable and reliable), being polite (courteous and well-mannered), and being intellectual. Consistently behaving in these ways earns respect and recognition from society.

  11. Describe the three determinants of personality. (3)
    Topic: Core Subjective Topics

    Personality is determined by three factors: 1. Heredity: the characteristics inherited from parents at conception, such as physique, temperament, energy level, and biological rhythms. Research on twins shows heredity accounts for a large share of personality. 2. Environment: the culture in which a person is raised, family norms, social groups, and other life experiences, which shape values, attitudes, and behavior patterns. 3. Situation: the immediate context; a person's behavior changes with situational constraints, so personality predicts behavior better in weak situations than in strong ones.

  12. Explain the importance of emotional intelligence (EI) and its five key components. (10)
    Topic: Core Subjective Topics

    Emotional intelligence is a person's ability to detect and manage emotional cues and information: to perceive emotions in oneself and others, understand them, and regulate them. EI is important because jobs increasingly involve teamwork, customer contact, and leadership, all of which demand social interaction; people high in EI perform better in such jobs, make better team members and leaders, and handle stress and conflict more effectively. Its five key components are: 1. Self-awareness: being aware of what you are feeling. 2. Self-management: the ability to manage one's own emotions and impulses. 3. Self-motivation: the ability to persist in the face of setbacks and failures. 4. Empathy: the ability to sense how others are feeling. 5. Social skills: the ability to handle the emotions of others and manage relationships.

  13. "Optimistic people tend to interpret their surroundings more positively than those who are pessimistic." Do you agree with this statement or not? Discuss. (5)
    Topic: Core Subjective Topics

    Yes, I agree. Perception is not a passive recording of reality; it is influenced by factors in the perceiver, including attitudes, personality, motives, interests, past experiences, and expectations. Optimism and pessimism are personality-related outlooks (closely linked to positive and negative affectivity). An optimistic person expects favorable outcomes, notices positive cues, and interprets ambiguous events in a positive light, while a pessimistic person attends to negative cues and interprets the same events unfavorably. This is why two employees can perceive the same manager, job, or change in completely different ways: the perceiver's characteristics color what is perceived.

  14. Which factors of the perceiver could affect the perception process? (3)
    Topic: Core Subjective Topics

    The characteristics of the perceiver that influence perception include: 1. Attitudes: a perceiver with a positive or negative attitude toward a target interprets it accordingly. 2. Motives: unsatisfied needs and motives direct attention toward things that promise satisfaction. 3. Interests: people notice what interests them and ignore the rest. 4. Past experiences: previous encounters create expectations that shape present perception. 5. Expectations: people see what they expect to see, which can create self-fulfilling effects. Personality traits such as optimism, pessimism, and locus of control also color perception.

  15. A decision-making style is the combination of an individual's perception and his response to certain information. Explain the different decision-making styles exhibited by managers. (5)
    Topic: Core Subjective Topics

    Decision-making styles differ along two dimensions: value orientation (concern for task versus people) and tolerance for ambiguity. Their combination produces four styles: 1. Directive style: low tolerance for ambiguity and a rational way of thinking. Such managers are efficient and logical, make fast decisions, and focus on the short run. 2. Analytical style: high tolerance for ambiguity and a rational way of thinking. These managers carefully consider many alternatives and much information and adapt well to new situations. 3. Conceptual style: high tolerance for ambiguity and an intuitive way of thinking. These managers take a broad, long-term view, consider many alternatives, and are creative problem solvers. 4. Behavioral style: low tolerance for ambiguity and an intuitive way of thinking. These managers work well with others, are concerned about people's achievements, are receptive to suggestions, and avoid conflict.

  16. It is argued that most of the time managers use the rational decision-making model when risk is high. Do you agree with this statement or not? Give logical reasons to support your answer. (5)
    Topic: Core Subjective Topics

    I do not agree. The rational model assumes the decision maker has complete information, can identify all alternatives and their consequences, and can choose the alternative that maximizes the outcome. In practice these assumptions rarely hold, and least of all when risk is high: under high risk the probabilities of outcomes are uncertain, information is incomplete, and time and cost constraints bind. Real managers therefore rely on bounded rationality: they simplify the problem, consider a limited number of alternatives, and satisfice, choosing the first alternative that is good enough. Under high risk and uncertainty they also lean more heavily on intuition, experience, and judgment. The rational model describes how decisions ought ideally to be made, not how managers actually make them.

  17. Different cultures have different ways of decision making. How do cultural differences affect the decision making of managers? Discuss. (5)
    Topic: Core Subjective Topics

    The rational model makes no acknowledgment of cultural differences. We need to recognize that the cultural background of the decision maker can have significant influence on: (a) selection of problems, (b) depth of analysis, (c) the importance placed on logic and rationality, and (d) whether organizational decisions should be made autocratically by an individual manager or collectively in groups. Cultures also differ in terms of time orientation, the importance of rationality, their belief in the ability of people to solve problems, and preference for collective decision making.

  18. List and explain the three groups of core needs in ERG theory. (3)
    Topic: Core Subjective Topics

    Clayton Alderfer's ERG theory groups human needs into three categories: 1. Existence needs: the basic material requirements of life, corresponding to Maslow's physiological and safety needs: food, shelter, pay, and working conditions. 2. Relatedness needs: the desire for meaningful interpersonal relationships, corresponding to Maslow's social needs and the external part of esteem needs. 3. Growth needs: the intrinsic desire for personal development, corresponding to Maslow's internal esteem needs and self-actualization. Unlike Maslow, ERG theory allows more than one need to operate at the same time, and if a higher-level need is frustrated, the desire to satisfy a lower-level need increases (frustration-regression).

  19. How does ERG theory differ from Maslow's hierarchy of needs? (10)
    Topic: Core Subjective Topics

    Maslow's theory describes five levels of needs, from bottom to top: (1) physiological needs (food, existence, shelter), (2) safety needs (feeling safe and protected from environmental hazards), (3) social needs (belongingness and social relationships), (4) esteem needs (recognition and respect in society), and (5) self-actualization needs (reaching one's full potential). Alderfer's ERG theory condenses these into three groups: existence needs (Maslow's physiological and safety needs), relatedness needs (social and external esteem needs), and growth needs (internal esteem and self-actualization). The major difference is in how the needs operate. According to Maslow, a lower-level need must be substantially satisfied before the next higher need becomes important; once satisfied, a need no longer motivates. According to Alderfer, this strict hierarchy does not hold: the priority of needs differs from person to person, more than one need can motivate at the same time, and if a person is frustrated in satisfying a higher-level need, a lower-level need re-emerges as a motivator (frustration-regression). This was the biggest weakness of Maslow's assumption that lower-level needs must be fulfilled before higher-level needs can motivate.

  20. How do unsatisfied needs energize us towards goal achievement? (3)
    Topic: Core Subjective Topics

    Motivation begins with an unsatisfied need, which creates tension within the individual. This tension drives the person to search for goals that, if attained, will satisfy the need and reduce the tension. The greater the tension, the more effort the person is willing to exert. So unsatisfied needs energize behavior: they direct the individual's effort toward goal achievement, and once the goal is reached and the need satisfied, the tension drops and the motivating force of that need declines until another need becomes dominant.

  21. Explain the relationship of motivation with performance and satisfaction. (5)
    Topic: Core Subjective Topics

    Motivation is one of the key determinants of performance, along with ability and the work environment: performance is a function of ability, motivation, and opportunity. A motivated employee directs more effort, and effort of good quality, toward the job, so motivation generally raises performance, although even a highly motivated employee cannot perform well without the needed ability and resources. The relationship between motivation and satisfaction runs in both directions. When effort leads to performance and performance leads to rewards the employee values, satisfaction results (as in Porter and Lawler's model). Satisfied employees are then more committed and more willing to exert effort in the future. However, satisfaction by itself does not guarantee performance; it is the link between performance and valued rewards that sustains motivation.

  22. Motivation is based on the perception of equity and fairness. Do you agree with the statement or not? Discuss it in the light of equity theory. (5)
    Topic: Core Subjective Topics

    Motivation is based on the worker's perception of the work situation. Yes, I fully agree with this statement. Equity occurs when an individual's outcome/input ratio equals that of the referent. Because the comparison of these ratios (rather than absolute levels) determines whether equity is perceived, equity can exist even if the referent receives more than the person making the comparison. When workers perceive the ratios to be equal, they are motivated to maintain the status quo or to increase inputs to receive greater outcomes. Unequal ratios result in tension and a desire to restore equity. Overpayment inequity occurs when an individual perceives his or her outcome/input ratio to be greater than the referent's; underpayment inequity occurs when the individual perceives his or her ratio to be less than the referent's. In either case the individual is motivated to restore equity: by changing inputs or outcomes, distorting perceptions, choosing a different referent, or leaving the field.

  23. How does an employee stock ownership program positively influence employee motivation? (5)
    Topic: Core Subjective Topics

    Employee stock ownership plans can be used to keep plan participants focused on company performance and share price appreciation. By giving plan participants an interest in seeing that the company's stock performs well, these plans are believed to encourage participants to do what's best for shareholders, since the participants themselves are shareholders. Ownership creates a sense of psychological ownership and organizational commitment, links the employee's own wealth to organizational outcomes, and motivates employees to work harder, stay longer, and protect the company's long-term performance.

  24. Explain the ways through which a manager can enhance goal acceptance among employees. (5)
    Topic: Core Subjective Topics

    Goal-setting theory shows that specific, difficult goals improve performance only when employees accept them. A manager can enhance goal acceptance by: 1. Participation: involving employees in setting the goals, so they feel ownership of them. 2. Making goals specific and clear, so employees know exactly what is expected. 3. Keeping goals challenging but attainable; goals perceived as impossible are rejected. 4. Providing regular feedback on progress toward the goals. 5. Linking goal achievement to valued rewards. 6. Showing visible commitment and support from top management, and explaining the purpose and importance of the goals. 7. Building employees' self-efficacy through training and encouragement, so they believe the goals are within their capability.

  25. Suppose you are the HR manager of a company that has a chain of restaurants all over Pakistan. At your restaurant every employee is assigned a specific task: one person serves drinks and soups, one person serves the vegetables, and one person has the duty of dish washing every day. Every person has got specialization in his task; however, tardiness and absenteeism has increased. Which strategy of job redesign will help the workers to enhance their motivation level? Give logical reasons to support your answer. (5)
    Topic: Core Subjective Topics

    The appropriate strategy is job rotation, supported by job enlargement. The present system is extreme work specialization: each employee repeats one narrow task every day. Repetition and monotony produce boredom and dissatisfaction, which show up as tardiness and absenteeism. Job rotation periodically moves employees from one task to another (serving drinks, serving food, washing dishes, cash counter), which reduces monotony, widens skills, and makes the work more interesting; it also gives the restaurant flexibility when someone is absent. Job enlargement adds more tasks at the same level to each job, increasing task variety. Together these redesign strategies raise the motivating potential of the jobs, reduce boredom-driven withdrawal behavior, and should bring tardiness and absenteeism down.

  26. Compare and contrast command, task, interest, and friendship groups by giving practical examples. (10)
    Topic: Core Subjective Topics

    A command group is determined by the organization chart: it consists of subordinates who report directly to a common manager. Example: a branch manager and the eight officers reporting to her form a command group. A task group is formed to complete a specific job or project and dissolves when the task ends; its members may come from different departments. Example: a committee formed to organize the company's annual conference. An interest group is formed by people who share a common interest or goal, whether or not they work together. Example: employees who join together to press management for better transport facilities. A friendship group develops because members share common characteristics such as age, background, or beliefs, and it often extends beyond the work situation. Example: a group of colleagues who play cricket together after office hours. The contrast is that command and task groups are formal groups created by the organization, while interest and friendship groups are informal groups that emerge naturally; command groups are permanent, task groups temporary, and informal groups last as long as the shared interest or friendship lasts.

  27. Group formation can meet both organizational and personal goals. How do groups help organizations and individuals in their goal achievement? (5)
    Topic: Core Subjective Topics

    Groups help organizations because they get work done that individuals cannot do alone: they pool skills, knowledge, and effort, allow division of labor and specialization, generate more ideas and better decisions, and help implement decisions because members who participate in a decision are committed to it. Groups also socialize new employees and transmit organizational culture. Groups help individuals by satisfying their social and belongingness needs, providing security and support, building self-esteem and status, giving them power they would not have alone, and helping them achieve goals, such as completing a difficult task or protecting shared interests, that they could not achieve individually.

  28. What is cohesiveness? (3)
    Topic: Core Subjective Topics

    Group cohesiveness is the degree to which members are attracted to each other and are motivated to stay in the group; it is the 'we feeling' that binds members together. Cohesiveness increases with smaller group size, more time spent together, greater difficulty of entry, external threats, and past success. Cohesive groups communicate more, support each other, and enforce group norms more strongly.

  29. "High cohesiveness in a group leads to higher group productivity." Do you agree with this statement or not? Discuss. (5)
    Topic: Core Subjective Topics

    I agree only partly: cohesiveness alone does not guarantee higher productivity. Research shows the effect of cohesiveness on productivity depends on the group's performance norms. If cohesiveness is high and performance norms are also high, productivity is high. If cohesiveness is high but performance norms are low, productivity is low, because members conform to the group's low standards and may even restrict output. If cohesiveness is low and performance norms are high, productivity rises but less than in the high-cohesiveness case. So management must build cohesiveness together with high performance norms, clear goals, and accountability.

  30. How does the size of a group affect its productivity? (3)
    Topic: Core Subjective Topics

    Group size affects productivity in opposing ways. Larger groups pool more knowledge, skills, and viewpoints, which helps problem solving. But as size increases, coordination losses grow and social loafing appears: individual effort falls because responsibility is diffused and individual contribution becomes less visible. Evidence shows smaller groups (around five to seven members) complete tasks faster and are more productive per member, while groups of twelve or more are better for gathering diverse input and fact-finding. Beyond a point, adding members reduces per-person productivity even if total output rises slightly.

  31. What is the difference between groupthink and groupshift? (3)
    Topic: Core Subjective Topics

    Groupthink is the tendency of the members of a group to yield to the desire for consensus or unanimity at the cost of considering alternative courses of action. Groupthink is said to be the reason why intelligent and knowledgeable people sometimes make disastrous decisions. Groupshift is different: when people are in groups, they make decisions about risk differently from when they are alone. In a group they are likely to make riskier decisions, because the shared risk makes the individual risk seem less; discussion also makes members more confident in their initial position. In short, groupthink is about suppressing dissent to preserve agreement, while groupshift is about the group's decision becoming more extreme (usually riskier) than the members' individual decisions.

  32. What possible benefits can group decision making provide to an organization? Also discuss its drawbacks. (5)
    Topic: Core Subjective Topics

    Benefits: groups bring more complete information and knowledge to a problem; they generate a greater diversity of viewpoints and more alternatives; decisions made by groups enjoy greater acceptance and legitimacy because those affected participated in them; and participation increases commitment to implementing the decision. Drawbacks: group decision making is time-consuming; a dominant minority or a single strong personality can control the outcome; social pressure produces conformity and groupthink; responsibility for the decision becomes ambiguous because no single person is accountable; and conflicts within the group can delay or distort the decision.

  33. Describe the situations under which a company or a manager should use teams. (3)
    Topic: Core Subjective Topics

    A manager should use teams when the work is challenging and complex and cannot be performed well by one person alone: when the task requires diverse knowledge, skills, and experience, when different units or functions must coordinate, when creativity and multiple viewpoints are needed, and when the work is interdependent so that members must continuously adjust to each other. Teams are also appropriate for critical tasks where meeting objectives and targets depends on collective effort and shared responsibility.

  34. How can an organization build effective teams for different tasks? (5)
    Topic: Core Subjective Topics

    To build an effective team, a manager needs to understand the individual strengths each person brings to the team, keep those strengths in mind while selecting members, and allocate work assignments accordingly. The main points a manager should keep in mind are: 1. Size of the team: very small teams are likely to lack diversity of views, while large teams have difficulty getting much done; the best range is about 5 to 12 members. 2. Member flexibility: members who can perform each other's tasks improve adaptability and make the team less dependent on any single member. 3. Leadership and structure: agreeing on the specifics of work and how members' skills fit together requires team leadership and structure; self-managed teams often perform better than teams without it. 4. Climate of trust: members should trust each other and their leaders, which helps them take risks and commit to the leader's goals. 5. Conflict: without conflict a team is likely to become apathetic and stagnant; on non-routine tasks, disagreement about task content is often beneficial because it lessens the likelihood of groupthink. 6. Accountability: effective teams counter social loafing by holding themselves accountable at both the individual and the team level.

  35. What are the different strategies that an organization can adopt to transform its employees from individuals to team players? (5)
    Topic: Core Subjective Topics

    Organizations can turn individuals into team players through three main strategies: 1. Selection: hiring people who already have the interpersonal skills and disposition to work in teams, and screening out those who cannot function as team members. 2. Training: workshops and exercises that develop problem-solving, communication, negotiation, conflict-management, and coaching skills, so individual specialists learn to share information, trust others, and put the team above personal preferences. 3. Rewards: redesigning the reward system to encourage cooperative rather than competitive behavior, for example team-based performance bonuses, profit sharing, and recognition of collaborative achievements, while still recognizing individual contributions so social loafing does not appear.

  36. Describe the communication process and explain the seven parts of the communication process model. (10)
    Topic: Core Subjective Topics

    The communication process consists of seven parts: 1. The sender: the person who initiates the communication because he or she has an idea or thought to convey. 2. Encoding: the sender converts the idea into a message by putting it into words, symbols, or gestures. 3. The message: the actual physical product of the encoding: what is said, written, or signaled. 4. The channel: the medium through which the message travels, such as a face-to-face conversation, telephone, memo, or email, chosen by the sender. 5. Decoding: the receiver translates the message into a form he or she understands. 6. The receiver: the person for whom the message is intended. 7. Feedback: the receiver's response, which tells the sender whether the message was received and understood as intended. Noise, anything that interferes with transmission or understanding, can distort the message at any stage.

  37. List and explain the different barriers to effective communication. (5)
    Topic: Core Subjective Topics

    The main barriers to effective communication are: 1. Filtering: the sender deliberately manipulates information so it appears more favorable to the receiver. 2. Selective perception: the receiver sees and hears selectively according to his or her needs, background, and interests. 3. Information overload: when information exceeds a person's processing capacity, some of it is ignored or forgotten. 4. Emotions: how the receiver feels when the message arrives affects how it is interpreted. 5. Language: words mean different things to different people because of age, education, and cultural background; jargon also excludes outsiders. 6. Communication apprehension: undue tension or anxiety about oral or written communication that distorts messages. 7. Gender differences and politically correct communication can also create misunderstandings.

  38. How can the barriers of communication be minimized or eliminated? (3)
    Topic: Core Subjective Topics

    These barriers can be eliminated or minimized by increasing the readability and clarity of written communication and by improving the quality of electronic devices and other communication devices. Use of appropriate wording is also very important. Other useful steps are: motivating the receiver, communicating feelings as well as facts, using feedback to confirm understanding, using simple language, listening actively, and constraining emotions when communicating important matters.

  39. Identify and describe the different types of communication flows. (3)
    Topic: Core Subjective Topics

    Communication in an organization flows in four directions: 1. Downward communication: flows from higher to lower levels, used by managers to assign goals, give job instructions, explain policies, and provide feedback. 2. Upward communication: flows from lower to higher levels, keeping managers informed about progress, problems, and feelings, through reports, suggestions, and grievances. 3. Lateral (horizontal) communication: takes place among members of the same level, saving time and facilitating coordination. 4. Diagonal communication: cuts across both levels and functions, for example a credit officer contacting a sales manager directly.

  40. What are the major strengths of today's advanced means of communication as compared to the traditional means of the past? (3)
    Topic: Core Subjective Topics

    The major strengths are: - More knowledge. - Multiple choices. - We can share more in a shorter time. - Everything has become almost instant (SMS, Internet connectivity, cellular phone technology, Wi-Fi, and other technologies).

  41. How do cultural factors affect the communication process? Explain. (5)
    Topic: Core Subjective Topics

    Culture shapes every stage of communication. Words, gestures, and symbols carry different meanings in different cultures, so encoding and decoding differ: the same gesture that is polite in one culture may be offensive in another. High-context cultures (such as many Asian and Arab societies) rely heavily on nonverbal cues and the situation, while low-context cultures (such as Germany and the United States) rely on explicit words. Cultures also differ in formality, directness, use of silence, attitudes toward hierarchy (whether a subordinate may question a superior), and language itself. These differences create barriers of misinterpretation, which managers can reduce through cultural awareness, simple and clear language, feedback, and avoiding assumptions that one's own culture's ways are universal.

  42. What is the difference between leadership and management? (3)
    Topic: Core Subjective Topics

    Management is about coping with complexity: it plans, budgets, organizes, staffs, controls, and solves problems to bring order and consistency to the organization. Leadership is about coping with change: it sets a direction and vision, aligns people behind it, and motivates and inspires them to overcome obstacles. A manager's authority comes from the formal position, while a leader's influence may rest on personal qualities as well. Organizations need both: strong management to keep the system running efficiently and strong leadership to move it in new directions.

  43. Discuss when formal leadership may not be that important. (3)
    Topic: Core Subjective Topics

    Formal leadership is not always important because certain individual, job, and organizational variables can act as substitutes for leadership or neutralize the leader's influence. Substitutes for leadership include subordinates' experience, training, and professionalism, tasks that are highly structured, routine, or intrinsically satisfying, and cohesive work groups that provide their own guidance and support. Neutralizers, such as rigid rules, physical distance, and reward systems outside the leader's control, prevent the leader from acting. In such situations, investing in a formal leader adds little, and organizations do better to strengthen the substitutes themselves.

  44. Identify and explain the two dimensions of leadership described in the Ohio State studies. (5)
    Topic: Core Subjective Topics

    The Ohio State studies discovered two dimensions of leader behavior: 1. Initiating structure: the extent to which a leader defines and structures his or her own role and the roles of subordinates toward goal attainment. The leader assigns particular tasks, expects workers to maintain definite standards of performance, sets goals, plans and organizes work, and emphasizes meeting deadlines. 2. Consideration: the extent to which a leader has job relationships characterized by mutual trust, respect for subordinates' ideas, and regard for their feelings. Such leaders emphasize interpersonal relations and show trust, respect, and friendship toward their followers. Leaders high on both dimensions (the 'high-high' leaders) generally achieved higher subordinate performance and satisfaction, though with some exceptions.
