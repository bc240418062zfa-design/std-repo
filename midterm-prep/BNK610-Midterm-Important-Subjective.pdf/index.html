  1. Define the Islamic economic system. How does the Islamic view of wealth and property differentiate it from the capitalist and socialist systems?
    Topic: Islamic Economic System

    The Islamic economic system is a system governed in conformity with the rules of Sharia'h, the divine commands and laws that regulate every aspect of individual and collective life. The basic difference lies in the nature of wealth and property. According to the Quran, wealth in all its forms is created by Allah and is in principle His property; He has granted man only the right to use and exploit it. Man's right of property is therefore real but not absolute or arbitrary: he must spend wealth where Allah has ordered and refrain where Allah has forbidden. Capitalism, by contrast, treats property as man's own and gives him unconditional power to deal with it as he pleases, which leads to concentration of wealth, monopolies and inequality. Socialism goes to the other extreme and denies the right of private property altogether, vesting all resources in the state. Islam strikes a balance: by calling wealth 'the property of Allah' it cuts the roots of capitalist thinking, and by adding 'which He has bestowed upon you' it affirms private property and cuts the roots of socialism.

  2. What are the five categories of individual and collective life under Sharia'h?
    Topic: Islamic Economic System

    Differentiate between Ibadaat and Muamlaat. Sharia'h provides guidance for five categories of life: (1) Beliefs (Aqaid), (2) Acts of Worship (Ibadaat), (3) Dealing with others (Muamlaat), (4) Manners (Ikhlaqiat), and (5) Economics (Maishat). Ibadaat are the rights of Allah and govern the relationship between man and Allah; the general principle in Ibadaat is that nothing is permitted unless it is explicitly permitted by Sharia'h, and acts must be performed strictly according to its principles. Muamlaat are the rights of human beings and govern socio-economic dealings; they are governed by the rule of General Permissibility, meaning all acts are permitted unless clearly prohibited by Sharia'h. Another difference is that Allah may forgive a lapse in Ibadaat, but a lapse in Muamlaat has to be forgiven by the aggrieved person himself.

  3. Name and briefly explain the sources of Sharia'h.
    Topic: Islamic Economic System

    The rules and regulations of Sharia'h are derived from the following sources. (1) Quran: the word of Allah revealed to Prophet Muhammad (PBUH); it is the first and main source, and its commands are obligatory. (2) Sunnah: the sayings, actions and approvals of the Holy Prophet (PBUH), preserved in the Ahadith; it is the second source and provides the detailed code of conduct, e.g. the rules of Riba al-Fadl are extracted from the Sunnah. (3) Ijma: the consensus of the scholars of the Ummah on a particular issue, supported by the Prophet's saying that his Ummah shall never combine on an error. (4) Qiyas: applying a ruling of the Quran and Sunnah to a similar new situation by analogy; for example, khamr (date wine) is declared Haraam, so all intoxicating drinks such as beer and whisky are Haraam by Qiyas. (5) Ijtihad: literally 'ultimate effort' - the utmost effort of a qualified scholar (Mujtahid) to ascertain the Sharia'h ruling on an issue where no clear injunction exists; it must never contradict a clear rule of the Quran, Sunnah or Ijma.

  4. Compare the factors of production and the distribution of wealth in the capitalist, socialist and Islamic systems.
    Topic: Factors of Production in Islam

    In the capitalist system there are four factors of production: capital (the produced means of production), labor (human exertion), land (natural resources) and the entrepreneur, who combines the other factors and bears the risk. Wealth is distributed as interest to capital, wages to labor, rent to land and profit to the entrepreneur. In the socialist system the state owns capital, land and entrepreneurship as collective property, so no question of interest, rent or profit arises; labor is the only factor entitled to wealth, which it receives as wages. In the Islamic system there are only three factors: (1) Capital - those means of production which cannot be used unless wholly consumed or changed in form (e.g. money, foodstuff); it cannot be leased and its share is profit, not interest, and if the residual is negative the capital owner bears the loss. (2) Land - those means whose corpus and original form remain unaltered and which can therefore be leased (land, houses, machines, vehicles); its compensation is rent. (3) Labor - human exertion, physical, mental or of the heart, including organization and planning; its share is wages. The entrepreneur is not an independent factor in Islam; he is included in one of the three factors, and the risk of loss is treated as an inherent characteristic of capital itself.

  5. What is meant by the primary and secondary rights to wealth in Islam?
    Topic: Factors of Production in Islam

    According to the Islamic theory of distribution, two kinds of people have a right to wealth. The primary right to wealth belongs to those who have directly taken part in the process of production, i.e. the factors of production (capital, land and labor) which directly contribute to producing wealth; they receive their shares as profit, rent and wages respectively. The secondary right to wealth belongs to those who have not directly contributed to the process of production, but upon whom it has been made compulsory for the producers to make them co-sharers in their wealth, for example the beneficiaries of Sadaqat-ul-Wajibaat (obligatory charities). In this way Islam protects the weaker members of society and prevents the concentration of wealth in a few hands.

  6. Trace the history and evolution of Islamic banking in the world and in Pakistan.
    Topic: Introduction, History and Evolution of Islamic Banking

    Although Islamic banking in spirit dates from the time of the Holy Prophet (PBUH), who traded on the basis of Mudarabah, the first proper Islamic banking model appeared in Egypt in 1963, founded by Ahmed Al Najjar to provide Riba-free banking. In the same year Tabung Haji, the pilgrims' fund board, started in Malaysia. The Islamic Development Bank was formed in 1974 and began its activities on 20 October 1975 as the first officially registered Islamic bank. The first privately held commercial Islamic bank was formed in Dubai in 1975, followed by Faysal Islamic Bank in Egypt (1977) and Bahrain and Jordan Islamic Banks (1978); Malaysia established its first Islamic bank in 1983. In Pakistan, the Quaid-e-Azam advised the State Bank at its inauguration on 1 July 1948 to develop an economic system based on Islamic concepts of equality and social justice, and Article 38(f) of the 1973 Constitution requires the state to eliminate Riba as early as possible. The Council of Islamic Ideology issued its comprehensive report on the elimination of interest in June 1980, and banks offered PLS counters from 1981, but the process suffered setbacks. The Federal Shariat Court declared the 1985 banking procedure un-Islamic in 1991 and the Shariat Appellate Bench rejected the appeals in 1999. Finally, the State Bank adopted a three-point strategy (full-fledged Islamic banks, subsidiaries, and stand-alone Islamic banking branches of conventional banks) and issued its first Islamic banking license to Meezan Bank Limited in 2002, after which Islamic banking grew on a parallel basis with conventional banking.

  7. Define Riba. What are the three essential factors of a transaction involving Riba?
    Topic: Riba: Definition, Prohibition and Types

    The word Riba means excess, increase or addition. In Sharia'h terminology it implies any excess compensation without due consideration, and this consideration does not include the time value of money. It can also be defined as the giving and taking, paying and receiving of any excess amount in exchange for a loan at an agreed rate, whether the rate is high or low. A transaction involving Riba has three main factors: (1) there must be an excess given or taken; (2) there must be a contract of loan or debt; and (3) the excess given or taken must be pre-agreed. Any excess that is not predetermined, such as a borrower voluntarily repaying a loan in a better way, is not Riba.

  8. Explain how Riba was prohibited gradually in the Quran.
    Topic: Riba: Definition, Prohibition and Types

    Riba was not forbidden at once; the Quran prohibited it gradually in four stages. First revelation (Surah al-Rum 30:39, revealed in Makkah): it was declared that Riba does not increase wealth with Allah and carries no reward in the hereafter, whereas Zakah multiplies wealth; a direct prohibition was not yet given. Second revelation (Surah al-Nisa 4:161): the Jews were condemned for charging Riba although it had been forbidden to them, and for devouring people's property by false means; prohibition for Muslims could be inferred but was not yet explicit. Third revelation (Surah Al-Imran 3:130-132, revealed in the 2nd year after Hijrah around the Battle of Uhud): Muslims were clearly commanded not to consume Riba doubled and multiplied. Fourth revelation (Surah al-Baqarah 2:275-281): the complete and final prohibition - Allah has permitted sale and prohibited Riba; believers must give up what remains of Riba, failing which there is a declaration of war from Allah and His Messenger; on repentance the creditor is entitled only to his principal, neither wronging nor being wronged.

  9. Differentiate between Riba An Nasiyah and Riba Al Fadl.
    Topic: Riba: Definition, Prohibition and Types

    Riba An Nasiyah is any increased or excess amount which a lender receives over and above his principal through a pre-agreed interest-based loan transaction. Imam Abu Bakr Jassas defined it as that kind of loan where a specified repayment period and an amount in excess of capital are predetermined. It is directly prohibited by the Quran (Surah al-Baqarah 2:278-281), hence it is called Riba Al Quran, and also Riba Al Jahiliyyah because it was practised in the days of ignorance; the Prophet (PBUH) said, 'Every loan that draws interest is Riba.' Riba Al Fadl, on the other hand, arises in barter or exchange transactions: it is any excess given or taken in the exchange of homogeneous commodities in a spot sale. Its prohibition is established by the Sunnah, hence it is called Riba Al Hadees. The Hadith names six commodities - gold, silver, wheat, barley, dates and salt - which must be exchanged in equal quantities and hand to hand when exchanged for the same kind. Riba Al Fadl is prohibited to ensure justice in exchange and to close the back door that could lead to Riba An Nasiyah.

  10. What are Amwaal-e-Ribawiyyah?
    Topic: Riba: Definition, Prohibition and Types

    Explain the 'illat' of Riba Al Fadl according to the different schools of fiqh. Amwaal-e-Ribawiyyah are the commodities in whose exchange Riba Al Fadl can occur. The Hadith names six: gold, silver, wheat, barley, dates and salt. To extend the ruling to other commodities, jurists identified the common attributes (illat) of these six items. According to Imam Abu Hanifa, the two attributes are weight or volume, and exchange between similar commodities; hence every commodity sold by weight or measure falls under the rule. According to Imam Shafi, the attributes are being a medium of exchange (currency) and being edible, so the rule covers currencies and all eatables. According to Imam Maalik, the attributes are being edible and being preservable (storable for future needs). Three opinions are related from Imam Ahmad bin Hanbal: one following Abu Hanifa, one following Shafi, and a third combining edibility with weight and volume.

  11. Write a detailed note on the essentials of a valid Islamic contract (Aqd).
    Topic: Islamic Contracts

    A contract (Aqd) is a bilateral agreement executed between two or more parties; it is binding on all parties and enforceable through a court of law. Four essential elements are required for a valid Aqd. (1) Parties to the contract (Mutaa'qidain): the contracting parties must be competent - major, of sound mind and not Mahjoor (restricted). The Mahjoor include an insane person, a child not mature enough to understand the nature of the transaction, and a slave not permitted by his master. (2) Wording of the contract (Alfaz-e-Aqd): the wording must be absolute, immediate and unconditional; a contract contingent on a future event is not allowed, and any condition attached must not violate the rules of Sharia'h. (3) Subject matter (Ma'qood Alaih): it must exist at the time of the contract, be of some value, not be prohibited by Sharia'h, be capable of ownership, delivery and possession, and be specified and quantified to remove ambiguity. (4) Consideration (Ma'qood Bihi): it must be specified, quantified and determined at the time of the contract (for example a price of Rs. 300); an agreement without consideration is void, although consideration is not required in non-compensatory contracts such as gift or guarantee.

  12. Differentiate between a unilateral promise (Wa'da) and a bilateral promise (Muwa'adah), and discuss their enforceability.
    Topic: Islamic Contracts

    A unilateral promise (Wa'da) is a one-sided undertaking by one person to execute a specific transaction or contract in the future, for example A promises to sell his house to B within six months for Rs. 500,000. It is only a promise, not a sale, because a future sale is prohibited in Islam. A bilateral promise (Muwa'adah or Mu'ahadah) is a mutual promise between two parties, for example A promises to sell his house to B for Rs. 500,000 and B promises to purchase it for the same amount; when both parties actually execute the transaction it becomes a contract (Aqd), and non-fulfilment is a breach of contract. Regarding enforceability: according to the Hanafi school a unilateral promise is not enforceable by law but is a moral obligation on the promisor, while according to Imam Maalik it is enforceable by law; the present-day consensus of Sharia'h scholars is that a unilateral promise is enforceable, and if the promisor fails to fulfil it through his own wilful act or negligence he must compensate the promisee for the actual loss. A bilateral promise is enforceable under law; however, according to the majority of present-day scholars and AAOIFI, Muwa'adah is not allowed in situations where the contract itself is not allowed, such as forward contracts, and is then not enforceable.

  13. Differentiate between Uqood-e-Mu'awadah and Uqood-e-Ghaer Mu'awadah, giving examples. What is the effect of a void condition on each?
    Topic: Islamic Contracts

    Uqood-e-Mu'awadah are compensatory contracts in which all parties receive something in return as compensation, for example the sale of a pen by A to B for Rs. 50, where B gets the pen and A gets the money; sale, purchase and lease agreements belong to this category. Uqood-e-Ghaer Mu'awadah are non-compensatory contracts in which it is not necessary that all parties receive compensation, for example a contract of loan, a contract of Hiba (gift) and a contract of guarantee. As to void conditions: in a compensatory contract a void condition makes the whole contract void - for example, A purchases a car from B on the condition that B will use the car twice a week; the condition favours one party against the contract, so the sale is void. In a non-compensatory contract a void condition does not invalidate the contract - for example, if A gives a loan to B on the condition of repaying it with interest, the condition of interest is void but the loan contract itself remains valid.

  14. What are the essentials of a valid sale (Bai Sahih)?
    Topic: Sale and Its Types

    Differentiate between Bai Baatil, Bai Fasid and Bai Makrooh. A valid sale requires four essentials. (1) Contract (Aqd): there must be offer and acceptance (Ijab-o-Qobool), made orally (Qauli) or impliedly (Isharaa, e.g. Taati - hand-to-hand sale in supermarkets - or Istijrar); the parties must be sane and mature; the sale must be non-contingent, unconditional (except valid or market-practised conditions) and immediate, not attributed to a future date. (2) Subject matter (Mabee'): it must exist at the time of sale (except in Salam and Istisna), be valuable, be Sharia'h-compliant (sale of wine or pork is void), be capable of ownership and delivery, and be specified and quantified. (3) Price (Thaman): it must be specified in a known unit of currency and quantified and certain. (4) Possession or delivery (Qabza): the subject matter must be in the seller's physical (Haqiqi) or constructive possession, constructive possession meaning the commodity is in his ownership and its risk and reward have passed to him. Bai Baatil is a void, non-existing sale in which an essential condition (of offer and acceptance, the parties, or the subject matter's existence, value, usability or deliverability) is not fulfilled; the buyer gets no title and the seller no price. Bai Fasid is a sale which exists but is defective because a condition of the contract, subject matter or price is not complied with; it is voidable and becomes valid if the defect is rectified. Bai Makrooh is a valid but disliked sale, e.g. a sale after the Juma Azaan, a sale after hoarding, or intervening to buy goods already under negotiation between other parties.

  15. What is meant by Khiyars in a sale?
    Topic: Sale and Its Types

    Briefly describe the five types of Khiyar. Khiyar means the option or right of the buyer or the seller to rescind (cancel) a sale contract. There are five types. (1) Khiyar-e-Shart (option by stipulation): at the time of sale either party stipulates that the sale may be rescinded within a specified number of days, which must be clearly mentioned; if stipulated by the buyer it is Khiyar-e-Mushtari and if by the seller Khiyar-e-Bai; this option cannot be transferred to the heirs of the parties. (2) Khiyar-e-Roiyyat (option of inspection): the buyer may return goods bought without seeing them if, on inspection, they are not according to the required specification. (3) Khiyar-e-Aib (option of defect): the buyer may return goods found defective; the seller must disclose all defects, and hiding a defect amounts to fraud. (4) Khiyar-e-Wasf (option of quality/description): the buyer may rescind the sale if a quality specified by the seller, e.g. automatic transmission in a car, is absent in the delivered goods. (5) Khiyar-e-Ghaban (option of price): if the seller sells goods at a price far above the market price and the market price was not known to the buyer, the buyer may return the goods and cancel the contract.

  16. Describe the prohibited sale transactions: short selling, sale of debt and Bai al Inah.
    Topic: Sale and Its Types

    (1) Short selling (sale before possession, Qabl-al-Qabza): selling a thing without having taken possession of it is prohibited; the Prophet (PBUH) said, 'Whoever purchases foodstuff should not sell it until he takes its possession' (Bukhari). Short selling in equity, currency and commodity markets falls in this category. (2) Sale of debt (Bai al Dain): selling a debt receivable at a discounted price to obtain cash on the spot, as in discounting a bill of exchange, is prohibited by the consensus (Ijma) of Muslim jurists. A debt parallels money, so exchanging it for a lesser amount involves Riba, and trading it even at face value involves great Gharar because receipt from the debtor is uncertain. A debt may therefore only be assigned at face value and with recourse, the assignor remaining liable if the original debtor defaults. (3) Bai al Inah: a sale and buy-back transaction in which a person sells an asset on credit and then buys it back at a lower price for cash; the difference is similar to interest on a loan, so the transaction is prohibited - for example B sells a machine to A for $12 on credit and buys it back from him for $10 cash, earning $2 like interest.

  17. Differentiate between Islamic and conventional banks.
    Topic: Banking Framework: Islamic vs Conventional Banks

    The differences exist at three levels. At the conceptual and socio-religious level, conventional banks are money-lending institutions doing the business of lending and borrowing on interest, whereas Islamic banks are trading or investment companies working under socio-religious laws that prohibit giving and taking interest; Islamic banks also avoid Haram transactions such as gambling, speculation, short selling and sale of debts, and do not finance harmful industries such as alcohol, tobacco and pork, while conventional banks finance all industries allowed by the law of the land. At the level of business model and governing framework, the Islamic bank's model is based on trading activities and active participation in trade and production, and it has a governing framework based on Sharia'h: a Sharia'h supervisory board approves all its products and transactions, which is the litmus test distinguishing it from a conventional bank, which has no such board and only lends money. At the product level, conventional bank products are interest-based and treat money as a commodity, while Islamic bank products are asset-backed - trading of assets, renting of assets and participation on a profit-and-loss basis - and any loan given by an Islamic bank must be interest-free. Consequently the banker-customer relationship also differs: in conventional banking it is always creditor and debtor, while in Islamic banking it varies with the product - seller and buyer in sale modes, lessor and lessee in rental modes, partners in participation modes, and service provider and client in service modes - and the bank's compensation takes the form of sale profit, rent or shared profit rather than interest, with the bank bearing the risk of the trade or business activity.

  18. Why are bank deposits governed by the law of Qard and not Amanah?
    Topic: Banking Framework: Islamic vs Conventional Banks

    Support your answer with the example of Hazrat Zubair bin Awwam (R.A). Amanah (trust) has two special features: the thing held as Amanah cannot be used by the custodian for his own business or benefit, and the custodian cannot be held liable for any loss or damage to the Amanah beyond his control. Bank deposits fulfil neither feature: banks use deposits in their business to earn profit, and a bank is fully liable to return deposits on demand even in case of loss or damage. Hence deposits in banks, whether current, savings or fixed, are governed by the law of Qard (loan) rather than Amanah, and the bank acts as guarantor of the amount. Hazrat Zubair bin Awwam (R.A) was famous for his honesty, and people used to leave their valuables with him as Amanah. He declined to accept them as Amanah and accepted them as Qard instead, so that the depositors felt secure (he being fully responsible for any loss) and so that he could employ the property to earn profit. Accordingly, in conventional banks any excess paid on such deposits (Qard) is interest (Riba); in Islamic banks the current account is based on Qard with no profit paid, while savings and term deposits are governed by the principles of Mudarabah/Musharakah and share actual business profit.

  19. Define Mudarabah.
    Topic: Liability Side of Islamic Banks: Mudarabah and Deposit Pools

    Explain its types and the conditions for a valid profit-sharing ratio. Mudarabah is a kind of partnership in which one person provides the capital and the other manages the business with his skills. The provider of capital is called Rab-ul-Maal and the manager is called Mudarib; the profit is shared between them in an agreed ratio, while financial loss falls on the Rab-ul-Maal (the Mudarib losing his effort), provided the Mudarib has worked with due diligence. There are two types: Mudarabah Al Muqayyadah (restricted), in which the Rab-ul-Maal restricts the investment to a particular business, sector or place; and Mudarabah Al Mutlaqah (unrestricted), in which the Mudarib is free to invest in any business he deems fit - most Islamic banks in Pakistan run their remunerative deposits on this basis. The conditions for the profit-sharing ratio are: it must be agreed at the time of the contract as a proportion of the actual profit; no party may be allocated a specific fixed amount of profit, nor a share expressed as a percentage of the capital (e.g. 30% of capital); different proportions may be agreed for different situations, such as different goods or different towns; the Mudarib cannot claim any salary or extra remuneration beyond his agreed share of profit; and if the parties have not determined a ratio, the profit is shared 50:50. If the profit is fixed in any way the Mudarabah becomes void; the Rab-ul-Maal then takes the entire profit and capital and the Mudarib is entitled only to Ujrat-e-Misl, a market-rate salary for his services.

  20. Explain the deposit pool management and the weightage system used by Islamic banks for profit distribution.
    Topic: Liability Side of Islamic Banks: Mudarabah and Deposit Pools

    Islamic banks group the funds received from depositors into pools according to the nature and source of funds, and invest each pool in Sharia'h-compliant financing such as Murabaha, Ijarah, Salam, Istisna and Diminishing Musharakah. The main pools maintained in Pakistan are the general deposit pool in PKR, the general deposit pool in FCY, the equity pool, special or specific customers' pools, export refinance scheme pools and the treasury/financial institutions pool, all managed at head office under the monitoring of the Sharia'h board. Profit is calculated on a gross income basis, usually monthly, and is shared between the bank (as Mudarib) and the depositors (as Rab-ul-Maal) in a predetermined ratio on the actual profit earned - expected or budgeted profit is never distributed. Within a pool, the depositors' share is distributed through weightages, a profit-sharing mechanism among the depositors based on the type of deposit, amount tiers, investment tenure and profit payment option; a weightage can be amended only at the beginning of the accounting period. For example, in a pool of Rs. 10,000 with three-month deposits of Rs. 3,000 (weightage 0.60), six-month deposits of Rs. 4,000 (0.70) and one-year deposits of Rs. 3,000 (1.00), the weighted averages are 1,800, 2,800 and 3,000 (total 7,600); if the pool earns Rs. 1,000 shared 50:50, the depositors' Rs. 500 is divided in proportion to the weighted averages, giving approximately Rs. 119, Rs. 184 and Rs. 197 to the three categories.

  21. What is Musharakah?
    Topic: Musharakah

    Describe the kinds of Shirkah (partnership). Musharakah, from the Arabic word Shirkah meaning sharing, is a joint enterprise in which all partners contribute capital, the profit is distributed according to the agreement of the partners and the loss is distributed strictly according to the ratio of investment, as stated by Hazrat Ali (R.A). Shirkah is of two main kinds. (1) Shirkat-ul-Milk (partnership in ownership): two or more persons jointly own an asset without a formal partnership contract; it may be optional (Ikhtiari), e.g. two persons jointly purchase equipment, or compulsory (Ghair Ikhtiari), e.g. heirs jointly own inherited property. (2) Shirkat-ul-Aqd (partnership by contract): partners enter into a contract to share profit through a joint commercial enterprise. Its forms are: Shirkat-ul-Amwal, where all partners invest capital (this is what Musharakah commonly refers to); Shirkat-ul-Aamal (also Shirkat-us-Sanai or Shirkat-ul-Abdan), where partners contribute services and skills, e.g. two tailors; Shirkat-ul-Wujooh, where partners have no capital and buy goods on credit on the strength of their goodwill and sell them for cash; Shirkat-ul-Mufawada, where capital, labour, profit, risk and reward are all equal - if any element becomes unequal it turns into Ainan; and Shirkat-ul-Ainan, the most common form, where capital, management and profit shares need not be equal.

  22. State the rules of profit and loss distribution in Musharakah, and differentiate Musharakah from Mudarabah.
    Topic: Musharakah

    The rules of profit in Musharakah are: the profit-sharing ratio must be agreed as a percentage of the actual profit earned; it is invalid to fix a lump sum amount (e.g. Rs. 10,000 per month) or a percentage of a partner's investment as his profit; a partner who works in the business may receive a profit share higher than his investment ratio because his labour also counts, but a sleeping partner's profit ratio must not exceed his investment ratio. Loss, by contrast, is distributed strictly in proportion to each partner's investment, as stated by Hazrat Ali (R.A): loss is distributed exactly according to the ratio of investment, and profit according to the agreement of the partners. Musharakah differs from Mudarabah in that: in Musharakah all partners invest capital, while in Mudarabah only the Rab-ul-Maal invests and the Mudarib contributes labour; in Musharakah every partner has an equal right to manage the business, while in Mudarabah management is the sole right of the Mudarib and the Rab-ul-Maal has no right to manage; in Musharakah loss is borne by all partners in proportion to investment, while in Mudarabah the entire financial loss is borne by the Rab-ul-Maal (provided the Mudarib exercised due diligence) and the Mudarib loses only his effort; and in Musharakah the pooled assets are mutually owned by all partners from the start.

  23. What is Murabaha?
    Topic: Murabaha and Diminishing Musharakah

    Describe the steps of Murabaha to the Purchase Orderer (MPO) and the issues relating to default, rollover and rebate. Murabaha is a sale in which the cost of the goods and the profit charged on it are known to both the buyer and the seller; it is a sale, not a loan, and almost two-thirds of Islamic bank financing is done through it. Because trading is not the primary function of banks, they use Murabaha to the Purchase Orderer (MPO): the customer approaches the bank for financing of a commodity; an overall agreement is signed (in Pakistan the Master Murabaha Financing Agreement, MMFA); the bank may appoint the customer as its agent under an Agency Agreement to purchase the asset; the customer purchases the asset and takes possession as agent and informs the bank through a Declaration Form; the bank inspects the asset and issues a Letter of Disbursement, paying the vendor preferably directly by pay order; the asset thereby comes into the bank's ownership and constructive possession, with its risk and reward; the Murabaha sale is then executed by the customer's offer to purchase and the bank's acceptance, with the Murabaha price and payment date decided at that time; payment may be lump sum, in installments, or partly both. The main issues are: no penalty can be charged on default - instead the client undertakes to pay an amount to charity, which is disbursed under the directions of the Sharia'h board; security by mortgage or hypothecation and a third-party guarantee are allowed, but the guarantor cannot charge a fee; rollover of Murabaha is not possible because once the asset is sold it belongs to the customer and the seller cannot sell it again; and a rebate on early payment must not be stipulated in the contract or become regular practice, though a discretionary discount by the bank is allowed.

  24. Explain Diminishing Musharakah as a mode of home financing.
    Topic: Murabaha and Diminishing Musharakah

    Diminishing Musharakah is a recently developed form of Musharakah widely used for house financing and fixed-asset financing. The financier and the customer jointly purchase the asset; the financier's share is divided into equal units, and the customer periodically purchases these units until the financier's share is exhausted and the customer becomes the sole owner. The customer also pays rent for using the financier's share, and the rent decreases as he purchases more units. Home financing is based on Shirkat-ul-Milk and is structured through three separate and independent contracts which must not be made contingent on one another: (1) joint ownership (Shirkat-ul-Milk) of the house; (2) Ijarah, under which the bank leases its share to the client for rent; and (3) a unilateral promise by the client to purchase the bank's units. For example, if a house costs 1,000,000 dirhams and the bank finances 80%, the bank's share is 800,000 and the client's 200,000; over a 10-year tenure the bank's share is divided into 120 units (months), so the price per unit is 800,000 / 120 = 6,666.67 dirhams, and each monthly payment consists of the purchase of one unit plus rent on the units still owned by the bank, the rent component decreasing every month.

  25. Define Ijarah and state its basic rules. How does Ijarah differ from conventional leasing?
    Topic: Ijarah

    Ijarah means to give something on rent. It is used in two senses: hiring human services, where the employer is Mustajir, the employee is Ajir and the wages are Ujrah; and transferring the usufruct of an asset to another person for an agreed rent, where the lessor is Mujir and the lessee is Mustajir. Its basic rules are: only the usufruct is transferred while ownership remains with the lessor; the subject matter must be valuable, identified and quantified; consumable things such as money, wheat and rice cannot be leased; all liabilities of ownership are borne by the lessor; the Ijarah period must be specified; and the asset must be used only for the purpose stated in the contract. The lessee is an Ameen (trustee): he compensates only for damage caused by his negligence or misuse, while damage from factors beyond his control is borne by the lessor. Rent must be determined at the time of contract for the whole period, the lessor cannot increase it unilaterally, and the Ijarah period begins from the date the asset is delivered to the lessee. Ijarah differs from conventional leasing in that in Ijarah the asset is fully owned by the bank, loss to the asset is borne by the bank unless caused by the lessee's negligence, rent cannot be charged before delivery of the asset (conventional leasing charges from before delivery), the Ijarah contract is binding and cannot be terminated unilaterally without breach, and no late-payment penalty can be charged to the lessee - any amount taken from a wilfully defaulting lessee must be given to charity.

  26. Define Salam and state the conditions for a valid Salam contract. What is prohibited in a Salam contract?
    Topic: Salam, Istisna, Istijrar and Tawarruq

    Salam is a sale in which the price is paid in full in advance and the delivery of the goods is deferred to a future date. It is an exception to the rule that one cannot sell what one does not possess, and was permitted by the Holy Prophet (PBUH), who required that advance payment be made for a known quality, specified measure and weight, with the price and time of delivery specified. The conditions of a valid Salam are: (1) the price must be paid in full at the time of the contract - otherwise the transaction would become sale of debt against debt, which is strictly prohibited, and the purpose of meeting the seller's immediate need would be defeated; (2) the quantity and quality of the goods must be exactly specifiable, so precious stones cannot be sold through Salam because every stone differs; (3) the Salam cannot be tied to the produce of a particular field, farm or tree, because the crop may be destroyed and delivery become uncertain; (4) the quality must be clearly specified without ambiguity; (5) the quantity must be agreed in absolute terms; (6) the exact date and place of delivery must be specified; and (7) commodities whose deferment risks Riba, such as gold exchanged for gold or silver, cannot be the subject matter of Salam - such exchanges must be on the spot. It is also prohibited to deliver money instead of the commodity; only the commodity contracted is delivered. Salam is used mainly to finance small farmers and import/export traders.

  27. What is Parallel Salam?
    Topic: Salam, Istisna, Istijrar and Tawarruq

    Also differentiate between Salam and Istisna. In Parallel Salam, after purchasing goods through a Salam contract, the bank sells the same kind of goods through a second, independent Salam contract to a third party for the same date of delivery; the second Salam has a shorter period and a higher price, and the difference is the institution's profit. The two contracts must be completely independent - neither may be made contingent on the other - so even if the first seller defaults or delivers faulty goods, the bank remains bound to deliver conforming goods to the third party. Salam and Istisna differ as follows: the subject of Salam may or may not need manufacturing, whereas the subject of Istisna must necessarily be manufactured; in Salam the price must be paid in full in advance, whereas in Istisna full spot payment is not necessary and the price may be deferred or paid in installments; in Salam the time of delivery must be specifically known, whereas in Istisna an exact date cannot be determined and only a maximum timeframe may be fixed, after which the buyer is not bound to accept; and a Salam contract cannot be cancelled unilaterally once signed, whereas an Istisna contract can be rescinded before the manufacturing work has started.

  28. What is Tawarruq? How is it different from Bai al Inah?
    Topic: Salam, Istisna, Istijrar and Tawarruq

    Tawarruq means to buy a commodity on credit and sell it at spot value to a third party with the objective of obtaining cash. It is used by Islamic banks for liquidity management, personal financing and credit cards. Tawarruq has mainly been discussed by the Hanbali and Shafi jurists; the Maliki jurists see no major impermissibility in it and regard it as a way of avoiding Riba, while the Hanafi majority hold that selling the commodity in the market to a third party is valid. Tawarruq differs from Bai al Inah in the identity of the second buyer: in Tawarruq the commodity is sold to a third party in the market, whereas in Bai al Inah the commodity is sold back to the original seller (on credit at a higher price and bought back for cash at a lower price), which is prohibited because it is a device to earn interest on a loan. Scholars advise that Tawarruq be used only in extreme and unavoidable liquidity needs, mainly in the corporate sector, because its excessive use may harm the Islamic banking industry; and if the bank appoints the customer (Mutawarriq) as its agent both to buy the commodity and to sell it to himself, the transaction is invalid because the bank never took possession or risk of the commodity.

  29. Why can an Islamic bank not charge a fee on a Letter of Credit guarantee?
    Topic: Financing of Foreign Trade

    Describe how Islamic banks finance imports and exports. A guarantee contract in Sharia'h is a gratuitous (non-compensatory) contract, so no fee or commission can be charged on the guarantee itself, and no compensation may be charged merely for undertaking to pay an amount. An Islamic bank may charge only service charges based on actual cost, such as documentation charges, credit assessment fee, correspondence charges, account maintenance and monitoring charges. For imports, Islamic banks finance through Murabaha (the importer is appointed the bank's agent, an Agreement to Murabaha and Agency Agreement are signed, all actual-cost charges are included in the cost of the asset, and after shipment and receipt of documents the bank sells the commodity to the importer on Murabaha), Ijarah (the bank leases the imported equipment or building to the customer and may sell it to him at the end of the term) and Musharakah (bank and importer jointly import and sell the commodity, the working partner's profit share not being less than his investment share). Export financing is of two kinds. Pre-shipment financing is provided through Murabaha, Istisna and Musharakah/Mudarabah - Mudarabah if the exporter does not invest his own funds and Musharakah if he does, this being described as the most appropriate method. Post-shipment financing replaces bill discounting and is provided through Wakalah with Qard-e-Hassana (the bank collects the receivable as agent for a fee and lends the exporter an interest-free amount repayable on the due date), Salam (the bank purchases foreign currency from the exporter in advance, paying PKR on the spot at a rate lower than the future rate, with delivery of the currency on a specified future date not contingent on the export documents) and Murabaha (at a price typically equal to the face value of the export bill, payable at the bill's maturity). Under the Islamic Export Refinance Scheme, the State Bank of Pakistan invests through Musharakah agreements with Islamic banks in a pool of Sharia'h-compliant financing.

  30. What problems do conventional treasury operations pose for Islamic banks, and what Sharia'h-compliant solutions are used?
    Topic: Treasury Operations

    Conventional treasury operations include short-term acceptances, short-term investment and liquidity management, bill purchase and discounting of receivables, spot and forward currency trading, and derivatives and options. The first two are practised through interest-based borrowing and lending (Riba) and the last three are also impermissible due to Gharar as well as Riba, so Islamic banks, whose treasury operations are still in their infancy, use Sharia'h-compliant alternatives. For short-term acceptances, banks create and securitize pools of their financing assets (Murabaha, Salam, Istisna and Ijarah, booked preferably with high-rated clients) on Musharakah/Mudarabah basis; for trading of mixed funds, tangible assets must be more than 51% of the pool and Ijarah assets at least 51%, and each institution is assigned a specific weightage, a profit-sharing ratio and a unique identification number (PIN) for its pool. For liquidity, excess funds - Islamic banks generally suffer from excess liquidity because of limited permissible placements - are placed through Musharakah/Mudarabah asset pools, and Tawarruq (commodity Murabaha) is used by some institutions, though it is not widely accepted by scholars and should not become regular practice. Forward purchase and sale of currencies is prohibited; only a unilateral promise may be used, and that too for a genuine trade need and not for speculation, while 'spot' in the Islamic perspective means delivery of both currencies at the time of execution of the contract. Bill discounting is replaced by Wakalah and Hawalah (assignment of debt is allowed, the original creditor remaining responsible if the debtor defaults), Bai Salam of currencies, Murabaha and Musharakah/Mudarabah. Call and put options themselves are allowed, but selling the option right as derivatives is not permissible because of Gharar, and short sale is prohibited because Islam forbids selling what one does not own and possess.

  31. What is Sharia'h audit?
    Topic: Sharia'h Audit and Compliance

    Describe the role of the Sharia'h Advisory Board and the methodology of Sharia'h audit. Sharia'h audit is the inspection of the financial and operational data of an Islamic financial institution according to Sharia'h principles, conducted to express an independent opinion that the institution's policies, procedures and practices adhere to the rules of Sharia'h and to secure the interest of stakeholders. A distinctive feature of an Islamic bank is its independent Sharia'h audit and compliance unit, reporting directly to the Sharia'h advisor or Sharia'h board. The Sharia'h Advisory Board is a key component of the institution: it ensures that all procedures and practices comply with Sharia'h rulings derived from the Quran, Sunnah, Ijma and Qiyas; it oversees existing and potential products, approves new product concepts, documentation and process flows, and conducts and supervises the annual Sharia'h audit. Under the compliance guidelines, all products and services, all contracts and agreements, all financial and accounting entries, the deposit pool management and profit distribution mechanism, every fee charged, the treatment of securities held against credit risk, and every local or offshore investment must be approved by the Sharia'h board. The methodology of Sharia'h audit covers three areas: (1) documentation, procedures and implementation - reviewing master agreements (which describe the overall bank-customer relationship, credit limits, guarantees and security), transaction documents (which are product-specific) and security documents (such as letters of hypothecation, lien and pledge and guarantees), all against the board's guidelines; (2) product-level audit - applying specific guidelines for each product, e.g. for Murabaha the process flow cannot be changed without the board's approval, offer and acceptance may be signed only after the bank has taken possession of the asset, and the Murabaha must not be rolled over or its price later increased; for Ijarah the contract can be signed only when the asset is delivered; and for Diminishing Musharakah the joint ownership must be properly created and the mode must not be used for running finance; and (3) the general environment of the bank - the dress and ethics of employees, their basic understanding of Islamic banking, availability of Fatawas and product guidelines in branches, and periodic staff training.
