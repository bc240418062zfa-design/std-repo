  1. What is 2,3-bisphosphoglycerate (2,3-BPG)? Describe its role in the transport of oxygen by hemoglobin.
    Topic: Core Subjective Topics

    2,3-Bisphosphoglycerate (2,3-BPG) is a negatively charged molecule formed as an alternate product of glycolysis in red blood cells; its concentration in RBCs increases in states of low oxygen delivery to the tissues. 2,3-BPG binds in the central cavity between the two beta subunits of deoxyhemoglobin, where positively charged amino acid residues interact with its negatively charged groups. By binding there it stabilizes the taut (T) form of hemoglobin, lowers its affinity for oxygen and promotes the release of oxygen in the tissues. In this way the interaction of 2,3-BPG with hemoglobin refines and regulates the oxygen-transport function of hemoglobin.

  2. Explain the allosteric effect of hemoglobin.
    Topic: Core Subjective Topics

    The ability of hemoglobin to reversibly bind oxygen is affected by the pH of the environment, the partial pressure of CO2 and the availability of 2,3-bisphosphoglycerate. These are collectively called allosteric effectors, because they bind at a site on the hemoglobin molecule different from the oxygen-binding (heme) site (allo = other, steric = site) and their binding affects the binding of oxygen at the heme groups. Binding of oxygen to one subunit also changes the conformation of the other subunits (T to R transition), which is the basis of cooperativity. The binding of oxygen to myoglobin is not influenced by these allosteric effectors.

  3. What are epimers? Give examples.
    Topic: Core Subjective Topics

    Epimers are two sugars that differ only in the configuration around a single carbon atom. For example, glucose and galactose are epimers that differ in configuration at carbon 4 (C-4), and glucose and mannose are epimers differing at C-2. Epimers are a type of diastereomers (stereoisomers that are not mirror images of each other).

  4. Describe the general structure of an amino acid. How do amino acids differ from each other?
    Topic: Core Subjective Topics

    Each amino acid has the same fundamental structure: a central carbon atom, called the alpha carbon, bonded to four groups — an amino group (NH2), a carboxyl group (COOH), a hydrogen atom, and a variable side chain called the R group. All 20 common amino acids are alpha-amino acids, i.e. the carboxyl and amino groups are attached to the same (alpha) carbon. Amino acids differ from each other in their R groups (side chains), which vary in structure, size and electric charge and influence the solubility of the amino acid in water.

  5. What is cis-isomerism in unsaturated fatty acids?
    Topic: Core Subjective Topics

    Unsaturated fatty acids contain one or more double bonds. Because there is no freedom of rotation about a double bond, the groups attached to the double-bonded carbons can be arranged in two ways, giving geometric (cis-trans) isomers. In the cis configuration both hydrocarbon chains lie on the same side of the double bond, which introduces a bend or kink in the chain (e.g. oleic acid, cis-9-octadecenoic acid). In the trans configuration the chains lie on opposite sides and the molecule remains nearly straight, like a saturated fatty acid. The cis bend prevents tight packing of the chains and lowers the melting point, keeping such fatty acids liquid (oils) at room temperature.

  6. Calculate the isoelectric point (pI) of histidine.
    Topic: Core Subjective Topics

    Histidine has three dissociable groups: the alpha-carboxyl group (pKa = 1.82), the imidazole R group (pKa = 6.0) and the alpha-amino group (pKa = 9.17), so it has three dissociable hydrogens. The isoelectric point is the average of the two pKa values that bracket the zwitterion (the form with zero net charge), i.e. the R-group pKa and the amino-group pKa: pI = (6.0 + 9.17) / 2 = 7.59. So the pI of histidine is approximately 7.59.

  7. Calculate the isoelectric point (pI) of glycine and describe its dissociation.
    Topic: Core Subjective Topics

    Glycine is a diprotic acid: when fully protonated it has two groups, COOH and NH3+, that can yield protons, with pKa1 = 2.34 (alpha-carboxyl group) and pKa2 = 9.60 (alpha-amino group). On raising the pH, the carboxyl group loses its proton first, forming the zwitterion (COO- and NH3+); at still higher pH the amino group loses its proton. The isoelectric point is the average of the two pKa values: pI = (2.34 + 9.60) / 2 = 5.97. So the pI of glycine is 5.97, the pH at which its net charge is zero.

  8. Calculate the isoelectric point (pI) of alanine and write its dissociation.
    Topic: Core Subjective Topics

    Alanine is a simple monoamino monocarboxylic alpha-amino acid and a diprotic acid when fully protonated, with pKa1 = 2.34 (alpha-carboxyl group) and pKa2 = 9.69 (alpha-amino group). At low pH both groups are protonated and the net charge is +1; as the pH rises the COOH group dissociates to COO-, giving the dipolar zwitterion with zero net charge; at high pH the NH3+ group also loses its proton and the net charge becomes -1. pI = (2.34 + 9.69) / 2 = 6.0 (approximately). So the isoelectric point of alanine is about 6.0.

  9. Describe the titration curve of glycine. What information is obtained from it?
    Topic: Core Subjective Topics

    The titration curve of glycine shows two distinct stages, corresponding to the loss of a proton from each of its two dissociable groups. Each stage has a midpoint at which the pH equals a pKa: pKa1 = 2.34 for the carboxyl group and pKa2 = 9.60 for the amino group. The curve therefore has two regions of buffering power, one centred near pH 2.34 and the other near pH 9.60. From the curve we obtain the pKa values of the ionizable groups, the isoelectric point (pI = 5.97, midway between the two pKa values) and the buffering regions of glycine. Glycine is not a good buffer at the pH of intracellular fluid or blood (about 7.4), because this lies far from both of its pKa values.

  10. What is starch? Explain its structure.
    Topic: Core Subjective Topics

    Starch is the storage form of carbohydrates in plants and the most important dietary carbohydrate; it is a homopolysaccharide made of glucose units. It has two components: amylose (about 13-20%), which consists of long, unbranched, helical chains of D-glucose joined by alpha(1-4) glycosidic linkages, and amylopectin, which is highly branched, with alpha(1-4) linkages in the chains and alpha(1-6) linkages at the branch points. Thousands of glucose monomers are linked in this way, and starch is stored in plants in tubers and seeds.

  11. What is osazone? How is it prepared?
    Topic: Core Subjective Topics

    Osazones are crystalline derivatives of sugars obtained by adding phenylhydrazine hydrochloride and sodium acetate to a sugar solution and heating in a water bath for about 45 minutes; the crystals form on slow cooling. The reaction involves only the carbonyl carbon and the adjacent carbon (C-1 and C-2) of the sugar, so glucose, mannose and fructose give the same osazone (glucosazone). Osazones have characteristic crystal shapes, melting points and precipitation times — glucosazone forms needle-shaped crystals like a broom or hedgehog, lactosazone forms sunflower/petal-shaped crystals and maltosazone forms sunflower-shaped crystals — so they are used in the identification of sugars.

  12. Describe the levels of protein structure.
    Topic: Core Subjective Topics

    Protein structure is described at four levels. Primary structure is the linear sequence of amino acid residues in the polypeptide chain, held by covalent peptide bonds (and disulfide bonds); it has two ends, the amino terminus (N-terminus) and the carboxyl terminus (C-terminus). Secondary structure refers to particularly stable arrangements of amino acid residues giving rise to recurring structural patterns, e.g. the alpha helix and beta conformation. Tertiary structure is the overall three-dimensional folding of a single polypeptide chain. Quaternary structure is the arrangement in space of two or more polypeptide subunits in a multi-subunit protein.

  13. What is cooperative binding of oxygen by hemoglobin?
    Topic: Core Subjective Topics

    Hemoglobin binds four oxygen molecules, one at each of its four heme groups. The binding of oxygen to one heme group increases the affinity of the remaining heme groups for oxygen, so successive oxygen molecules bind more and more easily; this is cooperative binding. It is associated with a conformational change from the T (taut/tense) state, in which the binding sites are hindered and the oxygen affinity is low, to the R (relaxed) state, in which the binding sites are open and the affinity is high. The net effect is that the affinity of hemoglobin for the last oxygen bound is approximately 300 times greater than its affinity for the first, and the oxygen dissociation curve of hemoglobin is sigmoidal (S-shaped).

  14. Explain D and L isomerism in carbohydrates.
    Topic: Core Subjective Topics

    The designation of a sugar as the D form or the L form is determined by its spatial relationship to the parent compound of the carbohydrates, glyceraldehyde. It depends on the orientation of the -H and -OH groups around the carbon atom most distant from the carbonyl carbon, i.e. the penultimate carbon (carbon 5 in glucose). When the -OH group on this carbon is on the right side, the sugar belongs to the D series; when it is on the left, the sugar belongs to the L series. Most naturally occurring sugars are D-sugars.

  15. Write the biomedical importance of oligosaccharides.
    Topic: Core Subjective Topics

    Oligosaccharides are short chains of 3-10 monosaccharide units joined by glycosidic linkages, and they are covalently attached to many proteins and lipids. Integral membrane proteins carry oligosaccharides on their extracellular face, and many secreted proteins such as antibodies and coagulation factors also contain oligosaccharide units. They participate in molecular targeting and cell-to-cell recognition, they mark the passage of time and determine when proteins are removed from the circulation, and the human ABO blood groups illustrate the effects of glycosyltransferases: the A and B antigens differ from the O antigen by the addition of one extra monosaccharide (N-acetylgalactosamine for A, galactose for B) through an alpha-1,3 linkage.

  16. Differentiate between homopolysaccharides and heteropolysaccharides.
    Topic: Core Subjective Topics

    Homopolysaccharides are polymers built of only one kind of monosaccharide unit, e.g. starch, glycogen, cellulose, inulin, dextrins and dextrans, all made of glucose or a single sugar unit. Heteropolysaccharides are polymers built of two or more different kinds of monosaccharide units or their derivatives, e.g. the mucopolysaccharides (glycosaminoglycans) and the bacterial peptidoglycan, which is built from two alternating monosaccharide units and provides extracellular support.

  17. Describe the beta sheet (beta conformation) of the secondary structure of proteins.
    Topic: Core Subjective Topics

    The beta conformation is a regular secondary structure in which the polypeptide chain is almost fully extended but slightly puckered (zigzag). Several such strands lie side by side to form a beta sheet (beta pleated sheet), which maximizes hydrogen bonding between the peptide backbones of neighbouring strands. A beta strand is typically 3 to 10 amino acids long. If the strands run in the same direction the sheet is parallel; if in opposite directions, antiparallel. Clusters of twisted beta strands form the core of many globular proteins. Beta bends (turns), usually of four residues, connect the strands and reverse the direction of the chain.

  18. What is the role of proton binding with the heme group in tissues and in the lungs?
    Topic: Core Subjective Topics

    Oxygen and protons (H+) bind at different sites in hemoglobin, but their binding is reciprocally coupled (the Bohr effect). In the tissues, where the pH is lower because of CO2 and H+ produced by metabolism, proton binding stabilizes deoxyhemoglobin in the T state, lowers its oxygen affinity and promotes the release of oxygen to the tissues; deoxyhemoglobin binds one proton for every two O2 molecules released, which also contributes to the buffering capacity of blood. In the lungs, the higher pH and exhalation of CO2 cause deprotonation, stabilize the R state, increase oxygen affinity and favour the loading of oxygen. Hemoglobin thus transports about 40% of the total H+ formed in the tissues to the lungs and kidneys.

  19. Write the functions and properties of lipids.
    Topic: Core Subjective Topics

    Functions: lipids act as an energy reserve and are energy-rich molecules; they are structural components of biological membranes (phospholipids, sterols); they insulate the body and protect organs such as the heart and kidneys from injury (fat padding); they regulate hormones (steroid hormones), transmit nerve impulses and transport fat-soluble nutrients/vitamins. Properties: lipids may be liquid or non-crystalline solids at room temperature; pure fats and oils are colourless, odourless and tasteless; they are insoluble in water but soluble in organic solvents; fats are solid and oils are liquid at room temperature.

  20. Name the factors that influence the free energy of protein molecules. Also write the equation for the change in free energy.
    Topic: Core Subjective Topics

    The free-energy difference between the folded (native) and unfolded (denatured) states of a protein is small, about 5-15 kcal/mol. The factors influencing the free energy of protein molecules are: (1) hydrophobicity (hydrophobic interactions), (2) hydrogen bonds, (3) electrostatic interactions, and (4) conformational entropy due to restricted motion. The change in free energy at constant temperature and pressure is given by: delta G = delta H - T delta S, where delta G is the free-energy change, delta H is the enthalpy change, T is the absolute temperature and delta S is the entropy change.

  21. What is keratin?
    Topic: Core Subjective Topics

    Keratin is a fibrous structural protein of the family of closely related proteins that are nearly entirely alpha-helical. Keratins are insoluble, mechanically strong proteins that form the major component of hair, nails, hoofs, horns, wool and the outer layer of skin. Their alpha helices are coiled together and cross-linked by disulfide bonds, which give keratin its toughness; the more disulfide cross-links, the harder the keratin (e.g. in horns compared with hair).

  22. What is lactose?
    Topic: Core Subjective Topics

    Lactose is a disaccharide, the principal sugar of milk, made of galactose and glucose joined by a beta(1-4) glycosidic linkage. It is a reducing sugar and is dextrorotatory. In the lactating mammary gland lactose is synthesized from glucose, and in the intestine it is hydrolysed by the enzyme lactase, present in the intestinal brush border. Deficiency of lactase causes lactose intolerance, in which the body cannot easily digest lactose. Bacteria such as E. coli convert the lactose of milk to lactic acid (by beta-galactosidase), causing souring of milk.

  23. What is a motif in a protein?
    Topic: Core Subjective Topics

    A motif, also called a super-secondary structure, is a recognizable folding pattern involving two or more elements of secondary structure and the connection(s) between them. Motifs are structural units intermediate between secondary and tertiary structure; examples include the beta barrel and combinations of alpha helices and beta strands that recur in many proteins and are often associated with particular functions.

  24. Explain the induced fit model.
    Topic: Core Subjective Topics

    A molecule bound reversibly by a protein is called its ligand, and it binds at a specific binding site that is complementary to the ligand in size, shape, charge and hydrophobicity. According to the induced fit model, the binding site is not perfectly rigid: binding of the ligand induces a conformational change in the protein so that the site fits the ligand more closely. This structural adaptation between protein and ligand is called induced fit, and such conformational changes are also the basis of cooperativity in multisubunit proteins.

  25. What is amyloidosis?
    Topic: Core Subjective Topics

    Amyloidosis is a generic term for the extracellular tissue deposition of fibrils (amyloid) composed of low molecular weight subunits of a variety of misfolded proteins, e.g. immunoglobulin light chains. A soluble protein normally secreted from the cell is secreted in a misfolded state and converted into an insoluble extracellular amyloid fibre. Diseases arising from this common misfolding mechanism are collectively called amyloidoses and include Alzheimer's disease (deposition of amyloid-beta protein cleaved from the amyloid precursor protein, APP) and type 2 diabetes (deposition of islet amyloid polypeptide/amylin in the pancreatic islets).

  26. What is protein folding? Describe the steps of protein folding.
    Topic: Core Subjective Topics

    Protein folding is the physical process by which a polypeptide folds into its characteristic and functional three-dimensional (native) structure from a random coil. In cells, folding takes place in an orderly, guided, stepwise fashion: as the newly synthesized polypeptide emerges from the ribosome, short segments fold into secondary structural units, providing local regions of organized structure; each element of secondary or super-secondary structure then facilitates proper folding by directing the process toward the native conformation and away from unproductive alternatives. Folding is dictated by thermodynamics, and the native form is also the most energetically favoured form. Auxiliary proteins called chaperones help overcome kinetic barriers during folding.

  27. Define renaturation. Explain the renaturation of ribonuclease A.
    Topic: Core Subjective Topics

    If a denatured protein regains its native structure and biological activity when returned to conditions in which the native conformation is stable, the process is called renaturation. The classic example is ribonuclease A: purified ribonuclease A is completely denatured in a concentrated urea solution in the presence of a reducing agent, which breaks its disulfide bonds and leaves it randomly coiled and inactive. When the urea and the reducing agent are removed, the denatured ribonuclease spontaneously refolds into its correct tertiary structure and its catalytic activity is restored, showing that the information for folding lies in the amino acid sequence.

  28. Write the physical and chemical properties of fatty acids.
    Topic: Core Subjective Topics

    Physical properties: fatty acids are long-chain carboxylic acids with hydrocarbon chains of 4 to 36 carbons, mostly with an even number of carbons; saturated fatty acids are solid at room temperature while unsaturated ones are liquids (oils); the melting point increases with chain length and decreases with the number of double bonds; they are insoluble in water (except very short-chain ones) and soluble in organic solvents. Chemical properties: the carboxyl group can form salts (soaps) with alkalis (saponification) and esters with alcohols; unsaturated fatty acids undergo hydrogenation (addition of hydrogen across double bonds, converting them to saturated fatty acids and hardening oils), oxidation and halogenation; by reduction of the carboxylic group they can produce alkyl alcohols.

  29. What are monosaccharides? Give their classification.
    Topic: Core Subjective Topics

    Monosaccharides are the simplest carbohydrates — polyhydroxy aldehydes or ketones that cannot be hydrolysed into simpler sugars; examples are glucose, fructose and ribose. They are classified in two ways: (1) by the nature of the carbonyl group — aldoses (aldehyde group, e.g. glyceraldehyde, glucose) and ketoses (ketone group, e.g. dihydroxyacetone, fructose); and (2) by the number of carbon atoms — trioses (3C), tetroses (4C), pentoses (5C, e.g. ribose), hexoses (6C, e.g. glucose, fructose, galactose) and heptoses (7C). The two simplest monosaccharides are the trioses glyceraldehyde (aldotriose) and dihydroxyacetone (ketotriose).

  30. What is geometric isomerism?
    Topic: Core Subjective Topics

    Geometric isomers are stereoisomers that differ in the arrangement of substituent groups with respect to a non-rotating double bond. Because rotation about the double bond is not possible, the groups on the double-bonded carbons may lie on the same side (cis) or on opposite sides (trans) of the bond. Geometric isomers are separable and have distinct chemical and physical properties; in fatty acids, cis double bonds introduce a bend in the chain, whereas trans forms are nearly straight.

  31. Give the characteristics of furfural compounds.
    Topic: Core Subjective Topics

    Furfural compounds are formed when monosaccharides are decomposed by hot concentrated acids: pentoses yield furfural with 12% HCl, whereas hexoses give hydroxymethyl furfural with hot strong acids. The furfural products then condense with certain organic phenols (such as alpha-naphthol) to form compounds with characteristic colours. This reaction is the basis of tests for the detection of sugars, such as Molisch's test.

  32. Write the biomedical importance of carbohydrates.
    Topic: Core Subjective Topics

    Carbohydrates are the chief source of energy for the body; they are constituents of compound lipids and conjugated proteins; lactose is the principal sugar of milk; their degradation products are used for the synthesis of fatty acids, amino acids and cholesterol; they are constituents of mucopolysaccharides that form the ground substance of mesenchymal tissues; certain derivatives are used as drugs (cardiac glycosides); and their metabolism is clinically important because enzyme deficiencies cause galactosemia, glycogen storage diseases and lactose intolerance, and glucose metabolism is deranged in diabetes mellitus (with glucosuria in poorly controlled cases).

  33. Write five examples of the biomedical importance of proteins.
    Topic: Core Subjective Topics

    (1) Respiratory proteins: heme-containing proteins such as hemoglobin are involved in respiration and oxygen transport. (2) Digestive proteins: enzymes such as amylase, pepsin and lipases digest food materials (carbohydrates, proteins and lipids). (3) Transport proteins: proteins transport various substances from one part of the body to another, e.g. hemoglobin and transferrin. (4) Protective proteins: fibrinogen forms the fibrin clot and stops bleeding; immunoglobulins protect against microbes. (5) Structural role: proteins are an essential component of protoplasm and form body structures such as collagen and keratin.

  34. Enlist the essential amino acids.
    Topic: Core Subjective Topics

    Essential amino acids cannot be made by the body and must come from food. Nine of the twenty amino acids are essential: histidine, isoleucine, leucine, lysine, methionine, phenylalanine, threonine, tryptophan and valine.

  35. What are ketogenic amino acids?
    Topic: Core Subjective Topics

    Amino acids whose catabolism yields acetoacetate or its precursors (acetyl-CoA or acetoacyl-CoA) are termed ketogenic amino acids, because these products can form ketone bodies rather than glucose. Leucine and lysine are the only exclusively ketogenic amino acids in proteins. (Amino acids whose catabolism yields pyruvate or other gluconeogenesis substrates are called glucogenic.)

  36. What are enantiomers? Write their characteristics. How do they differ from diastereomers?
    Topic: Core Subjective Topics

    Enantiomers are chiral molecules that are non-superimposable mirror images of one another. Characteristics: they have identical physical and chemical properties except that they rotate the plane of polarized light in opposite directions (one dextrorotatory, the other levorotatory) and react differently with other chiral substances/enzymes; they contain an asymmetric (chiral) carbon with four different groups attached; an equal mixture of the two is optically inactive and is called a racemic mixture. Diastereomers are stereoisomers whose molecules are not mirror images of one another and are not superimposable; they have different physical properties, and unlike enantiomers they can be interconverted only by breaking covalent bonds.

  37. What is HSP60?
    Topic: Core Subjective Topics

    HSP60 is a family of chaperone proteins (chaperonins) that assist protein folding. The Hsp60 chaperonins differ in sequence and structure from the Hsp70 family and act later in the folding process, often together with an Hsp70 chaperone. They form a multi-subunit, barrel-shaped structure; the unfolded protein fits into the barrel cavity, which excludes water and serves as a template (an isolated environment) for the folding process, using energy from ATP hydrolysis.

  38. What are chaperones? Write their main function.
    Topic: Core Subjective Topics

    Chaperones are auxiliary proteins that assist the folding of other proteins. Cells employ chaperones to overcome the kinetic barriers encountered while a folding protein passes through many high-energy conformations. Chaperones use energy provided by ATP hydrolysis to assist folding; they prevent immature (premature) folding of the nascent polypeptide emerging from the ribosome and help it reach its final native structure. They participate in the folding of over half of mammalian proteins; examples are the Hsp70 and Hsp60 (chaperonin) families.

  39. How is Duchenne muscular dystrophy caused?
    Topic: Core Subjective Topics

    Duchenne muscular dystrophy is caused by a deletion of a larger portion of the polypeptide chain of the protein dystrophin: a large segment of the dystrophin gene on the X chromosome is deleted, resulting in a small, truncated dystrophin protein. Because the primary structure of the protein is altered, its function is changed/lost, and the muscle fibres progressively weaken. It illustrates how a change in the amino acid sequence (primary structure) of a protein causes disease.

  40. Differentiate between hemiacetals and hemiketals.
    Topic: Core Subjective Topics

    A hemiacetal is formed by the reaction of an alcohol with an aldehyde, whereas a hemiketal is formed by the reaction of an alcohol with a ketone. In monosaccharides both reacting groups belong to the same molecule, so aldoses cyclize to hemiacetals (e.g. glucopyranose) and ketoses cyclize to hemiketals (e.g. fructofuranose). The carbonyl carbon that becomes the hemiacetal/hemiketal carbon is called the anomeric carbon.

  41. What are anomers? What is the anomeric carbon?
    Topic: Core Subjective Topics

    Anomers are isomeric forms of monosaccharides that differ only in the configuration about the hemiacetal or hemiketal carbon atom. That carbonyl carbon (C-1 in aldoses) which becomes a new chiral centre on cyclization is called the anomeric carbon. The two anomers are designated alpha and beta; they are not mirror images (they are diastereomers), so enzymes can distinguish them and use one preferentially — e.g. glycogen is synthesized from alpha-D-glucopyranose while cellulose is made from beta-D-glucopyranose. In aqueous solution the alpha and beta anomers of D-glucose interconvert (mutarotation) to a mixture of about one-third alpha and two-thirds beta.

  42. What is a domain in a protein? Write its function.
    Topic: Core Subjective Topics

    The tertiary structure of large, complex proteins is often described in terms of physically independent regions called structural domains. A domain is a section of the protein structure sufficient to perform a particular chemical or physical task, such as the binding of a substrate or another ligand. Domains fold independently, often have distinct functions, and a single protein may contain several domains performing different tasks.

  43. How is the alpha helix stabilized? Which amino acids disturb the alpha helix, and why is glycine not frequent in the alpha helix?
    Topic: Core Subjective Topics

    The alpha helix is stabilized by hydrogen bonding within the chain: every peptide bond (except those close to each end of the helix) participates in hydrogen bonding between the carbonyl oxygen of one residue and the amino hydrogen of the residue four positions ahead. The helix is disturbed by bulky or charged residues: the bulk and shape of serine, threonine and cysteine residues can destabilize the helix if they occur close together, and charged R groups (aspartate, lysine) can also disrupt it. Proline introduces a destabilizing kink because its nitrogen atom is part of a rigid ring and rotation about the N-C bond is not possible. Glycine is not frequent in the alpha helix because it is very small and flexible — it allows too much freedom of rotation and destabilizes the helix (glycine and proline are instead common in beta turns).

  44. Define tautomers (tautomerization).
    Topic: Core Subjective Topics

    Tautomerization is the shifting of a hydrogen atom from one carbon atom to another, producing enediols (compounds with a double bond and two hydroxyl groups on adjacent carbons). For example, when glucose is kept in alkaline solution for several hours it isomerizes, through an enediol intermediate, to a mixture of D-fructose and D-mannose. The interconvertible forms are called tautomers.

  45. Give examples of protein denaturation from daily life.
    Topic: Core Subjective Topics

    Denaturation is the loss of the three-dimensional structure of a protein sufficient to cause loss of its function. Daily-life examples: (1) curdling of milk when its acidity increases; (2) thermal denaturation on cooking, e.g. the setting of egg white on heating; (3) mechanical denaturation when whisking an egg; (4) perming of hair, in which disulfide bonds are broken and then re-formed in new positions.

  46. What is the equilibrium constant of a reversible reaction?
    Topic: Core Subjective Topics

    For a reversible reaction, the equilibrium constant (Keq) expresses the tendency of the reaction to proceed. For the ionization HA -> A- + H+, Keq is the acid dissociation (ionization) constant: the stronger the acid, the greater its tendency to lose a proton and the larger its Keq. Ionization constants are designated pK (pKa = -log Ka); the stronger the tendency to give up a proton, the stronger the acid and the lower its pKa. At the midpoint of a titration the concentrations of proton donor and acceptor are equal and pH = pKa numerically.

  47. Differentiate between the secondary and tertiary structure of proteins.
    Topic: Core Subjective Topics

    Secondary structure refers to particularly stable arrangements of amino acid residues giving rise to recurring structural patterns (alpha helix, beta conformation, beta turns); it involves only local regions of the chain and is stabilized mainly by hydrogen bonds between peptide-bond groups. Tertiary structure refers to all aspects of the complete three-dimensional folding of a whole polypeptide chain, including the arrangement of distant residues and side chains; it is stabilized by hydrophobic interactions, electrostatic interactions, hydrogen bonds and disulfide bridges.

  48. Define electrostatic forces in protein interactions.
    Topic: Core Subjective Topics

    Electrostatic interactions are forces between two opposite charges — the attraction between a negatively charged group and a positively charged group (ionic/salt bridges). In proteins they arise between the positively charged side chains of basic amino acids (lysine, arginine, histidine) and the negatively charged side chains of acidic amino acids (aspartate, glutamate). They play an important role in protein folding and in stabilizing the three-dimensional (tertiary) structure of proteins; extremes of pH alter the net charge on the protein and disrupt these interactions, causing denaturation.

  49. Write the side-chain properties of glutamate and alanine.
    Topic: Core Subjective Topics

    Glutamate: its side chain carries a carboxyl group, so it is an acidic (negatively charged) R group; at neutral/physiological pH the side chain is fully ionized (carboxylate, COO-) and acts as a proton donor, making glutamate negatively charged. Alanine: its side chain is a simple methyl group (CH3); it is a non-polar, aliphatic amino acid. The alanine side chain is very non-reactive and is rarely directly involved in protein function, but it can play a role in substrate recognition and specificity, particularly in interactions with other non-reactive atoms such as carbon.

  50. Explain the single-letter naming symbols of amino acids.
    Topic: Core Subjective Topics

    Each amino acid has a three-letter abbreviation and a one-letter symbol (e.g. Methionine = Met = M; Arginine = Arg = R; Glutamate = Glu = E; Glutamine = Gln = Q). The rules for the one-letter symbols are: (1) if only one amino acid begins with a particular letter, that letter is used as its symbol (e.g. Isoleucine = I); (2) if more than one amino acid begins with a particular letter, the most common of them receives the letter (e.g. glycine is more common than glutamate, so Glycine = G); (3) for the remaining amino acids, a symbol is assigned that is as close in the alphabet as possible to the initial letter of the amino acid.

  51. Write the properties of proteins.
    Topic: Core Subjective Topics

    Proteins are linear polymers of amino acids joined by peptide bonds; they spontaneously fold into three-dimensional structures determined by their amino acid sequence, and their function depends directly on this structure. They contain a wide range of functional groups (alcohols, thiols, thioethers, carboxylic acids, carboxamides and basic groups), which accounts for the broad spectrum of protein functions. Proteins interact with one another and with other macromolecules to form complex assemblies that act synergistically. A protein in its natural folded, fully functional form is called a native protein; loss of the 3D structure with loss of function is denaturation, which may be reversible (renaturation) in some cases.

  52. Name two diseases caused by misfolding of proteins. How does misfolding of proteins occur?
    Topic: Core Subjective Topics

    Diseases caused by protein misfolding (amyloidoses) include Alzheimer's disease, Parkinson's disease, Huntington's disease and type 2 diabetes. Misfolding may occur spontaneously, or be caused by a mutation in a particular gene that produces an altered protein; some apparently normal proteins can, after abnormal proteolytic cleavage, take on a unique conformational state leading to long fibrillar protein assemblies (amyloid) consisting of beta pleated sheets, which are deposited in the tissues.

  53. What are digestive proteins? Explain with examples.
    Topic: Core Subjective Topics

    Digestive proteins are the digestive enzymes that digest food materials — carbohydrates, proteins and lipids — in the alimentary canal. Examples: amylase (secreted by the salivary glands and pancreas) digests starch into smaller molecules yielding maltose; maltase then cleaves maltose into two glucose molecules; pepsin digests proteins in the stomach; and lipases digest lipids. Sucrase-isomaltase, lactase and maltase are located on the surface (brush border) of the intestinal epithelial cells.

  54. Define allotropy.
    Topic: Core Subjective Topics

    Allotropy is the existence of a chemical element in two or more different forms (allotropes) that differ in the arrangement of atoms in crystalline solids, e.g. carbon exists as diamond and graphite. The different forms of the same element have different physical properties although they are made of the same atoms.

  55. What is the Bohr effect?
    Topic: Core Subjective Topics

    The Bohr effect, first described in 1904 by the Danish physiologist Christian Bohr, is the phenomenon in which hemoglobin's oxygen-binding affinity is inversely related both to acidity (H+ concentration) and to the concentration of carbon dioxide. Binding of protons stabilizes deoxyhemoglobin in the T state and promotes oxygen release in the tissues; in the lungs, loss of CO2 and protons favours oxygen loading. This reciprocal coupling of proton and oxygen binding is termed the Bohr effect.

  56. What is selenocysteine? Name two proteins that contain selenocysteine and write the function of selenocysteine in them.
    Topic: Core Subjective Topics

    Selenocysteine is the 21st amino acid; it is an analogue of cysteine in which the sulfur atom is replaced by selenium (Se). It is formed by modification of serine bound to a unique tRNA and is inserted during translation; it is located in the active sites of enzymes that participate in oxidation-reduction reactions. Examples of selenoproteins are glutathione peroxidase (protects cells against oxidative damage by reducing peroxides) and thioredoxin reductase (maintains thioredoxin in its reduced state for redox reactions); in these enzymes the selenocysteine residue takes part in the catalysis of electron-transfer reactions.

  57. What is a zwitterion? Define the isoelectric point.
    Topic: Core Subjective Topics

    A zwitterion is the dipolar form of an amino acid that carries both a positive and a negative charge. At neutral pH amino acids exist predominantly as zwitterions: the amino group is protonated (-NH3+) and the carboxyl group is deprotonated (-COO-), so the overall net charge is zero, yet the molecule can act both as an acid (proton donor) and as a base (proton acceptor); such substances are called amphoteric or ampholytes. The isoelectric point (pI) is the characteristic pH at which a molecule carries no net electrical charge.

  58. Differentiate between alpha, beta and gamma carbons.
    Topic: Core Subjective Topics

    In organic molecules the alpha carbon is the first carbon atom attached to a functional group (e.g. the carbonyl group); the second carbon atom is called the beta carbon, and the carbon next to the beta carbon is the gamma carbon. In amino acids the alpha carbon is the central carbon bonded to the amino group, carboxyl group, hydrogen and R group; additional carbons of the R group are designated beta, gamma, delta and so on, moving away from the alpha carbon.

  59. What is mutarotation?
    Topic: Core Subjective Topics

    Mutarotation is the change in optical rotation of a sugar solution because of the change in the equilibrium between its two anomers. When alpha-D-glucose or beta-D-glucose is dissolved in water, the two forms interconvert through the open-chain form until an equilibrium mixture (about one-third alpha and two-thirds beta) is reached, and the optical rotation changes accordingly.

  60. How are disulfide bridges formed in the tertiary structure of proteins?
    Topic: Core Subjective Topics

    A disulfide bridge is a covalent bond formed between two cysteine residues of a protein by the oxidation of their sulfhydryl (-SH) groups: two cysteine molecules lose their hydrogens and their sulfur atoms join (cystine). The reaction is a spontaneous (non-enzymatic) oxidation, and the enzyme protein disulfide isomerase catalyses the interchange or shuffling of disulfide bonds until the bonds of the native conformation are formed. Disulfide bridges strongly stabilize the tertiary (and quaternary) structure of proteins.

  61. Write the importance of glucose in the human body.
    Topic: Core Subjective Topics

    Glucose is the major metabolic fuel of mammals and the chief physiological sugar of the blood, kept at a fairly constant level. All tissues use glucose for energy; erythrocytes use glucose solely, and brain cells rely heavily on it. Glucose is the precursor for the synthesis of all other carbohydrates in the body: glycogen for storage in the liver and muscles, ribose and deoxyribose for nucleic acids, and galactose for lactose and glycolipids. It also combines with proteins in glycoproteins and proteoglycans. In poorly controlled diabetes mellitus, excess glucose is excreted in the urine (glucosuria).

  62. What are carbohydrates? Write the four major groups of carbohydrates.
    Topic: Core Subjective Topics

    Carbohydrates are polyhydroxy aldehydes or polyhydroxy ketones, or substances that yield such compounds on hydrolysis. The four major groups are: (1) monosaccharides — single units that cannot be hydrolysed further, e.g. glucose, fructose, ribose; (2) disaccharides — yield two monosaccharide molecules on hydrolysis, e.g. maltose, lactose, sucrose; (3) oligosaccharides — short chains of 3-10 monosaccharide units joined by glycosidic linkages; and (4) polysaccharides (glycans) — yield more than ten monosaccharide molecules on hydrolysis and contain hundreds to thousands of units, e.g. starch, glycogen and cellulose.

  63. Write the properties of non-polar aliphatic R groups. Also write the properties of the side chains of serine and threonine.
    Topic: Core Subjective Topics

    Non-polar aliphatic R groups are hydrophobic: these amino acids have non-polar side chains that do not bind or give off protons; they are oily/lipid-like and promote hydrophobic interactions, and their side chains tend to cluster together in the interior of the protein. This class includes glycine, alanine, proline, valine and leucine (and isoleucine and methionine). Serine and threonine have aliphatic, polar, uncharged R groups containing hydroxyl groups; they are more soluble in water (more hydrophilic) than the non-polar amino acids because the hydroxyl groups form hydrogen bonds with water, and being close to the main chain they can also hydrogen-bond with it; their hydroxyl groups also serve as attachment sites for phosphate groups and oligosaccharide chains.
