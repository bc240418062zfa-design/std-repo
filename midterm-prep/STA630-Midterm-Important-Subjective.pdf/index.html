  1. What is research?
    Topic: Introduction to Research

    Discuss the important characteristics of the scientific method of research. Research is the process of finding solutions to a problem after a thorough study and analysis of the situational factors; it is gathering the information needed to answer a question and thereby help in solving a problem. The scientific method of research is systematic, empirical and critical. Its important characteristics are: (1) it is based on empirical evidence obtained through observation and experience; (2) it uses relevant concepts and operational definitions; (3) it is objective and value-free; (4) it follows logical reasoning (inductive and deductive); (5) its results are testable, verifiable and replicable; (6) it is parsimonious, i.e. it prefers the simplest adequate explanation; and (7) it aims at generalization and prediction, i.e. to explain, predict and/or control phenomena.

  2. Classify research on the basis of (a) the purpose of doing research, (b) the use of research, and (c) the time dimension. (a) Purpose: Exploratory (formulative) research is done to explore a problem and gain insights and familiarity when little is known; Descriptive research describes the characteristics of a population or phenomenon as it is; Explanatory research explains why things happen and establishes cause-and-effect relationships. (b) Use: Basic (fundamental) research is undertaken to expand knowledge and develop theory without an immediate practical use, while Applied research is carried out to solve a specific, practical problem; evaluation and action research are types of applied research. (c) Time dimension: Cross-sectional research collects data at one point in time, whereas Longitudinal research collects data repeatedly over a period of time, e.g. panel studies (same respondents), cohort studies (same cohort) and time-series studies.

  3. A research was conducted by the education department to see the impact of strict teaching practices on the rate of failure among students. (1) Identify the dependent and independent variables. (2) Classify this research according to the purpose of doing research — exploratory, descriptive or explanatory — and explain your choice. (3) Classify it according to the use of research — basic or applied — and explain your choice. (Marks: 5) (1) Independent variable: strict teaching practices (the cause, which is presumed to produce the effect). Dependent variable: the rate of failure among students (the effect, which depends on teaching practices). (2) It is Explanatory research, because the study does not merely describe the failure rate; it seeks to explain the phenomenon by establishing the cause-and-effect relationship between teaching practices and student failure. (3) It is Applied research, because it is conducted by the education department to solve a practical problem (reducing student failures) and its findings will be used for decision-making, rather than for pure theory building.

  4. Defend the use of the term 'inductive' to describe qualitative data analysis and interpretation. (Marks: 5) Qualitative data analysis is rightly called inductive because it proceeds from the particular to the general — the opposite direction of deduction. The qualitative researcher does not begin with a theory or hypotheses to be tested; instead, categories, themes and patterns are allowed to emerge from the data themselves during collection, coding and interpretation (as in grounded theory). Hypotheses in qualitative studies are often generated as the data are collected, interpreted and analyzed, rather than stated in advance. Through constant comparison of incidents and cases, the researcher builds concepts and generalizations upward from the empirical observations. Hence 'inductive' accurately describes the logic of qualitative analysis: theory is the outcome of the research, not its starting point.

  5. Define concept, construct and variable. What is the difference between a conceptual (theoretical) definition and an operational definition?
    Topic: Concepts and Definitions

    A concept is a generalized idea about a class of objects, attributes, occurrences or processes, e.g. leadership, motivation, productivity. A construct is a concept that is deliberately invented for a special scientific purpose and is expressed at a higher level of abstraction, e.g. intelligence or job satisfaction. A variable is anything that can take on differing or varying values (e.g. age, income, performance); all variables are concepts, but all concepts are not variables. A conceptual/theoretical definition explains a concept in terms of other concepts (e.g. the dictionary-type meaning of the concept). An operational definition, by contrast, defines a concept in terms of the specific, empirical procedures and measures used to observe it — e.g. 'job satisfaction' operationalized as the score obtained on a specified five-point satisfaction scale. The facts that should be collected to measure a variable depend upon its operational definition; vague meanings attached to a concept create problems of measurement.

  6. What are the ways to get conceptualization about a concept?
    Topic: Concepts and Definitions

    (Marks: 3) Conceptualization is the process of refining an abstract concept by giving it a clear conceptual or theoretical definition. A researcher can develop the conceptualization of a concept by: (1) reviewing the relevant literature to see how earlier researchers and theorists have defined and used the concept; (2) consulting dictionaries, encyclopedias and standard textbooks for accepted definitions; (3) discussing the concept with experts and experienced researchers in the field; (4) identifying the dimensions and facets of the concept, i.e. breaking it into its constituent aspects; and (5) comparing it with related and contrasting concepts so that its boundaries and scope become clear.

  7. What is a theoretical framework?
    Topic: Variables and Theoretical Framework

    Discuss its basic features (components). A theoretical framework is a logically developed, described and elaborated network of associations among the variables that have been identified through the literature survey, interviews and observations; it provides the conceptual model of the study. Its basic features/components are: (1) The relevant variables are clearly identified and labelled (independent, dependent, moderating, intervening/extraneous); (2) It states how two or more variables are related to each other (nature and direction of relationships); (3) It explains the logic underlying the relationships, i.e. why we would expect these relationships to exist; and (4) The relationships are presented in the form of an inventory of propositions and, where possible, a schematic diagram (model). A good theoretical framework makes the findings meaningful and generalizable.

  8. What are the various types of variables that can be identified during research?
    Topic: Variables and Theoretical Framework

    Explain with suitable examples. (Marks: 10) (1) Independent variable — the cause variable, which is presumed to influence or explain changes in another variable; e.g. training (its effect on performance is studied). (2) Dependent variable — the effect/outcome variable whose variation is explained by the independent variable; e.g. employee performance. (3) Moderating variable — a variable that modifies (strengthens or weakens) the relationship between the independent and dependent variables; e.g. the effect of training on performance may be stronger for younger employees (age moderates). (4) Intervening (mediating) variable — a variable that surfaces in between and transmits the effect of the independent variable to the dependent variable; e.g. training improves skills (intervening), which improves performance. (5) Extraneous/confounding variables — uncontrolled variables that may affect the dependent variable and threaten validity. (6) Control variables — variables that are held constant or neutralized to prevent them from influencing the outcome. Variables may also be classified as discrete (whole-number values, e.g. number of children) and continuous (any value within a range, e.g. income, weight).

  9. Why is it important to identify extraneous variables in a research study?
    Topic: Variables and Theoretical Framework

    (Marks: 5) Extraneous variables are uncontrolled variables, other than the independent variable, that can influence the dependent variable. It is important to identify them because, if they are left uncontrolled, they confound the results: the researcher cannot be sure whether the observed change in the dependent variable was produced by the independent variable or by the extraneous factor. This threatens the internal validity of the study and weakens any cause-and-effect conclusion. Once identified, extraneous variables can be controlled through randomization, matching, holding them constant, blocking, or statistical techniques such as analysis of covariance, so that the true effect of the independent variable can be isolated and the findings become trustworthy.

  10. What is a hypothesis?
    Topic: Hypotheses

    Discuss the types of hypotheses and the characteristics of a good hypothesis. A hypothesis is a tentative, testable statement (prediction) about the relationship between two or more variables, derived logically from theory. Types: (1) Descriptive hypothesis — states the existence, size or distribution of a variable (e.g. '60% of employees are dissatisfied'); (2) Relational/co-relational hypothesis — states that two variables are associated (e.g. 'High productivity is positively associated with job commitment'); (3) Explanatory (causal) hypothesis — states a cause-and-effect relationship (e.g. 'Extensive use of chocolate causes pimples'); (4) Directional hypothesis — specifies the direction of the relationship (one-tailed), while a Non-directional hypothesis states a relationship without direction (two-tailed); (5) Null hypothesis (H0) — states the absence of any relationship/difference, which is tested statistically (hypothesis testing is also called a significance test). Characteristics of a good hypothesis: it should be conceptually clear, specific and stated in testable form, have empirical referents, be consistent with existing knowledge, and be stated before the data are collected (in quantitative research; in qualitative studies hypotheses are often generated as data are collected and analyzed).

  11. What is a literature review?
    Topic: Literature Review

    Discuss its purposes and the main sources of literature review. A literature review is a systematic survey and critical summary of the published work (books, journals, reports and previous research) relevant to the research problem. Its purposes are: (1) to find out what is already known about the area and avoid duplication; (2) to identify inconsistencies, gaps and unanswered questions in the existing knowledge; (3) to provide a solid foundation for developing the theoretical framework; (4) to help in identifying variables, formulating hypotheses and selecting an appropriate methodology; and (5) to demonstrate awareness of the theoretical context of the study. Main sources include scholarly journals, books, dissertations and theses, conference papers and proceedings, government documents and reports, bibliographical indexes and abstracts, encyclopedias, handbooks and yearbooks. (An experiment is a method of data collection, not a source of literature review.)

  12. "Because literature survey is a time-consuming exercise, a good in-depth interview will be sufficient to develop a theoretical framework." Discuss this statement. (Marks: 5) The statement is not acceptable. A theoretical framework is a conceptual model that identifies the important variables and states the relationships among them, and it must rest on the body of knowledge already established in the field. In-depth interviews with a few people can only give preliminary, experience-based information about the problem situation; they cannot substitute for the literature survey, which (1) shows what is already known and which variables earlier studies found significant, (2) reveals the theories and findings on which the proposed relationships can be logically grounded, (3) prevents duplication and helps identify research gaps, and (4) guides the formulation of hypotheses. Interviews are a part of preliminary data collection and supplement the literature survey, but without the survey the framework would be based on opinion rather than evidence and would lack a solid foundation.
    Topic: Literature Review

  13. What is meant by problem definition?
    Topic: Problem Definition and Research Process

    Discuss the techniques a researcher uses to narrow down a broad problem area into an investigable research question. (Marks: 10) Problem definition means clearly identifying and stating the real problem to be investigated; a problem well defined is half solved. The research process begins with a broad problem area, which is narrowed down through: (1) Preliminary data collection — reviewing literature and holding discussions/interviews with people in the setting to understand the nature of the problem; (2) Identifying the symptoms and distinguishing them from the real problem; (3) Reviewing the existing literature to see how similar problems were studied; (4) Delimiting the scope of the study in terms of unit of analysis, time and setting; (5) Stating the specific problem and formulating clear, empirical and testable research questions; and (6) Developing the theoretical framework and hypotheses. In this way a vague managerial concern (e.g. declining productivity) is converted into a specific researchable question.

  14. What is a research proposal?
    Topic: Problem Definition and Research Process

    Explain its different sections. (Marks: 10) A research proposal is a written plan/document that presents the research problem and the detailed procedures proposed for investigating it, so that its significance and feasibility can be evaluated before the study is conducted. Its main sections are: (1) Introduction — background of the study, statement of the problem, objectives, research questions/hypotheses and significance of the study; (2) Literature Review — summary and critique of relevant previous research and the theoretical framework; (3) Methodology/Research Design — research design, population and sampling technique, instruments/tools of data collection, variables and their operational definitions, and procedures; (4) Data Analysis — the techniques proposed for processing and analyzing the data; (5) Time Schedule and Budget; and (6) References/Bibliography. (Results and conclusions are not part of a proposal, as the study has not yet been conducted.)

  15. Discuss the steps involved in the research process.
    Topic: Problem Definition and Research Process

    The research process consists of the following main steps: (1) Defining the broad problem area — identifying the general area/situation that needs investigation; (2) Preliminary data collection — literature review and discussions to understand the problem background; (3) Problem definition — stating the specific problem and research questions precisely; (4) Developing the theoretical framework — identifying variables and their relationships on the basis of the literature survey; (5) Formulation of hypotheses; (6) Research design — decisions about study type, sampling design, data collection methods and measurement; (7) Data collection; (8) Data analysis and interpretation — processing, testing hypotheses and drawing conclusions; and (9) Report writing and presentation of findings. The first step of the process is defining the broad problem area, and the final step of a scientific investigation is reporting the findings.

  16. Researchers interviewed a group of people in a specific room. They were told that only their interaction with each other and their points of view on a specific issue were being studied, but in fact high-frequency sound waves were passed through that room and their effect on human behaviour was being observed. Identify and discuss the ethical issue involved. (Marks: 5) The ethical issue involved is deception of the research participants, together with the absence of informed consent. The participants consented to a study of their interaction and views, whereas the actual treatment — exposure to high-frequency sound waves — was concealed from them. This violates research ethics in several ways: (1) Informed consent: participants must be told the true purpose and procedures of the study and what will be done to them before they agree to participate; (2) Right to safety: exposing people to sound waves whose effects on health and behaviour are unknown may cause physical or psychological harm; (3) Right to withdraw and to privacy were compromised, as the subjects did not know they were experimental subjects at all. Deception in business/social research — e.g. representing the research as something it is not — is regarded as unethical; where any deception is unavoidable, ethics committees require justification and full debriefing afterwards, which was also missing here.

  17. Involvement with deviants is an ethical dilemma of field research. Explain. (Marks: 3) In field research (e.g. ethnographic observation of deviant or criminal groups), the researcher must gain acceptance and build rapport with the group to study it from inside. This creates an ethical dilemma: to maintain access and trust, the researcher may witness — or be expected to participate in — illegal or immoral activities (drug use, theft, violence). Participation can make the researcher legally liable and morally compromised, while refusing or reporting may endanger the researcher, expose the group and destroy the research. The researcher also faces conflicting obligations of confidentiality to the participants versus duty to society and law. Hence involvement with deviants is a classic ethical dilemma of field research, requiring the researcher to set clear limits in advance on how far involvement will go.

  18. Discuss the four levels (scales) of measurement: nominal, ordinal, interval and ratio.
    Topic: Scales of Measurement

    (1) Nominal scale — the least powerful scale; it only classifies objects/persons into categories with no order or magnitude, e.g. gender, religion, nationality. Only counting/frequency statistics are permissible. (2) Ordinal scale — has all the characteristics of the nominal scale plus ranking/order of objects, but it does not tell the magnitude of differences between ranks, e.g. preference ranks, socio-economic classes. (3) Interval scale — has order and equal intervals between values, but no true zero; differences are meaningful but ratios are not, e.g. temperature in Celsius/Fahrenheit. (4) Ratio scale — the most powerful scale; it has order, equal intervals and an absolute (true) zero, so ratios are meaningful, e.g. income, weight, age. The scale used determines which statistical techniques can legitimately be applied to the data.

  19. Highlight the difference between interval and ratio scales of measurement. (Marks: 5) Both are quantitative scales with equal intervals between values; the difference lies in the zero point. An interval scale has an arbitrary zero that does not indicate the complete absence of the attribute — for example, 0 degrees Celsius does not mean 'no temperature', and 20 degrees C is not twice as hot as 10 degrees C, so ratios are not meaningful. A ratio scale has an absolute (true) zero indicating absence of the attribute — for example, zero income means no income, and a weight of 40 kg is exactly twice a weight of 20 kg, so ratios are meaningful. Because of the true zero, all arithmetic and statistical operations are permissible on ratio data (income, weight, age, height), whereas interval data (temperature, calendar years, IQ) permit addition and subtraction but not meaningful multiplication or division.

  20. What is the primary difference between test/retest reliability and internal consistency reliability?
    Topic: Validity and Reliability

    (Marks: 3) Both are measures of the reliability (consistency) of an instrument, but they differ in what is repeated. Test/retest reliability is the stability of a measure over time: the same instrument is administered to the same respondents on two different occasions, and the correlation between the two sets of scores shows consistency — a measure that gives consistent results over multiple occasions has high test-retest reliability. Internal consistency is the homogeneity of the items within a single administration: it checks whether all the items of a scale consistently measure the same concept, i.e. each item correlates with the other items (commonly measured by Cronbach's alpha). In short, test-retest = consistency across time/occasions; internal consistency = consistency across items at one time.

  21. Differentiate between validity and reliability.
    Topic: Validity and Reliability

    Also explain internal validity and external validity. Reliability refers to the consistency of the scores obtained from a measure — if repeated measurements give the same results, the instrument is reliable. Validity refers to the accuracy of a measure — whether the instrument actually measures what it is intended to measure. A measure can be reliable without being valid (consistently wrong), but a valid measure must be reliable. Internal validity is the confidence that the changes observed in the dependent variable were actually produced by the experimental manipulation (independent variable) and not by extraneous factors; when we are confident the manipulation produced the measured changes, the study has good internal validity. External validity is the generalizability of the findings to other settings, people and times; quantitative researchers' preoccupation with generalization is an attempt to establish external validity (law-like findings). Highly controlled studies often gain internal validity at the cost of external validity.

  22. Explain the different types of validity of a measuring instrument.
    Topic: Validity and Reliability

    (Marks: 5) (1) Face validity — the instrument appears, on its face, to measure what it is supposed to measure; it is the weakest form but helps gain respondents' cooperation. (2) Content validity — the extent to which the instrument adequately covers all the dimensions of the concept being measured, usually judged by experts. (3) Criterion-related validity — the scores on the instrument agree with an external criterion; it has two forms: predictive validity (the instrument predicts a future criterion, e.g. an entry test predicting later academic performance) and concurrent validity (agreement with a criterion measured at the same time). (4) Construct validity — the extent to which the instrument measures the theoretical construct it claims to measure, evidenced by convergent validity (agreement with other measures of the same construct) and discriminant validity (lack of agreement with measures of unrelated constructs).

  23. How would you differentiate between the History Effect and Mechanical Loss in experimental research?
    Topic: Research Design and Survey Research

    (Marks: 3) Both are threats connected with the conduct of an experiment, but they are different in nature. History Effect refers to extraneous events that occur during the course of the experiment (between the pre-test and post-test) which are not part of the treatment but can affect the dependent variable — e.g. a government policy announcement during a study of advertising effects; it threatens internal validity. Mechanical Loss (also called experimental mortality/attrition) refers to the loss of subjects or data during the experiment because of mechanical/technical failures or drop-outs — e.g. equipment breakdown, recording failure, or respondents leaving the experiment midway — which reduces the sample and may bias the results. In short: history = outside events influencing results; mechanical loss = loss of subjects/data due to failures in the experimental arrangements.

  24. Briefly discuss the Solomon Four Group Design in experimental research with the help of an example. The Solomon Four Group Design is a true experimental design that controls the sensitizing effect of the pre-test by combining the pre-test/post-test control group design with the post-test-only control group design. It uses four randomly assigned groups: Group 1 — pre-test, treatment, post-test; Group 2 — pre-test, no treatment, post-test; Group 3 — no pre-test, treatment, post-test; Group 4 — no pre-test, no treatment, post-test. Comparing the post-tests of Groups 1 and 3 shows whether pre-testing interacted with the treatment. Example: to test a new teaching method on achievement, four randomly formed classes are used; two receive pre-tests and two do not, one pre-tested and one un-pre-tested class receive the new method, and all four are post-tested. If both treated groups outperform both control groups, the effect is attributable to the method and not to pre-test sensitization. It is considered one of the most rigorous designs, though costly.

  25. What is personal interviewing? How can it be conducted and at which places?
    Topic: Research Design and Survey Research

    (Marks: 5) Personal (face-to-face) interviewing is a method of data collection in which a trained interviewer meets the respondent in person, asks questions from an interview schedule/questionnaire and records the responses; it allows probing, clarification and observation of non-verbal cues. It can be conducted as: (1) Structured interviews — the same standardized questions in the same order for every respondent; (2) Unstructured/in-depth interviews — flexible, guided by topics, used in exploratory and qualitative research; and (3) Focus group interviews — a small group of respondents interviewed together under a moderator. Places where personal interviews are conducted include the respondent's home (door-to-door), the workplace/office, shopping malls and markets (mall intercept interviews), institutions, and any mutually convenient public place. Its advantages are a high response rate and rich data; its limitations are high cost, time consumption and possible interviewer bias.

  26. Why is preliminary notification essential in a self-administered questionnaire?
    Topic: Research Design and Survey Research

    (Marks: 3) In a self-administered (e.g. mail) questionnaire there is no interviewer present to motivate the respondent, explain the study or clarify questions, so the response rate is generally low. Preliminary notification — informing the respondents in advance (by letter, phone or message) that a questionnaire is being sent, who is sending it and why — is essential because it: (1) prepares the respondent and creates a favourable disposition toward the study; (2) establishes the legitimacy and importance of the research, reducing suspicion and refusals; (3) increases the response rate and the speed of return; and (4) works together with follow-up reminders to minimize non-response bias. Without it, many questionnaires are ignored or discarded as junk mail.

  27. Write a note on any three varieties of non-reactive (unobtrusive) observation.
    Topic: Research Design and Survey Research

    (Marks: 5) Non-reactive observation studies behaviour without the subjects knowing they are being observed, so their behaviour is not affected by the observation. Three varieties are: (1) Physical traces — studying the evidence people leave behind, divided into erosion measures (wear and tear, e.g. worn-out pages of a library book showing its popularity) and accretion measures (deposits left, e.g. litter or graffiti). (2) Archives/available data — using existing records and documents (company reports, official statistics, newspapers) which were not produced for the research and therefore are non-reactive. (3) Simple/contrived observation — observing behaviour in natural or arranged settings without the subjects' awareness, e.g. counting customers' choices in a store, or using one-way mirrors and hidden observers; contrived observation creates a situation to observe reactions. Its main advantage is the absence of observer effect, though it raises ethical questions about observation without consent.

  28. What is a questionnaire?
    Topic: Research Design and Survey Research

    Discuss the critical decisions involved in questionnaire construction and the principles of good questionnaire design. A questionnaire is a structured instrument consisting of a set of written questions to which respondents record their answers, usually within closely defined alternatives; it is the main tool of survey research and may be personally administered, mailed or electronic. Critical decision areas in its construction are: (1) Question content — deciding what to ask so that every question is relevant to the objectives; (2) Question wording — simple, unambiguous language, avoiding leading, double-barrelled and sensitive wording; (3) Response strategy/format — open-ended vs closed questions and appropriate scales; and (4) Question sequence and layout — easy, interesting questions first, difficult/demographic questions later, with clear presentation, adequate spacing and detailed, unambiguous instructions (especially important for mail questionnaires). A pilot test (small-scale trial run) should be conducted to check completion, wording and logical consistency before the questionnaire is finalized.

  29. How can the response rate of a self-administered questionnaire be increased?
    Topic: Research Design and Survey Research

    (Marks: 5) The response rate of a self-administered (e.g. mail or online) questionnaire can be increased by: (1) sending a preliminary notification informing respondents in advance about the study and requesting cooperation; (2) attaching a covering letter that explains the purpose and importance of the study, assures confidentiality and anonymity, and appeals for a response; (3) keeping the questionnaire short, simple, clearly worded and attractively laid out, with unambiguous instructions; (4) providing a stamped, self-addressed return envelope (or an easy submission link) so returning it costs the respondent nothing; (5) offering a small incentive or token of appreciation; (6) sending polite follow-up reminders and, where necessary, a fresh copy of the questionnaire to non-respondents; and (7) securing sponsorship or endorsement by a respected organization, which increases the credibility of the survey.
