  1. What are the five core principles of Money & Banking? Discuss in detail. (Marks: 5)
    Topic: The Financial System & Financial Intermediaries

    The five core principles of money and banking are: 1. Time has value: Time affects the value of financial instruments; a rupee today is worth more than a rupee promised in the future, which is why interest is paid. 2. Risk requires compensation: Investors must be compensated for taking risk; the higher the risk of an investment, the higher the return investors demand. 3. Information is the basis for decisions: Reliable information is essential for making good financial decisions; collecting and processing information is costly, which creates information problems in financial markets. 4. Markets determine prices and allocate resources: Financial markets channel funds from savers to borrowers and set prices that allocate resources efficiently. 5. Stability improves welfare: A stable financial system reduces risk and uncertainty, smooths economic fluctuations and improves the welfare of everyone in the economy.

  2. "A financial instrument is a real or virtual document representing a legal agreement involving some sort of monetary value." Discuss financial instruments by giving examples. Point out some of its uses and important characteristics. (Marks: 10)
    Topic: The Financial System & Financial Intermediaries

    A financial instrument is a written obligation of one party to transfer something of value to another party at a future date under certain conditions. By written obligation we mean that it is enforced by the government, and this obligation is an important feature of a financial instrument. The party here can be an individual, a company or a government. The future date can be specified, or it can be when some event occurs. Examples: stocks, bonds, insurance, etc. are examples of financial instruments. Characteristics of financial instruments: 1. Standardization: It is a standardized agreement which enables reduction in costs of complexity, so most financial instruments today are similar. 2. Communicate information: They provide certain important information about the issuer which otherwise would have been difficult for the lenders to gather. Value of a financial instrument depends on: Size - larger the promised payment, more valuable is the financial instrument; Timing - the sooner the payment is made, the more valuable it is; Risk - a financial instrument is more valuable if there are greater possibilities that payment will be made; Circumstances - payments made when needed the most make the financial instrument more valuable. Uses of financial instruments: 1. Store of value: Stocks - the stock holder is a part owner of the firm and receives part of its profits. Bonds - a form of loan which promises to make repayment in future dates. Bank loans - borrowers obtain resources from lenders in exchange for promised payments. 2. Transfer of risk: Insurance - takes premium to assure payment under particular conditions (accident, death, etc.). Futures contracts - an agreement to exchange a fixed quantity of a commodity or an asset at a fixed price; they transfer the risk of price fluctuations. Options - give the holder the right to purchase a fixed quantity of an underlying asset at a predetermined price within a specific period.

  3. Define financial intermediaries. What functions do financial intermediaries perform regarding savings? (Marks: 5)
    Topic: The Financial System & Financial Intermediaries

    A financial intermediary is an institution that facilitates the flow of funds between lenders and borrowers indirectly; it acts as a middleman between investors (savers) and the firms raising funds. Financial intermediaries reduce transaction costs because they specialize in the issuance of standardized securities. Regarding savings, they perform the following functions: 1. Maturity transformation: Converting short-term liabilities into long-term assets. Banks deal with a large number of lenders and borrowers and settle their conflicting needs - savers can withdraw in the short term while borrowers get long-term funds. 2. Risk transformation: Converting risky investments into relatively risk-free ones, for example by lending to multiple borrowers to spread the risk. 3. Convenience: Matching small deposits with large loans and large deposits with small loans, so savers of every size can participate.

  4. "Financial intermediaries reduce transaction costs and information costs." How? (Marks: 3)
    Topic: The Financial System & Financial Intermediaries

    Financial intermediaries reduce transaction costs because they deal in large volumes: by pooling the resources of many small savers they achieve economies of scale, so the cost of each transaction (searching for a borrower, preparing contracts, etc.) falls per rupee lent. They also develop expertise and standardized contracts, which further lowers the cost of each deal. They reduce information costs because collecting and processing information about borrowers is their specialized business. Instead of every small saver investigating each borrower separately, the intermediary does it once for everyone: it screens borrowers before lending, monitors them afterwards, and processes information about their creditworthiness at a much lower average cost.

  5. How does the financial system promote economic efficiency? (Marks: 3)
    Topic: The Financial System & Financial Intermediaries

    The financial system promotes economic efficiency in the following ways: - It channels funds from savers to the borrowers with the most productive investment opportunities, so resources are used where they earn the highest return. - By reducing transaction costs and information costs, it makes lending and borrowing cheaper and reduces the wastage of resources. - It enables risk sharing and diversification, so risky but productive projects can still be financed. - It provides liquidity and an efficient payment system, so trade and investment can take place smoothly. As a result, investment, output and income of the economy grow and resources are allocated efficiently.

  6. Money is accepted in economic exchange. Discuss. (Marks: 3)
    Topic: Money, Payments & Information Problems

    Money is accepted in exchange because everyone is confident that everyone else will also accept it in the future. This general acceptability rests on trust in the issuer (the government declares it legal tender) and on the stability of its value. Money solves the problems of barter: barter needs a double coincidence of wants and a separate price for every pair of goods, while money acts as a medium of exchange accepted by all, a unit of account in which all prices are quoted, and a store of value. As long as people trust that money will keep its value and remain acceptable, it continues to be accepted in every economic exchange; if that trust or stability is lost (for example in very high inflation), people stop accepting it.

  8. Discuss the negative consequences of information costs and also suggest their solutions. (Marks: 5)
    Topic: Money, Payments & Information Problems

    Information is costly to collect and process, and borrowers usually know more about themselves than lenders do. This asymmetric information creates two problems. Adverse selection occurs before the transaction: the borrowers most eager to take a loan are often the riskiest ones, so lenders may lend to bad borrowers or refuse to lend at all. Moral hazard occurs after the transaction: once borrowers have the money, they may take excessive risks or misuse the funds. The negative consequences are that credit is rationed, interest rates rise to cover the risk, good and productive borrowers are discouraged or excluded, investment falls, and the financial system becomes less efficient; in extreme cases markets can break down completely. Solutions: (1) Financial intermediaries specialize in producing information - they screen borrowers before lending and monitor them afterwards. (2) Collateral and net worth requirements reduce the borrower's incentive to misbehave. (3) Restrictive covenants in loan contracts limit risky activities. (4) Credit rating agencies and credit bureaus collect and sell information about borrowers. (5) Government regulation requires disclosure of information so that investors can judge borrowers properly.

  9. Discuss adverse selection in financial markets. (Marks: 3)
    Topic: Money, Payments & Information Problems

    Adverse selection is an information problem that occurs before a transaction takes place. Borrowers know more about their own risk than lenders do, and the borrowers who are most eager to obtain funds at a given interest rate are often those least likely to repay - the bad credit risks actively seek out loans. Knowing this, lenders protect themselves by charging a higher interest rate or by refusing to lend. But higher rates drive the good, safe borrowers out of the market, leaving an even worse pool of borrowers, so the quality of the remaining applicants deteriorates further. In this way adverse selection can make lenders unwilling to lend even to creditworthy borrowers, reducing the efficiency of financial markets.

  10. Differentiate between yield to maturity and current yield. (Marks: 3)
    Topic: Time Value of Money, Bond Pricing & Yields

    Yield to maturity is the interest rate that makes the present value of all the payments of a bond (all coupon payments plus the face value at maturity) exactly equal to its price today. It is the most accurate measure of the return on a bond held until maturity, and it takes into account both the coupon income and any capital gain or loss arising because the price differs from the face value. Current yield is simply the annual coupon payment divided by the price of the bond (Current yield = C / P). It measures only the coupon income relative to the price and ignores any capital gain or loss at maturity. Therefore current yield is a good approximation of yield to maturity only when the bond price is close to its face value and the maturity is long; for a bond selling at a discount, current yield understates the yield to maturity, and for a bond selling at a premium it overstates it.

  11. Define yield to maturity. Also discuss its relationship with the bond price when YTM is equal to, greater than, and less than the coupon rate. (Marks: 5)
    Topic: Time Value of Money, Bond Pricing & Yields

    Yield to maturity (YTM) is the interest rate that equates the present value of the cash flows received from a bond (coupon payments and the face value) with its price today. It is the return an investor earns if the bond is bought at the current price and held until maturity. Relationship between YTM, coupon rate and price: - When YTM = coupon rate, the bond sells at its face value (at par). - When YTM > coupon rate, the bond sells below its face value (at a discount), because the bond's fixed coupons are less attractive than the market rate, so its price must fall to raise its yield. - When YTM < coupon rate, the bond sells above its face value (at a premium), because its coupons are more attractive than the market rate, so investors bid its price up. In general, bond prices and yields move in opposite directions: when the price rises, YTM falls, and when the price falls, YTM rises.

  12. Find the yield to maturity on a one-year bond with a 12% coupon rate and face value of $100, if its price is $130. (Marks: 3)
    Topic: Time Value of Money, Bond Pricing & Yields

    For a one-year bond, price = (Coupon payment + Face value) / (1 + YTM). Coupon payment = 12% of $100 = $12, so the total payment after one year = 12 + 100 = $112. 130 = 112 / (1 + YTM) 1 + YTM = 112 / 130 = 0.8615 YTM = -0.1385, i.e. -13.85%. The yield is negative because the bond is selling at a price ($130) higher than the total amount ($112) the holder will receive at maturity.

  13. A one-year bond with a 10% coupon rate has a face value of $100 and is selling at a price of $120. Find its yield to maturity. (Marks: 3)
    Topic: Time Value of Money, Bond Pricing & Yields

    Coupon payment = 10% of $100 = $10, so the total payment after one year = 10 + 100 = $110. Price = (Coupon + Face value) / (1 + YTM) 120 = 110 / (1 + YTM) 1 + YTM = 110 / 120 = 0.9167 YTM = -0.0833, i.e. -8.33%. The yield to maturity is negative because the purchase price ($120) exceeds the total payment of $110 receivable at maturity.

  14. Find the yield to maturity on a one-year bond with a 7% coupon rate and face value of $100, if its price is $90. (Marks: 3)
    Topic: Time Value of Money, Bond Pricing & Yields

    Coupon payment = 7% of $100 = $7, so the total payment after one year = 7 + 100 = $107. Price = (Coupon + Face value) / (1 + YTM) 90 = 107 / (1 + YTM) 1 + YTM = 107 / 90 = 1.1889 YTM = 0.1889, i.e. 18.89%. The yield is higher than the coupon rate because the bond is bought at a discount ($90 against a face value of $100), so the holder earns the coupon plus a capital gain.

  15. Suppose Mr. Ahmad buys a 10-year bond with an 8% coupon rate and a face value of $100 at its face value. (i) If the interest rate remains the same (8%), what will be his rate of return? (ii) Suppose that after one year the interest rate falls and the price of the bond rises to $109.16; find the capital gain. (iii) Find his one-year holding period return. (Marks: 5)
    Topic: Time Value of Money, Bond Pricing & Yields

    (i) The bond was bought at face value, so its price equals its face value and the yield to maturity equals the coupon rate. If the interest rate stays at 8%, the price after one year is still $100, there is no capital gain or loss, and the rate of return equals the current yield = 8%. (ii) Capital gain = New price - Initial price = 109.16 - 100 = $9.16, i.e. a capital gain rate of 9.16%. (iii) One-year holding period return = (Coupon payment + Capital gain) / Initial price = (8 + 9.16) / 100 = 17.16%. So Mr. Ahmad earns a total one-year return of 17.16%, consisting of 8% coupon income and 9.16% capital gain.

  16. A corporation issues a one-year bond that promises to pay interest at 5% and has a face value of Rs. 100. If the bond is default-free and the market interest rate is also 5%, what will be the price of the bond? (Marks: 5)
    Topic: Time Value of Money, Bond Pricing & Yields

    The price of a bond is the present value of the payments it promises, discounted at the market interest rate. After one year the bond pays interest of 5% of Rs. 100 = Rs. 5 plus the face value of Rs. 100, i.e. Rs. 105 in total. Price = 105 / (1 + 0.05) = 105 / 1.05 = Rs. 100. So when the market interest rate equals the coupon rate (5%), the bond sells exactly at its face value, i.e. at par. (In general, Price = 105 / (1 + i); if the market rate rises above 5% the price falls below Rs. 100, and if it falls below 5% the price rises above Rs. 100.)

  17. If you invest $1 at 12% interest rate, what will be the future value of this amount after 20 years? (Marks: 5)
    Topic: Time Value of Money, Bond Pricing & Yields

    Future value is calculated as FV = PV (1 + i)^n. Here PV = $1, i = 12% = 0.12 and n = 20 years. FV = 1 x (1.12)^20 = $9.65 (approximately). So $1 invested today at 12% per year grows to about $9.65 after 20 years.

  18. Briefly explain the factors which shift the bond demand. (Marks: 5)
    Topic: The Bond Market, Default Risk & Taxes

    The factors that shift the demand for bonds are: 1. Wealth: An increase in wealth shifts the demand for bonds to the right, as wealthier people invest more. This happens as the economy grows during an expansion; bond prices increase and yields fall. 2. Expected inflation: A fall in expected inflation shifts the bond demand curve to the right, increasing demand at each price, raising bond prices and lowering yields. 3. Expected return on bonds relative to other assets: If the expected return on bonds rises relative to stocks and other assets, people invest more in bonds and demand increases. 4. Risk relative to alternatives: If other assets (e.g. stocks) become more risky, bonds become relatively safer, so the demand for bonds increases and the demand curve shifts to the right. 5. Liquidity of bonds relative to alternatives: If bonds become more liquid than other assets, investors prefer them and the demand for bonds increases.

  19. Equilibrium in the bond market is the point at which bond supply equals bond demand. Discuss bond supply and bond demand. (Marks: 5)
    Topic: The Bond Market, Default Risk & Taxes

    Bond demand: At lower bond prices (higher interest rates), the quantity of bonds demanded is greater, because bonds offer a higher expected return, so the bond demand curve slopes downward. Demand increases (the curve shifts right) when wealth increases, expected inflation falls, the expected return on bonds rises relative to other assets, bonds become less risky relative to alternatives, or bonds become more liquid. Bond supply: At lower bond prices (higher interest rates), borrowing becomes more expensive, so the quantity of bonds supplied is smaller; the bond supply curve slopes upward. Supply increases (shifts right) when expected inflation rises, when the government budget deficit increases, and when business conditions improve and firms have more profitable investment opportunities. Equilibrium occurs where the two curves intersect: the price at which the quantity demanded equals the quantity supplied, which also determines the equilibrium interest rate. If the price is above equilibrium there is excess supply and the price falls; if it is below equilibrium there is excess demand and the price rises, until equilibrium is restored.

  20. Discuss default risk of a bond in detail. (Marks: 5)
    Topic: The Bond Market, Default Risk & Taxes

    Default risk means there is no guarantee that a bond issuer will make the payments which he promised. The higher the default risk, the higher the probability that those who hold the bond will not receive the promised payments, and thus the higher the yield investors require. Investors are risk averse and require compensation for risk; more compensation is required for higher risk. For example, suppose the risk-free rate is 10% and ABC Corporation issues a one-year bond at 10%. The price without risk = (100 + 10) / 1.10 = Rs. 100. Now suppose there is a probability that ABC Corporation goes bankrupt and pays nothing, so there are two possible payoffs: Rs. 110 and Rs. 0. The expected payoff is less than Rs. 110, so a risk-averse investor will pay less than Rs. 100 for this bond; equivalently, the bond must offer a higher yield (a default risk premium) to compensate for the risk of default. The difference between the yield on a risky bond and the yield on a default-free bond is called the risk premium.

  21. Mr. A holds a bond issued by ABC Corporation while Mr. B holds a bond issued by the government. Which investor's return will be affected by taxes and which will not? Discuss the tax effect. (Marks: 3)
    Topic: The Bond Market, Default Risk & Taxes

    The interest income earned on bonds issued by private corporations is taxable, while the interest on government bonds enjoys preferential tax treatment (it is exempt from tax). Therefore Mr. A's return on the ABC Corporation bond will be affected by taxes: his after-tax return will be lower than the stated yield, and when tax rates rise the demand for corporate bonds falls and their yields rise relative to government bonds. Mr. B's return on the government bond will not be affected by taxes in the same way, which is why investors accept a lower yield on government bonds. This difference in tax treatment is one of the reasons bonds with identical maturity can have different interest rates.

  22. Explain interest-rate risk in your own words. (Marks: 3)
    Topic: The Bond Market, Default Risk & Taxes

    Interest-rate risk is the risk that the market value of a bond (or any fixed-income asset) will change when market interest rates change. Bond prices move in the opposite direction to interest rates: if interest rates rise after a bond is bought, its price falls, and the holder suffers a capital loss if the bond is sold before maturity; if rates fall, the price rises and the holder gains. The risk is greater for bonds with a longer time to maturity, because their prices are more sensitive to interest-rate changes. An investor who holds the bond to maturity still faces the risk that the proceeds will be reinvested at a different rate.

  23. Discuss briefly the facts about the term structure of interest rates. (Marks: 3)
    Topic: Term Structure of Interest Rates

    Three empirical facts characterize the term structure of interest rates: 1. Interest rates on bonds of different maturities move together over time: when short-term rates rise, long-term rates tend to rise as well, and vice versa. 2. When short-term interest rates are low, the yield curve is more likely to be upward sloping; when short-term rates are high, the yield curve is more likely to be downward sloping (inverted). 3. The yield curve is usually upward sloping: interest rates on long-term bonds are usually higher than interest rates on short-term bonds. A satisfactory theory of the term structure must explain all three facts; the liquidity premium theory explains all of them, which is why it is the most widely accepted theory.

  24. Why are stocks risky? (Marks: 3)
    Topic: Stocks, Stock Valuation & Risk

    Stocks are risky because their returns are uncertain. A stockholder receives income in two forms - dividends and capital gains - and neither is guaranteed. Dividends depend on the firm's profits and on the management's decision; they can be reduced or omitted altogether. The price of the stock also fluctuates with the firm's performance, market conditions and investors' expectations, so the price at which the stock can be sold may be much lower than the purchase price. Moreover, stockholders are residual claimants: in case of liquidation they are paid only after all creditors and bondholders, so they bear the greatest risk of the firm.

  25. Define stock. What are its important characteristics? (Marks: 5)
    Topic: Stocks, Stock Valuation & Risk

    A stock (share) is a financial instrument that represents ownership in a corporation. The stockholder is a part owner of the firm and is entitled to a share of its profits. Important characteristics of stock are: 1. Ownership and residual claim: Stockholders own the firm in proportion to their shares and have a residual claim on its income and assets - they are paid after all other claimants. 2. Limited liability: A stockholder's loss is limited to the amount invested; personal assets cannot be claimed for the firm's debts. 3. Dividends are not guaranteed: Unlike bond interest, dividends are paid only if the firm earns profits and the directors decide to distribute them. 4. Voting rights: Common stockholders can vote in the election of the board of directors and on major decisions of the company. 5. Transferability: Stocks can be bought and sold in the stock market, which makes them liquid. 6. No maturity date: Unlike a bond, a stock has no maturity; it remains outstanding as long as the firm exists.

  26. "People differ in their opinions of how stocks should be valued." Discuss. (Marks: 5)
    Topic: Stocks, Stock Valuation & Risk

    The fundamental value of a stock is the present value of its expected future dividends (plus the expected future price). People differ in their valuation of the same stock because they hold different information and form different expectations about the future. Investors differ in their estimates of future dividends, in the growth rate they expect, in their assessment of the risk of the firm, and therefore in the discount rate they apply. One investor may expect high growth and value the stock highly, while another, more pessimistic or more risk averse, values it lower. These differences of opinion are exactly what make trade possible: those who value the stock above its market price buy it, and those who value it below the market price sell it. New information continually changes expectations and therefore prices, which is why stock prices keep moving.

  27. "The stock market plays a crucial role in every modern capitalist economy." Discuss the statement. (Marks: 3)
    Topic: Stocks, Stock Valuation & Risk

    The stock market is crucial because it channels savings into productive investment: firms raise long-term capital by issuing shares without having to borrow from banks. It provides liquidity, since investors can sell their shares quickly, which encourages people to save and invest. It helps in price discovery - share prices signal which firms and industries are performing well, guiding resources to their best uses. It spreads the ownership of large companies among the general public and allows investors to diversify risk. It also enforces discipline on managers, because a falling share price and the threat of takeover punish poor performance. For all these reasons, a well-functioning stock market supports investment, growth and employment in a capitalist economy.

  28. Discuss bubbles in your own words. (Marks: 5)
    Topic: Stocks, Stock Valuation & Risk

    A bubble is a situation in which the price of an asset (such as stocks or real estate) rises far above its fundamental value - the present value of the income it is expected to generate. Bubbles form when optimism spreads: rising prices attract more buyers who expect prices to rise further, and people buy not because the asset is worth the price but because they expect to sell it to someone else at an even higher price. Credit expansion and speculation feed the process. Eventually confidence breaks - often after bad news or rising interest rates - buyers disappear, prices crash suddenly, and those who bought at the top suffer heavy losses. Because banks and investors are exposed, a bursting bubble can damage the whole financial system and the economy, as happened in the stock market crash of 1929 and the financial crisis of 2007-09.

  29. The founder of an automobile company is exposed to idiosyncratic as well as systematic risks. Explain both types of risk with examples relevant to this case. (Marks: 5)
    Topic: Stocks, Stock Valuation & Risk

    Idiosyncratic risk is the risk that affects a particular firm or industry alone and can be reduced by diversification. For the founder of an automobile company, examples are: the company's new model fails in the market, a strike in its own plant stops production, a key supplier of the company defaults, or a competitor launches a better car that takes away its customers. Since the founder's wealth is concentrated in his own company, he is fully exposed to these risks. Systematic risk is the risk that affects the whole economy or market and cannot be eliminated by diversification. Examples are: a recession that reduces the demand for cars across the whole industry, a general rise in interest rates that makes car financing expensive, an increase in fuel prices, or a rise in inflation and taxes. Both types of risk matter for the founder, but only systematic risk commands a risk premium in the market, because idiosyncratic risk can be diversified away.

  30. Differentiate between risk neutral, risk averse and risk lover investors. (Marks: 3)
    Topic: Stocks, Stock Valuation & Risk

    A risk averse investor prefers a certain return to a gamble with the same expected return; he dislikes risk and will accept a risky investment only if it offers a higher expected return as compensation (a risk premium). Most investors are risk averse. A risk neutral investor cares only about the expected return and ignores risk altogether; he is indifferent between a certain return and a gamble with the same expected return, and requires no risk premium. A risk lover (risk seeking) investor enjoys risk and prefers a gamble to a certain return with the same expected value; he may even accept a lower expected return for the thrill of the gamble.

  31. Briefly discuss the different types of investment grades of long-term ratings by PACRA. (Marks: 3)
    Topic: Credit Ratings - PACRA

    PACRA (Pakistan Credit Rating Agency) rates companies that offer bonds or stocks to investors, so that investors know which companies are safe to invest in and which to avoid. Its long-term investment grades are: AAA: Highest credit quality. 'AAA' ratings denote the lowest expectation of credit risk. AA: Very high credit quality. 'AA' ratings denote a very low expectation of credit risk. A: High credit quality. 'A' ratings denote a low expectation of credit risk. BBB: Good credit quality. 'BBB' ratings indicate that there is currently a low expectation of credit risk.

  32. Briefly discuss the different types of speculative grades of long-term ratings by PACRA. (Marks: 3)
    Topic: Credit Ratings - PACRA

    The speculative (non-investment) grades of PACRA's long-term ratings are: BB: Speculative. 'BB' ratings indicate that there is a possibility of credit risk developing. B: Highly speculative. 'B' ratings indicate that significant credit risk is present, but a limited margin of safety remains. CCC, CC, C: High default risk. Default is a real possibility. D: The issuer is in default or has a very high risk of default.

  33. Briefly discuss the short-term ratings by PACRA (Pakistan Credit Rating Agency). (Marks: 5)
    Topic: Credit Ratings - PACRA

    PACRA's short-term ratings are: A1+: Highest capacity for timely repayment. A1: Strong capacity for timely repayment. A2: Satisfactory capacity for timely repayment; may be susceptible to adverse economic conditions. A3: An adequate capacity for timely repayment; more susceptible to adverse economic conditions. B: Timely repayment is susceptible to adverse changes in business, economic or financial conditions. C: An inadequate capacity to ensure timely repayment. D: High risk of default, or currently in default.
