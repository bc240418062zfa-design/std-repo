  1. What is consideration? State the essentials of a valid contract. (Marks: 3)
    Topic: Core Subjective Topics

    Consideration is a promise given by both parties as a price for entering into a contract. It can be anything of value which both parties to a legal contract must agree to exchange. Consideration is one of the vitals of a valid contract; without consideration, a contract is not binding. It is all about give and take — it is doing something, or not doing something, in return for something. Whatever you give to the other party in a contract is your promise, and whatever you get in return from the other party is the consideration for you. Essentials of a valid contract: (1) Offer and acceptance; (2) Intention to create a legal relationship; (3) Lawful consideration; (4) Competent parties; (5) Free consent of the parties; (6) Lawful object; (7) Terms of the agreement to be complete and certain; (8) Possibility of performance; (9) Contract to be in writing and registered wherever required; (10) Contract not declared void under the Act.

  2. What role does 'consideration' play in a promise? Elaborate with the help of an example. (Marks: 5)
    Topic: Core Subjective Topics

    Consideration must be provided by each party to a contract; it is a thing, action or promise that has value for the person receiving it. Every promise is supported by consideration: when a person makes a promise, he receives something in return for it, and that return is the consideration which makes the promise legally enforceable. A promise without consideration is a bare promise (nudum pactum) and cannot be enforced in a court of law. Example: A agrees to sell his car to B for Rs. 800,000. A's promise to deliver the car is supported by B's promise to pay Rs. 800,000, and B's promise to pay is supported by A's promise to deliver the car — each promise is the consideration for the other.

  3. What is meant by a 'single promise'? Who can demand performance in a single promise? (Marks: 3)
    Topic: Core Subjective Topics

    A single promise is a promise made by one party only, where the other party has already performed his part or where only one side remains bound. Performance of a single promise can be demanded by the promisee — the person to whom the promise is made. For example, a promise by one person to marry another is not binding unless and until the other also promises to marry the first person; mutual promises to marry may, however, be implied from the conduct of the parties.

  4. Differentiate between an executed contract and an executory contract, with the help of examples. (Marks: 3)
    Topic: Core Subjective Topics

    Executed contract: Such contracts are those where the contracting parties have completely performed their respective obligations under the contract. Example: Mr. Ali entered into an agreement with Mr. Aslam to sell his car for Rs. 800,000. Mr. Ali delivers the car to Mr. Aslam and he pays the promised amount of Rs. 800,000 to Mr. Ali. Such a contract is called an executed contract since both parties have performed their part of the promises. Executory contract: In such contracts both parties are yet to perform their obligations under the contract. Example: Mr. Ali agrees to sell his car to Mr. Aslam for Rs. 800,000, but Mr. Ali has not yet delivered the car and Mr. Aslam has not yet paid the promised price. Such a contract is an executory contract since both parties are yet to perform their part of the promises.

  5. What is free consent? Explain with an example. (Marks: 3)
    Topic: Core Subjective Topics

    According to Section 14 of the Contract Act, 1872, consent is said to be free when it is not caused by coercion, undue influence, fraud, misrepresentation or mistake. Consent given under any of these elements is not free, and the contract is generally voidable at the option of the party whose consent was so caused. Example: A agrees to sell his house to B because B threatens to harm A's family if he refuses. A's consent is caused by coercion, so it is not free consent and the contract is voidable at A's option.

  6. Under which conditions does a contract become voidable? (Marks: 5)
    Topic: Core Subjective Topics

    A contract becomes voidable mainly in the following conditions: (1) When the consent of a party is caused by coercion, undue influence, fraud or misrepresentation (Sections 19 and 19-A), the contract is voidable at the option of the party whose consent was so caused. (2) When a party to a contract promises to perform within a specified time and fails to do so where time is of the essence, the contract becomes voidable at the option of the promisee (Section 55). (3) When a promisor refuses to perform, or disables himself from performing, his promise in its entirety, the promisee may treat the contract as voidable and put an end to it (Section 39). A voidable contract remains valid and enforceable until the aggrieved party chooses to avoid it.

  7. What is soundness of mind under Section 12 of the Contract Act, 1872? (Marks: 5)
    Topic: Core Subjective Topics

    Section 12 provides that a person is said to be of sound mind for the purpose of making a contract if, at the time when he makes it, he is capable of understanding it and of forming a rational judgment as to its effect upon his interests. A person who is usually of unsound mind, but occasionally of sound mind, may make a contract when he is of sound mind. A person who is usually of sound mind, but occasionally of unsound mind, may not make a contract when he is of unsound mind. For example, a patient in a lunatic asylum who is at intervals of sound mind may contract during those intervals, while a sane man who is so delirious from fever that he cannot understand the terms of a contract cannot contract while the delirium lasts.

  8. Can a minor plead minority if he misrepresented his age? (Marks: 3)
    Topic: Core Subjective Topics

    Yes. A minor can always plead minority. The rule of estoppel does not apply to a minor: a minor may fraudulently represent to a person that he is of majority and competent to enter into a contract, and on the faith of such misrepresentation a person may enter into an agreement with the minor. Afterwards, such other person cannot claim that the minor shall be bound by his false representation, since the rule of estoppel does not apply to a minor. Thus, the other person cannot enforce the agreement against the minor on the basis of the false representation made by the minor, because a minor's agreement is void from the very beginning.

  9. Define a wagering contract under Section 30 of the Contract Act, 1872. (Marks: 5)
    Topic: Core Subjective Topics

    Wager means a bet, so a wager is simply a betting agreement. A wagering contract is a contract which is based on the mutual promises of the parties on the condition of the happening or non-happening of an unknown event: one party promises to pay money to the other on the happening of an uncertain event, and the other promises to pay if the event does not happen. Example: Ali makes a contract with Bilal that if it rains today, Bilal will pay Rs. 100 to Ali, but if it does not rain today, Ali will pay Rs. 100 to Bilal. The money won or lost as a result of the happening or non-happening of the unknown event is the wager. Under Section 30 of the Contract Act, 1872, agreements by way of wager are void, and no suit can be brought for recovering anything alleged to be won on any wager.

  10. What is a contingent contract? Explain with an example. (Marks: 5)
    Topic: Core Subjective Topics

    A contingent contract is a contract to do or not to do something which depends upon the happening or non-happening of an event collateral to the contract (Section 31). If the event happens, the contingent contract is enforced; if the event does not happen, the contract is not enforced. A contingent contract is a conditional contract, and the condition must be uncertain — something which may or may not happen. Example: Mr. Fahad contracts to pay Rs. 200,000 to Mr. Ahsan if his house is burnt. It is a contingent contract because there is a condition to pay Rs. 200,000 only if the house is burnt; otherwise the contract will not be enforced. There is uncertainty about the event of the burning of the house — it may or may not burn — so the contract is a contingent contract.

  11. Differentiate between a contingent contract and a wagering contract. (Marks: 5)
    Topic: Core Subjective Topics

    A contingent contract is a contract to do or not to do something depending upon the happening or non-happening of an uncertain future event collateral to the contract; it is a valid and enforceable contract when the event happens. Example: Mr. Fahad contracts to pay Rs. 200,000 to Mr. Ahsan if the house is burnt — the payment depends on the uncertain event of the house burning. A wagering contract, on the other hand, is simply a betting agreement based on the mutual promises of the parties on the happening or non-happening of an unknown event, in which each party stands to win or lose and neither party has any other interest in the event. Example: Ali agrees with Bilal that if it rains today Bilal will pay Ali Rs. 100, and if it does not rain Ali will pay Bilal Rs. 100. The main differences are: a contingent contract is valid and enforceable, while a wagering agreement is void under Section 30; in a contingent contract the parties have an interest in the event besides the stake, while in a wager the stake is the only interest; and a contingent contract is a genuine business contract (e.g., insurance), while a wager is a mere game of chance.

  12. What is the legal status of agreements that are contingent on impossible events? Cite an example. (Marks: 5)
    Topic: Core Subjective Topics

    A contract contingent on the happening of an impossible event is void, whether the impossibility of the event is known or not known to the parties at the time when the agreement is made (Section 32 read with Section 36 of the Contract Act, 1872). Examples: (a) A agrees to pay B Rs. 1,000 if two straight lines should enclose a space. The agreement is void. (b) A agrees to pay B Rs. 1,000 if B will marry A's daughter C. C was dead at the time of the agreement. The agreement is void.

  13. Can a contingent contract about an uncertain future event be enforced? If yes, when can it be enforced? (Marks: 5)
    Topic: Core Subjective Topics

    Yes. Under Section 32 of the Contract Act, 1872, contingent contracts to do or not to do anything if an uncertain future event happens cannot be enforced by law unless and until that event has happened. If the event becomes impossible, such contracts become void. Similarly, contingent contracts to do or not to do anything if an uncertain future event does not happen can be enforced when the happening of that event becomes impossible, and not before. Illustration: A agrees to pay B a sum of money if a certain ship does not return. The ship is sunk. The contract can be enforced when the ship sinks, because the happening of the event (the ship's return) has become impossible.

  14. What is undue influence? Explain. (Marks: 5)
    Topic: Core Subjective Topics

    Undue influence (Section 16 of the Contract Act, 1872) means the exercise of power and influence by a person who has some control over the other person. It is not just the existence of influence or dominance; to prove undue influence it is required that the influence of the power or dominance vested in a person has been exercised to derive undue advantage from the other party. A contract is said to be induced by undue influence where the relations between the parties are such that one of them is in a position to dominate the will of the other and uses that position to obtain an unfair advantage over the other — for example, a doctor over his patient, a guardian over his ward, or a spiritual adviser over his devotee. A contract caused by undue influence is voidable at the option of the party whose consent was so obtained (Section 19-A).

  15. Differentiate between fraud and misrepresentation. (Marks: 5)
    Topic: Core Subjective Topics

    Fraud means a wilful misrepresentation of a material fact, while misrepresentation means a bona fide representation which is false. Fraud (Section 17) is the making of a false statement with the intention to deceive the other party — the person knows that the statement is false but makes it purposely in order to deceive. Misrepresentation (Section 18) is the making of a false statement without any intention of deception: a person positively states that a fact is true although in reality it is not true, but he himself believes it to be true. The main difference is that fraud is done with the aim of deceiving others, which is not the case in misrepresentation. Therefore, in the case of misrepresentation the aggrieved party can only avoid the contract and cannot sue the other party for damages, whereas fraud entitles the aggrieved party both to avoid the contract and to file a suit against the other party for damages.

  16. Discuss the modes of discharge of a contract. (Marks: 5)
    Topic: Core Subjective Topics

    A contract, being an agreement enforceable by law (Section 2), creates a legal obligation which exists until it is discharged. A contract is discharged in the following situations: (1) By performance — the contract is completely performed by both parties and nothing is left to be done; performance is the principal and most usual mode of discharge. (2) By mutual agreement of the parties — both parties mutually agree to terminate, substitute or alter the contract. (3) By impossibility of performance — the contract is discharged when performance becomes impossible, e.g., an agreement to discover treasure by magic. (4) By operation of law — the contract is terminated by law, e.g., by death, insolvency or merger. (5) By breach of contract — the contract is discharged because one party breaks the contract and does not fulfil his promises.

  17. How does a breach of contract take place? Explain by citing an example. (Marks: 3)
    Topic: Core Subjective Topics

    A breach of contract is a failure, without legal excuse, to perform any promise that forms all or part of the contract. A contract is breached when the contract is not performed: in any contract there are two parties who promise to do something, and if any party does not perform or fulfil his side of the promise, the contract is said to be breached. Breach may be actual (non-performance on the due date), or anticipatory (a party refuses to perform, or disables himself from performing, before the due date). Example: A contracts to deliver 200 bags of sugar to B on 11th November at Rs. 500 per bag. On the due date A refuses to supply the sugar. A has committed a breach of contract, and B has the right to take legal action against A for this breach.

  18. What is breach of contract? Enlist the remedies available for breach of contract. (Marks: 5)
    Topic: Core Subjective Topics

    A breach of contract is a failure, without legal excuse, to perform any promise that forms all or part of the contract. When a contract is breached by one party, the other (aggrieved) party has the legal right to use the following remedies: (1) Suit for damages — a claim for compensation for the loss suffered due to the breach. (2) Suit for specific performance — asking the court to compel the defaulting party to actually perform the contract. (3) Suit for injunction — asking the court to restrain the defaulting party from doing something he promised not to do. (4) Suit upon quantum meruit — claiming payment for the work already done, in proportion to its value. (5) Rescission — treating the contract as ended and being restored to the original position.

  19. What are nominal damages? (Marks: 3)
    Topic: Core Subjective Topics

    Nominal damages refer to a damage award issued by a court when a legal wrong has occurred, but where there was no actual financial loss as a result of that legal wrong. Such damages are awarded merely to establish the right of the plaintiff, i.e., to show that his legal right was violated although he suffered no real loss. Typically, when a nominal damage award is used, the plaintiff will be awarded a very small token amount, such as one or two rupees.

  20. What are liquidated damages? Explain with an illustration. (Marks: 3)
    Topic: Core Subjective Topics

    Liquidated damages are damages whose amount the parties themselves fix at the time of the formation of the contract, to be paid by the party in breach to the injured party as compensation upon a specific breach. If the parties to a contract have mentioned the amount of damages for the breach at the time of entering into the contract, such damages shall be recoverable and these will be called liquidated damages. Illustration: A contracts with B to pay B Rs. 1,000 if he fails to pay B Rs. 500 on a given day. A fails to pay B Rs. 500 on that day. B is entitled to recover from A such compensation, not exceeding Rs. 1,000, as the court considers reasonable (Section 74).

  21. Discuss any three situations in which specific performance is NOT granted. (Marks: 3)
    Topic: Core Subjective Topics

    Under the Specific Relief Act, 1877, specific performance of a contract is not granted in the following situations: (1) Where compensation in money is an adequate relief for the non-performance of the contract. (2) Where the contract involves the performance of a continuous duty extending over a longer period than the court can supervise, or otherwise cannot be specifically enforced because the court cannot supervise its performance. (3) Where the contract is of a personal nature, e.g., a contract to sing, to paint a picture or to marry, which depends on the personal qualifications or volition of the parties. It is also refused where the contract is determinable in its nature, or where its terms are too uncertain to be enforced.

  22. In which instances can a suit for specific performance be filed before the court? (Marks: 5)
    Topic: Core Subjective Topics

    A suit for specific performance can be filed, under Section 12 of the Specific Relief Act, 1877, in the following instances: (1) When there exists no standard for ascertaining the actual damage caused by the non-performance of the contract. (2) When the act agreed to be done is such that compensation in money for its non-performance would not afford adequate relief — for example, a contract for the sale of a particular piece of land or a rare article, where money cannot buy an identical substitute. (3) Where the subject matter of the contract is unique or has a special value for the plaintiff. (4) Where it is probable that compensation in money cannot be got, for example because the defendant is insolvent. In such cases the court, in its discretion, may direct the defendant to perform the contract specifically instead of merely paying damages.

  23. A chartered accountant is employed in a company. Can he be banned from private practice during the service? Why or why not? (Marks: 3)
    Topic: Core Subjective Topics

    Yes, he can be banned from private practice during the period of his service. Such a condition is a negative covenant in a contract of service: it does not restrain the accountant from exercising his profession altogether, but only prevents him from serving a rival or practising privately while he remains in the company's employment. An agreement in restraint of trade is void under Section 27 of the Contract Act, 1872, but a negative stipulation in a service agreement, operating only during the term of employment and designed to protect the employer's interests, is valid and can be enforced by an injunction. For example, an employee contracted to serve as a weaving master for three years and agreed not to serve anyone else during the period; when he left after one year and joined another mill, the court held the agreement reasonable and issued an injunction against the employee. The ban, however, cannot be extended beyond the period of service.

  24. What is the status of an agreement in restraint of marriage? Explain with an example. (Marks: 5)
    Topic: Core Subjective Topics

    According to Section 26 of the Contract Act, 1872, every agreement in restraint of the marriage of any person, other than a minor, is void. The law favours marriage and does not allow a person to be restrained, wholly or partly, from marrying. Example: A agrees with B to pay B Rs. 50,000 if B does not marry, or if B does not marry a particular person. The agreement is void because it restrains B's marriage, and B cannot be forced to pay or to forfeit anything under it. The only exception is a minor: an agreement in restraint of the marriage of a minor is not void under this section.

  25. If a promisor dies before performance, who will be obliged to fulfil his liabilities? (Marks: 3)
    Topic: Core Subjective Topics

    According to Section 37 of the Contract Act, 1872, promises bind the representatives of the promisor in case of the death of such promisor before performance, unless a contrary intention appears from the contract. Illustration: A promises to deliver goods to B on a certain day on payment of Rs. 1,000. A dies before that day. A's representatives are bound to deliver the goods to B, and B is bound to pay the Rs. 1,000 to A's representatives. However, where the contract involves personal skill or service (for example, a promise to paint a picture), it is discharged by the death of the promisor and his representatives are not bound to perform it.

  26. Define a contract of indemnity. Give an example. (Marks: 3)
    Topic: Core Subjective Topics

    A contract of indemnity is a contract by which one party promises to save the other from loss caused to him by the conduct of the promisor himself, or by the conduct of any other person (Section 124 of the Contract Act, 1872). The person who promises to compensate the loss is called the indemnifier, and the person whose loss is to be made good is called the indemnity-holder. Example: Mr. Bashir signs a contract with an insurance company for his car by paying a premium. The insurance company promises to save Mr. Bashir from any loss caused, such as theft of the car, damage to the car or an accident; in case of such loss, the company will compensate the loss to Mr. Bashir. An insurance contract (other than life insurance) is the common example of a contract of indemnity.

  27. What is a contract of guarantee? Who is involved in this contract, and what are their roles? (Marks: 5)
    Topic: Core Subjective Topics

    A contract of guarantee is a contract to perform the promise, or discharge the liability, of a third person in case of his default (Section 126 of the Contract Act, 1872). Three parties are involved in it: (1) The surety — the person who gives the guarantee, i.e., who promises to perform or pay if the principal debtor defaults. (2) The principal debtor — the person in respect of whose default the guarantee is given; he is primarily liable to perform the promise or pay the debt. (3) The creditor — the person to whom the guarantee is given and to whom the principal debtor owes the obligation. A contract of guarantee thus involves three contracts: between the creditor and the principal debtor (the primary contract), between the creditor and the surety, and an implied contract between the surety and the principal debtor under which the debtor must indemnify the surety if the surety has to pay.

  28. What is a specific (ordinary) guarantee? How does it differ from a continuing guarantee? Explain with examples. (Marks: 5)
    Topic: Core Subjective Topics

    A specific guarantee, also called an ordinary guarantee, is a guarantee which is restricted to a single specific transaction or engagement, and comes to an end when that transaction is completed. For example, a guarantee given for availing a loan from a bank: the surety guarantees the repayment of that one loan only. A continuing guarantee, on the other hand, extends to a series of transactions, and remains in force until it is revoked by the surety. For example, a guarantee furnished to a supplier for making supplies to a particular person or business during a specified period, say one year: the surety remains liable for all supplies made during that period, up to the amount guaranteed. The main difference is that a specific guarantee covers only one transaction, while a continuing guarantee covers a series of transactions over a period of time.

  29. Define 'bailment' under Section 148 of the Contract Act, 1872. (Marks: 3)
    Topic: Core Subjective Topics

    A 'bailment' is the delivery of goods by one person to another for some purpose, upon a contract that they shall, when the purpose is accomplished, be returned or otherwise disposed of according to the directions of the person delivering them. The person delivering the goods is called the 'bailor', and the person to whom they are delivered is called the 'bailee'. For example, delivering a piece of cloth to a tailor to make a suit, or delivering ornaments to a neighbour for safe custody, is a bailment.

  30. State the duties of a bailee. (Marks: 3)
    Topic: Core Subjective Topics

    The duties of a bailee under the Contract Act, 1872 are: (1) To take as much care of the goods bailed to him as a man of ordinary prudence would, under similar circumstances, take of his own goods of the same bulk, quality and value (Section 151). (2) Not to make any use of the goods bailed which is not according to the conditions of the bailment (Section 154). (3) Not to mix the goods bailed with his own goods without the consent of the bailor; if the goods can be separated, he must bear the expense of separation and any damage arising from the mixture, and if they cannot be separated, he must compensate the bailor for the loss of the goods (Sections 155–157). (4) To return, or deliver according to the bailor's directions, the goods bailed, without demand, as soon as the time has expired or the purpose has been accomplished for which they were bailed (Section 160). (5) To return to the bailor any increase or profit which may have accrued from the goods bailed (Section 163).

  31. What is a pledge? State its characteristics. (Marks: 5)
    Topic: Core Subjective Topics

    A pledge is the bailment of goods as security for the payment of a debt or the performance of a promise (Section 172 of the Contract Act, 1872). The bailor in this case is called the 'pawnor' and the bailee is called the 'pawnee'. Its characteristics are: (1) It relates to movable property only. (2) There must be a transfer (delivery) of possession of the goods from the pawnor to the pawnee. (3) The ownership of the goods remains with the pawnor; only possession passes to the pawnee. (4) A case of mere custody is not a pledge — the delivery must be made by way of security for a debt or promise. (5) The pawnee has only a limited (special) interest in the goods pledged: he may retain them until the debt is paid, and on default he may sell the goods after giving the pawnor reasonable notice of the sale.

  32. Discuss and explain the statement: 'Agency refers to the relationship which exists between two persons, the Principal and the Agent.' (Marks: 5)
    Topic: Core Subjective Topics

    In general terms, agency refers to the relationship which exists between two persons, the Principal and the Agent, in which the agent has to perform different duties as per the instructions of the principal and also enters into contracts with third parties on behalf of the principal. The relationship of agency plays an important role in business and commercial dealings: through an agent, a person can act and bind himself in places and transactions where he cannot be personally present. This relationship is legal, created by virtue of an agreement between the principal and the agent, and the general rule is that whatever a person can do himself, he can do through an agent. The acts of the agent done within his authority are treated in law as the acts of the principal himself, so the principal becomes bound by, and entitled to the benefit of, the contracts made by his agent with third parties.

  33. How is an express agency different from an implied agency? Explain briefly. (Marks: 3)
    Topic: Core Subjective Topics

    An express agency is an actual agency created by a written or oral agreement between the principal and the agent, in which the principal expressly appoints the agent and defines his authority in words, spoken or written. An implied agency, on the other hand, is inferred from the acts, conduct, situation or relationship of the parties: it involves permission to act even though that permission is not explicitly established orally or in writing. For example, where a person regularly allows another to order goods in his name and pays for them, an implied agency is created by conduct.

  34. Can a minor be an agent? (Marks: 3)
    Topic: Core Subjective Topics

    Yes. According to Section 184 of the Contract Act, 1872, any person may become an agent — no qualification, not even majority or soundness of mind, is prescribed for an agent, because the agent does not incur personal liability: he merely creates a legal relationship between the principal and third parties. A minor can therefore be appointed as an agent, and the principal will be bound by the minor agent's acts towards third parties. However, as between the principal and the agent, a minor agent cannot be made responsible to the principal for his acts, because the minor is himself incompetent to contract.

  35. Discuss the rights and obligations of the different parties in a contract of agency created by ratification. (Marks: 5)
    Topic: Core Subjective Topics

    Ratification takes place when a person adopts an act done on his behalf by another person who had no authority to do it (Sections 196–200). Its effects on the parties are: (1) The principal may sue the third party, and the third party can also sue the principal, exactly as if the act had been done with the principal's prior authority. (2) No liability is incurred by the agent towards the third party once the act is ratified, because the agent is treated as having been authorised from the beginning. (3) The agent is not liable for exceeding his authority once the principal ratifies the act. (4) The principal is required under law to pay reasonable remuneration to the agent for the act ratified and performed. Ratification must, however, be of the whole act, must be made with full knowledge of the facts, and cannot be made if it would injure the rights of a third party.

  36. State the duties of a principal under a contract of agency. (Marks: 3)
    Topic: Core Subjective Topics

    The duties of a principal towards his agent include: (1) Payment of remuneration to the agent, as agreed, for the services rendered by him. (2) Not to prevent his agent from performing the duties under the contract, and to do everything necessary on his own part to enable the agent to perform. (3) To indemnify the agent for any legitimate expenses which have been incurred by the agent in the course of the performance of his duties, and for the consequences of all lawful acts done by the agent in the exercise of his authority (Sections 222–224).

  37. If an agent is misrepresented or commits fraud against a third party, what is the position of the principal? (Marks: 5)
    Topic: Core Subjective Topics

    According to Section 238 of the Contract Act, 1872, misrepresentation made, or fraud committed, by an agent acting in the course of the business of his principal, has the same effect on agreements made by such agent as if the misrepresentation or fraud had been made or committed by the principal himself. The principal is therefore bound by the agent's fraud or misrepresentation towards the third party, and the third party can avoid the agreement and claim his remedies against the principal, provided the agent acted within the scope of his authority. But misrepresentations made or frauds committed by an agent in matters which do not fall within his authority do not affect his principal.

  38. 'A stranger to consideration can sue, but a stranger to a contract cannot sue.' Discuss. (Marks: 5)
    Topic: Core Subjective Topics

    Under Section 2(d) of the Contract Act, 1872, consideration may move from the promisee or from any other person; therefore a person who is a stranger to the consideration — i.e., who has himself given no consideration — can still sue on the contract if he is a party to it, because the consideration was furnished by someone else on his behalf. But a stranger to the contract, i.e., a person who is not a party to the contract at all, cannot sue upon it: this is the doctrine of privity of contract, under which only the parties to a contract can enforce it. Recognised exceptions exist, for example a beneficiary under a trust or a family arrangement, and an assignee of the contract, who may sue although not an original party.

  39. Differentiate between analytical jurisprudence and ethical jurisprudence. (Marks: 3)
    Topic: Core Subjective Topics

    Analytical jurisprudence analyses the law as it exists at present: it is an analysis of the principles of law as they actually are, including an examination of a complex legal idea in its elementary sub-divisions, the relations between civil law and other forms of law, and a study of the legal sources of law. Ethical jurisprudence, on the other hand, deals with the law as it ought to be in an ideal state: it studies what the law should be in order to attain justice, and strives to bring the principles of law to such a form that they serve the ends of justice best. In short, analytical jurisprudence studies the law as it is, while ethical jurisprudence studies the law as it ought to be.

  40. What is imperative law? Explain with an example. (Marks: 3)
    Topic: Core Subjective Topics

    Imperative law means a rule of action imposed upon men by some authority which enforces obedience to it. In other words, it is a command enforced by some superior power, either physically or in any other form of compulsion: if a person disobeys it, he is punished or compelled by the sanction of the State. Example: the provisions of the Pakistan Penal Code, which command every person not to commit theft or murder and punish disobedience with imprisonment, are imperative law.

  41. Define an unenforceable contract, with an example. (Marks: 3)
    Topic: Core Subjective Topics

    An unenforceable contract is a contract which is valid in substance but cannot be enforced in a court of law because of some technical defect or legal bar, such as the expiry of the period of limitation or the absence of a required stamp or registration. Example: a time-barred debt — the debt remains a valid obligation and if the debtor pays it voluntarily he cannot recover it back, but the creditor cannot sue to enforce it because the limitation period has expired. Similarly, an agreement which is required by law to be stamped but is unstamped is unenforceable until the defect is cured.

  42. Briefly elaborate the classification of goods under the Sale of Goods Act, 1930. (Marks: 3)
    Topic: Core Subjective Topics

    Under the Sale of Goods Act, 1930, goods are classified as follows: (1) Existing goods — goods which are in existence and owned or possessed by the seller at the time of the contract of sale. Existing goods are further divided into specific goods (identified and agreed upon at the time of the contract), ascertained goods (identified after the contract in accordance with it) and unascertained goods (not yet identified). (2) Future goods — goods to be manufactured, produced or acquired by the seller after the making of the contract of sale. (3) Contingent goods — goods whose acquisition by the seller depends upon a contingency which may or may not happen. Goods are said to be in a 'deliverable state' when they are in such a state that the buyer would under the contract be bound to take delivery of them.

  43. What is a bill of exchange? Give its specimen. (Marks: 5)
    Topic: Core Subjective Topics

    A bill of exchange is an instrument in writing containing an unconditional order, signed by the maker, directing a certain person to pay a certain sum of money only to, or to the order of, a certain person, or to the bearer of the instrument (Section 5 of the Negotiable Instruments Act, 1881). The person who makes the bill is the drawer, the person directed to pay is the drawee, and the person to whom payment is to be made is the payee. Specimen: Rs. 50,000/- Lahore, 1st January 2026 Three months after date, pay to Mr. Ahmed Raza or order the sum of Rupees Fifty Thousand only, for value received. To: Mr. Bilal Khan, Karachi. (Signed) Muhammad Usman (Drawer)

  44. State the essentials of a valid endorsement. (Marks: 3)
    Topic: Core Subjective Topics

    An endorsement (indorsement) is made when the maker or holder of a negotiable instrument signs it, otherwise than as maker, for the purpose of negotiation (Section 15 of the Negotiable Instruments Act, 1881). Its essentials are: (1) It must be written on the instrument itself, usually on its back or face, or on a slip of paper affixed to it (allonge). (2) It must be signed by the endorser; a mere signature without words is a valid endorsement in blank. (3) It must be made for the purpose of negotiation, i.e., transferring the instrument to another person. (4) It is completed by the delivery of the instrument by the endorser to the endorsee. (5) It must be of the entire instrument — a partial endorsement, transferring only part of the amount, is invalid.

  45. How is the presiding officer of a Labour Court selected under the Industrial Relations Ordinance? (Marks: 3)
    Topic: Core Subjective Topics

    Labour Courts are established by the Provincial Government under the Industrial Relations Ordinance. The presiding officer of a Labour Court is appointed by the Provincial Government, and a person is qualified for such appointment if he is, or has been, a District and Sessions Judge or an Additional District and Sessions Judge, or has held a judicial office of equivalent standing. The appointment is made by notification in the official Gazette, and the presiding officer so appointed adjudicates the industrial disputes referred to the Labour Court.
