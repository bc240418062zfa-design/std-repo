  1. What is reflective practice? Discuss its importance for teachers.
    Topic: Core Subjective Topics

    Reflective practice is the process by which teachers carefully evaluate their own teaching — examining what they did, why they did it, how learners responded and how the experience can be improved. Schon (1983), who derived the term in his book 'The Reflective Practitioner', described it as thinking about one's actions while acting and after acting. It is important because it helps teachers to take control of their own professional development, improves the quality of teaching and learning, develops professional understanding, helps teachers understand their learners and their needs, and produces reflective learners. Teachers who reflect on their teaching can achieve the best outcomes for their students.

  2. Differentiate between reflection-in-action and reflection-on-action.
    Topic: Core Subjective Topics

    Reflection-in-action takes place during the teaching activity itself. The teacher thinks on his/her feet, monitors learners' responses while teaching and modifies the teaching strategy immediately, for example by re-explaining a point in a different way when students look confused. It involves quick thinking and draws on the teacher's experience and professional knowledge. Reflection-on-action takes place after the activity is over, outside the classroom. It is a reconstructive mental review in which the teacher looks back at the lesson, analyses what happened, evaluates strengths and weaknesses and plans improvements for future lessons. It has a dialogic nature because the teacher holds a conversation with the experience.

  3. What are the three levels of reflection? Explain each briefly.
    Topic: Core Subjective Topics

    1. Technical reflection: It is the most basic level. It focuses on the behaviours, skills and technical aspects of teaching — the effective application of teaching skills and strategies to achieve given goals. 2. Practical reflection: At this level the teacher investigates questions and clarifies the assumptions behind teaching activities. It considers the learning experience of the students and the consequences of teaching actions. 3. Critical reflection: It is the highest level. The teacher reflects upon the larger context of education, particularly ethical, moral, historic, political and cultural issues, and questions the assumptions and values that frame practical problems.

  4. Explain Dewey's paradigm of reflection and the three attitudes necessary for reflective thinking.
    Topic: Core Subjective Topics

    Dewey is regarded as the originator of the concept of reflection. In his paradigm, reflective thinking begins with a state of doubt, hesitation or perplexity — a felt difficulty or problem — and proceeds through defining the problem, forming hypotheses, reasoning about them and testing them in action, until the difficulty is resolved. Dewey held that an attitude of reflection in learning and teaching depends on three attitudes: 1. Open-mindedness: an active desire to listen to more sides than one, to admit the possibility of error and to be receptive to new ideas. 2. Whole-heartedness: single-mindedness and focused intention; the teacher is genuinely absorbed in and committed to the inquiry. 3. Responsibility: carefully considering the consequences of a projected step and being willing to adopt those consequences when they follow reasonably from the position taken.

  5. Describe Gibbs' Reflective Cycle (1988) and its stages.
    Topic: Core Subjective Topics

    Gibbs' Reflective Cycle (1988) is a structured model that encourages teachers to think about a given situation systematically and establish an action plan for dealing with it in future. It has six stages: 1. Description: What happened? A factual account of the event. 2. Feelings: What were you thinking and feeling during the experience? 3. Evaluation: What was good and bad about the experience? The teacher makes a judgement about the teaching. 4. Analysis: What sense can you make of the situation? Breaking the experience into parts and examining them. 5. Conclusion: What else could you have done? 6. Action plan: If the situation arose again, what would you do?

  6. Explain Kolb's experiential learning cycle.
    Topic: Core Subjective Topics

    Kolb's reflective model, known as the experiential learning cycle, emphasises the role that experience plays in learning and illustrates the developmental nature of reflection. Learning moves through four stages: 1. Concrete experience: the learner has an actual experience. 2. Reflective observation: the learner reviews and reflects on the experience. 3. Abstract conceptualisation: the learner draws conclusions and forms concepts or theories from the reflection. 4. Active experimentation: the learner plans and tries out what has been learned, which produces a new concrete experience and the cycle begins again.

  7. What is Boud's triangular model of reflection? Explain its elements.
    Topic: Core Subjective Topics

    Boud formulated his triangular model of reflective learning in 1985. It represents reflection as a process built around experience with three elements: 1. Experience: the behaviour, ideas and feelings of the learner during the event. 2. The reflective processes: returning to the experience, attending to feelings (using positive feelings and removing obstructive ones) and re-evaluating the experience. 3. Outcomes: new perspectives, changed behaviour and readiness for application. The three elements are connected in a cycle: experience leads to reflective processes, which produce outcomes, and the outcomes reshape future experience.

  8. Describe Johns' Model for Structured Reflection (2000) and explain the '10 Cs' of Johns' model.
    Topic: Core Subjective Topics

    Johns' Model for Structured Reflection (2000) guides reflection through structured questions organised around phases such as 'look in' (finding a space and paying attention to one's thoughts and feelings), 'look out' (describing and exploring the experience with the help of cues), and considering the influences on the situation and what has been learned. Johns' model of reflection is also summarised through the '10 Cs': 1. Commitment: willingness to invest time and effort. 2. Contradiction: seeing the gap between actual and desired practice. 3. Conflict: using the tension constructively. 4. Challenge: confronting one's assumptions. 5. Catharsis: releasing emotions. 6. Creativity: finding new ways of seeing and doing. 7. Connection: linking new insight with existing knowledge. 8. Caring: valuing oneself and others. 9. Congruence: honesty between thought, feeling and action. 10. Constructing: building personal knowledge through reflection.

  9. What is the SECI model of knowledge creation? Explain its four processes.
    Topic: Core Subjective Topics

    The SECI model (Nonaka and Takeuchi) describes how knowledge is created and converted in organisations through four processes: 1. Socialisation: tacit knowledge is shared through direct experience, observation and working together. 2. Externalisation: tacit knowledge is converted into explicit knowledge by expressing it in words, concepts, models and documents. 3. Combination: explicit knowledge from different sources is combined, sorted and systematised into new explicit knowledge. 4. Internalisation: explicit knowledge is converted back into tacit knowledge as individuals learn by doing and make the knowledge part of their own practice. The four processes work as a continuous spiral of knowledge creation.

  10. Define critical thinking. What are the core critical thinking skills?
    Topic: Core Subjective Topics

    Critical thinking is the higher-order cognitive process of actively analysing, evaluating and synthesising information rather than accepting it at face value. It is a questioning process: the critical thinker questions assumptions, examines evidence and reasons logically before reaching conclusions. Thinking is based on biological, psychological, cognitive and communication processes, and emotions can confuse critical thinking by pushing thought in particular directions. The core critical thinking skills are: 1. Interpretation: understanding and expressing the meaning of experiences, data and procedures. 2. Analysis: identifying the relationships among statements, concepts and descriptions. 3. Evaluation: assessing the credibility of claims and the strength of arguments. 4. Inference: drawing reasonable conclusions from information and evidence. 5. Explanation: stating and justifying one's reasoning. 6. Self-regulation: monitoring and correcting one's own thinking.

  11. What attributes should a teacher possess to become a critical thinker?
    Topic: Core Subjective Topics

    A teacher who is a critical thinker shows the following attributes: 1. Open-mindedness and willingness to consider other points of view. 2. Honesty about his/her own emotions, biases and prejudices. 3. Self-awareness, especially relating to personal bias. 4. An evaluative disposition: judging claims on the basis of evidence. 5. Willingness to question assumptions — his/her own and those of others. 6. Fair-mindedness in evaluating the reasoning of others. 7. Persistence in seeking relevant information and precision. 8. Confidence in his/her own reasoning abilities. 9. Readiness to revise judgements when new evidence appears.

  12. What is critical reflection? How does it facilitate transformational learning?
    Topic: Core Subjective Topics

    Critical reflection is the deepest form of reflection in which the practitioner examines the assumptions, values and beliefs that underlie his/her practice, together with the social, cultural and political context of that practice. According to Habermas, it requires a level of detachment and objectivity, and it is very much a cognitive, internal process. Critical reflection facilitates transformational learning (Mezirow) because it enables the practitioner to surface and challenge taken-for-granted frames of reference. When the original starting point creates a dilemma that needs to be addressed, the practitioner re-examines his/her assumptions, develops a new perspective and transforms practice accordingly — learning only takes place where there is a process of transformation.

  13. What are 'frames'? Explain rhetorical frames and action frames.
    Topic: Core Subjective Topics

    Frames are structures of beliefs, perceptions, values and appreciations through which a person organises experience and decides how to act. They are constructed from our beliefs, values and assumptions, and they are influenced by the metaphors we use; concepts determine the frames for perception. Two types of frames are distinguished: 1. Rhetorical frames: these are the espoused beliefs we use in argument. They are used as a weapon to attack other frames and to convince people that our frame is a good one. 2. Action frames: these relate to policies in use — the beliefs actually embodied in our behaviour — and they are used for dealing with the situation at hand.

  14. What is reframing? How does reframing help a reflective practitioner?
    Topic: Core Subjective Topics

    Reframing means looking at a situation, problem or belief from a different frame — changing the perspective through which it is viewed so that new meanings and new solutions become visible. Reframing of beliefs is a four-step process: identifying the core belief, identifying the supporting beliefs, identifying the opposites of the supporting beliefs, and arriving at a reframed core belief. The reframing matrix (the 4Ps: product, planning, potential and people perspectives) is a tool for this, because different people with different experience approach problems in different ways. Reframing helps the reflective practitioner to see things from other perspectives, to see alternative solutions and actions, to invent new strategies instead of focusing only on his/her own perspective, and to resolve conflicts between competing frames.

  15. Discuss Brookfield's four lenses of reflective practice.
    Topic: Core Subjective Topics

    Brookfield suggests that teachers view their practice through four complementary lenses: 1. The lens of their own autobiographies as learners: our past experiences as learners shape our assumptions about teaching. 2. The lens of students' eyes: seeing our teaching as our students experience it. 3. The lens of colleagues' perceptions: the views and feedback of fellow teachers. 4. The lens of educational literature (theory): what theory and research say about the issue. Reviewing practice through these lenses helps us to admit and surface the assumptions we have about teaching, the methods we use and the techniques we feel appropriate, so that we can ensure the best for student learning.

  16. What is the Johari Window? Explain its four quadrants and how the open area can be expanded.
    Topic: Core Subjective Topics

    The Johari Window is a communication model with four quadrants, used to improve understanding for individuals and between individuals in a team: 1. Open area: things known to the self and known to others. 2. Blind area: things not known to the self but known to others. 3. Hidden area: things known to the self but not known to others. 4. Unknown area: things unknown to the self and unknown to others. The open area expands horizontally through self-disclosure (sharing hidden information with others) and through feedback from others in the team, which reduces the blind area. As the open area grows, communication, trust and teamwork improve.

  17. Explain the conscious competence model with its four stages.
    Topic: Core Subjective Topics

    The conscious competence model describes the stages a learner passes through while acquiring a skill, in four quadrants: 1. Unconscious incompetence: the learner does not know what he/she does not know; the skill gap is not recognised. 2. Conscious incompetence: the learner recognises the gap in his/her knowledge and becomes aware of the relevance of the skill. 3. Conscious competence: the learner can perform the skill, but needs to think each step through carefully and consciously. 4. Unconscious competence: the learner can perform the skill fluently and skillfully without having to consider everything closely; it has become second nature.

  18. What is reflective writing? Describe its stages.
    Topic: Core Subjective Topics

    Reflective writing is a written, personal form of reflection that provides evidence of reflective practice. It focuses on the specific details within an experience and is more personal than other kinds of academic writing; the writer selects only the most significant parts of the event. Structured reflective writing has three stages: 1. Description: an account of what happened and what is being examined. 2. Interpretation: making sense of the experience — comparing and contrasting, connecting ideas, and examining the experience in a time frame (using words like 'previously', 'at the time', 'initially'). Interpretation is perhaps the most complex part of reflective writing. 3. Outcome (output): what has been learned and what the writer will do differently as a result.

  19. What is a Professional Development Journal (PDJ)? How is it different from a personal diary?
    Topic: Core Subjective Topics

    A Professional Development Journal is a written record of the teacher's experiences and feelings about planning, preparing and delivering lessons, maintained over time to support reflection and professional growth. It is a subjective dialogue with oneself — an on-going discourse in which the teacher examines practice, records insights and plans development; its writing may include diagrams and drawings. It is different from a personal diary because a diary merely records daily events and feelings, whereas the PDJ is purposeful and analytical: it focuses on professional practice, connects experience with theory, and leads to conclusions and action for improvement. It is not a diary.

  20. What is action research? Explain its steps and the assumptions underlying it.
    Topic: Core Subjective Topics

    Action research is a form of enquiry in which teachers systematically investigate their own practice in order to improve it. It engages teachers in a cycle of experience, critical reflection and action: identify a problem, plan an action, act, observe the effects, reflect, and plan again. Its main steps are: (1) identifying the research problem or focus; (2) developing a plan of action; (3) acting and obtaining the necessary information to answer the question; and (4) reflecting on the results and planning the next cycle. The assumptions underlying action research are that teachers and other education professionals have authority to make decisions, that they want to improve their practice, that they are committed to continued professional development, and that they will engage in systematic reflection on their work.

  21. Differentiate between single-loop, double-loop and triple-loop learning.
    Topic: Core Subjective Topics

    1. Single-loop learning: errors are detected and corrected within the existing rules, routines and assumptions. The strategies are rigid and the governing values do not change — the problem is fixed but nothing deeper is questioned. 2. Double-loop learning: the practitioner questions the underlying assumptions, values and beliefs themselves, changes the rules, applies new strategies to achieve outcomes and takes a wider perspective. 3. Triple-loop learning: this is learning about learning — learning how to learn, how to make meaning and how to derive understanding from our experiences. It questions the very principles and purposes that guide learning.

  22. Differentiate between deductive and inductive approaches to learning.
    Topic: Core Subjective Topics

    Deductive learning moves from the general to the specific: the learner starts with general principles, concepts or rules and applies them to detailed facts and particular cases. It is rule-based learning. Inductive learning moves in the opposite direction, from the specific to the general: the learner starts with particular observations and experiences and builds general principles and concepts from them. Reflective action planning can also be inductive: the teacher identifies priorities, explores practice in an exploratory, trial-and-error fashion, sets success criteria and identifies sources of evidence.

  23. What is concept-based learning? Discuss its benefits for the reflective practitioner.
    Topic: Core Subjective Topics

    Concept-based learning is learning organised around concepts rather than isolated facts. It means the reflective practitioner works with deeper levels of conceptual understanding: facts are related with concepts, meaning is made from concepts, and learning is transferred easily to new situations. It requires deeper intellectual engagement and promotes emotional engagement and motivation; the teacher encourages questions and investigation, and synergistic thinking links factual and conceptual levels together. Its benefits are that the practitioner makes meaning and processes information more deeply, develops a more thorough working knowledge of theories-in-use, thinks at more advanced levels (conceptual learning acts as a catalyst), and can use three-dimensional instruction — reframing concepts to improve learning across multiple contexts.

  24. Explain Bloom's taxonomy of the cognitive domain.
    Topic: Core Subjective Topics

    Bloom's taxonomy is a structured framework of six levels of the cognitive domain, moving from simple to complex thinking: 1. Knowledge: recall of facts and information (the lowest level). 2. Comprehension: understanding ideas — translation, interpretation and extrapolation. 3. Application: using knowledge and rules in new situations. 4. Analysis: breaking the whole into parts and studying the parts and their relationships. 5. Synthesis: combining parts and ideas into a new whole or a comprehensive account. 6. Evaluation: making value judgements about ideas, materials and methods (the highest level). A taxonomy gives the reflective practitioner a structured framework to move from absolute knowing towards contextual knowing.

  25. What is a critical friend? Explain warm feedback and cold feedback.
    Topic: Core Subjective Topics

    A critical friend is a trusted colleague who supports a teacher's reflection by asking probing questions, offering another perspective and giving honest feedback — both supportive and challenging. 1. Warm feedback: feedback that supports and praises the behaviour or skill observed; it recognises strengths and encourages the practitioner. 2. Cold feedback: feedback that challenges assumptions and asks for evidence to support practice; it raises difficult questions so that the practitioner examines practice more critically. A critical friend uses both kinds of feedback, so reflection is encouraged without becoming merely comfortable or merely critical.

  26. What is peer observation? Discuss its purposes and strengths.
    Topic: Core Subjective Topics

    Peer observation is a process in which colleagues observe each other's teaching and exchange feedback; it involves a two-way flow of information in which colleagues share ideas, strategies and resources. Its purposes are to allow beginning teachers to model effective teaching, to provide opportunities for teachers to share effective practice, and to provide opportunities for teachers to learn about teaching and assessment practices. Its major strength is the sharing of information and experience: teachers learn from each other's strengths, receive feedback in a non-threatening way and develop professionally together. Peer supervision extends this support by helping colleagues plan and improve practice collaboratively.

  27. What is mentoring? Discuss the roles and challenges of a mentor.
    Topic: Core Subjective Topics

    Mentoring is a supportive professional relationship in which an experienced teacher (the mentor) guides, advises and supports a less experienced colleague. Mentors need leadership abilities and coaching skills, and they act as guides, role models, coaches and critical friends. The most challenging role of a mentor is that of a critical friend, because it requires giving honest, sometimes uncomfortable feedback while maintaining trust. Challenges in establishing a mentoring relationship include time constraints, matching the mentor and mentee, maintaining confidentiality and trust, and balancing support with honest challenge.

  28. Discuss the barriers to reflective practice.
    Topic: Core Subjective Topics

    Several factors make the reflective practice process difficult to achieve for teachers: 1. Lack of time and space to think and reflect. 2. Unsupportive organisational culture that does not value reflection. 3. Teachers' own negative attitudes and self-imposed negative ideas — the most significant barrier is lack of knowledge, and a lack of conceptual clarity about what reflection is. 4. Group thinking, which creates stereotyped views and discourages questioning. 5. Lack of resources and of supportive colleagues. 6. The risks involved: constant striving for self-improvement may lead to self-disapproval, and self-reflection is limited by the difference between perception and reality.

  29. What is situated learning? How does it relate to communities of practice?
    Topic: Core Subjective Topics

    Situated learning is learning that takes place in the authentic context in which knowledge will be used. It focuses on teacher learning to make teachers think critically and originally within real professional situations, rather than in isolation from practice. Situated learning is closely related to communities of practice: groups of professionals who share a concern or a passion for what they do and learn how to do it better through regular interaction. New members learn by participating in the community's activities alongside experienced practitioners, gradually moving from peripheral participation to full participation, so knowledge is created and shared socially.

  30. What is Appreciative Inquiry? How can it be used in reflective practice?
    Topic: Core Subjective Topics

    Appreciative Inquiry (AI) is an approach to change and development that focuses on strengths rather than problems. Instead of analysing what is wrong, it appreciates and builds on what is already working well — the positive core of a person, a classroom or an organisation. In reflective practice, a teacher using Appreciative Inquiry identifies his/her best teaching experiences, discovers the factors that made them successful, imagines how practice could be at its best, and designs actions to make that vision happen. It supports reflective practice by keeping reflection constructive, energising and focused on possibilities rather than deficits.

  31. Describe the Socratic method and its use by the reflective practitioner.
    Topic: Core Subjective Topics

    The Socratic method is a method of enquiry based on asking and answering questions. The teacher acts as a 'midwife', helping ideas to be born through dialogue rather than delivering ready-made answers: questions probe assumptions, clarify concepts, demand evidence and reasons, explore implications and consider alternative viewpoints. The reflective practitioner uses the Socratic method of enquiry to improve his/her own teaching and learning: by questioning himself/herself in the same disciplined way — What do I assume? What is my evidence? What else could explain this? — the practitioner examines practice deeply instead of accepting it uncritically.

  32. What is grounded theory? How is it relevant to teacher research?
    Topic: Core Subjective Topics

    Grounded theory is a research approach in which theory is generated (grounded) from data rather than tested against it. The researcher collects qualitative data — observations, interviews, documents — codes it, compares incidents constantly, and builds categories and concepts until a theory emerges that explains the phenomenon studied. It is relevant to teacher research and reflective practice because teachers can use the same approach in action research: gathering evidence from their own classrooms, coding and comparing it, and developing their own grounded understanding of what works, instead of simply applying theories produced by others.

  33. Discuss the importance of professional development for teachers and explain what a professional development plan includes.
    Topic: Core Subjective Topics

    Professional development is essential because teaching knowledge and skills must keep growing: it improves the quality of teaching and student learning, builds professional confidence, keeps teachers updated with new ideas, and is a commitment every truly professional practitioner must have. Positive outcomes depend on opportunities for professional development, a positive organisational climate and the support of higher management. A professional development plan typically includes: identified development needs and priorities; clear goals and targets; the activities to be undertaken (courses, reading, peer observation, mentoring); a time frame; the support and resources required; success criteria; and the evidence by which development will be demonstrated and evaluated.

  34. What is work-based learning? Explain its features.
    Topic: Core Subjective Topics

    Work-based learning is learning that takes place through real work in the workplace rather than in a classroom away from the job. The workplace itself becomes the source of learning: teachers learn from their everyday practice, from problems that arise in it, and from colleagues. Its features include: the focus is on how to learn from work experience; learning is integrated with professional practice and professional growth; it is work-related and often self-directed; it uses reflection on real tasks and collaboration with colleagues; and team learning is its core, because effective teamwork supports learning in the workplace.

  35. Explain lesson study and its benefits for teachers.
    Topic: Core Subjective Topics

    Lesson study is a collaborative professional development process in which a group of teachers jointly plan a lesson, one teacher teaches it while the others observe the learners, and the group then discusses the evidence and refines the lesson. Its benefits include professional growth of teachers, improved teacher knowledge, enhanced student learning, and a culture of collaboration in which teachers share practice and learn from one another. The greatest benefits come when teachers work collaboratively rather than independently.
