  1. A company had the following data extracted from its financial statements for the year ending June 30, 2008: Current Ratio = 2; Acid Ratio = 1.5; Current Liabilities = $500,000; Inventory Turnover = 5; Gross Profit Margin = 20 percent. What were its sales for the year? (Marks: 5)
    Topic: Financial Statements & Ratio Analysis

    Current Assets = Current Ratio x Current Liabilities = 2 x 500,000 = $1,000,000. Quick Assets = Acid Ratio x Current Liabilities = 1.5 x 500,000 = $750,000. Inventory = Current Assets - Quick Assets = 1,000,000 - 750,000 = $250,000. Inventory Turnover = Cost of Goods Sold / Inventory, so CGS = 5 x 250,000 = $1,250,000. Gross Profit Margin = (Sales - CGS) / Sales = 20%, so Sales = CGS / (1 - 0.20) = 1,250,000 / 0.80 = $1,562,500.

  2. Calculate the Average Receivable Turnover (ART) and Average Collection Period (ACP) of the following firms, and state which firm is the most efficient in receivables utilization and why. Which company will face a high variance in the future regarding turnover ratio and collection period? Firm A: Annual credit sales = Rs.1,800,000; Average accounts receivable = Rs.1,100,000 Firm B: Annual credit sales = Rs.1,700,000; Average accounts receivable = Rs.1,000,000 Firm C: Annual credit sales = Rs.1,500,000; Average accounts receivable = Rs.1,300,000
    Topic: Financial Statements & Ratio Analysis

    Receivable Turnover = Annual Credit Sales / Average Accounts Receivable Average Collection Period = 360 / Receivable Turnover Firm A: Turnover = 1,800,000 / 1,100,000 = 1.64 times; ACP = 360 / 1.64 = 219.51 days. Firm B: Turnover = 1,700,000 / 1,000,000 = 1.70 times; ACP = 360 / 1.70 = 211.76 days. Firm C: Turnover = 1,500,000 / 1,300,000 = 1.15 times; ACP = 360 / 1.15 = 313.04 days. Firm B is the most efficient in receivables utilization because it collects its receivables 1.7 times during the year, the highest turnover (shortest collection period) of the three firms. Firm C will face a high variance in the future regarding turnover ratio and collection period, because its turnover is the lowest and its collection period the longest, showing weak and unstable receivables management.

  3. Bank A pays 6.2% interest compounded semiannually and Bank B pays 6% interest compounded monthly. Which bank offers the higher effective annual rate? (Marks: 10)
    Topic: Time Value of Money & Effective Annual Rate

    Bank A: EAR = (1 + 0.062/2)^2 - 1 = (1.031)^2 - 1 = 1.062961 - 1 = 6.2961%. Bank B: EAR = (1 + 0.06/12)^12 - 1 = (1.005)^12 - 1 = 1.061678 - 1 = 6.1678%. Bank A offers the higher effective annual rate (6.2961% against 6.1678%), even though its nominal rate is only slightly higher, because the comparison must always be made on the effective annual rate basis.

  4. Calculate and compare the effective annual interest rates for Bank A and Bank B, if Bank A is offering an interest rate of 10% per year compounded monthly and Bank B is offering an interest rate of 8% per year compounded quarterly. (Marks: 5)
    Topic: Time Value of Money & Effective Annual Rate

    Bank A (compounded monthly): EAR = (1 + 0.10/12)^12 - 1 = (1.008333)^12 - 1 = 1.10471 - 1 = 10.47%. Bank B (compounded quarterly): EAR = (1 + 0.08/4)^4 - 1 = (1.02)^4 - 1 = 1.082432 - 1 = 8.24%. Bank A offers the higher effective annual rate (10.47% against 8.24%).

  5. 'Person A' has decided to invest Rs.1,000 at the end of each year for the next 10 years. After 10 years the person will deposit nothing and will let the accumulated amount compound for 40 additional years. 'Person B' will invest nothing for the next 10 years, but will invest Rs.1,000 per year (at the end of each year) for the following 40 years. Assuming an 8 percent rate of return compounded annually, which investment program will be worth more 50 years from now? (Marks: 10)
    Topic: Time Value of Money & Effective Annual Rate

    Person A: Value at the end of year 10 = 1,000 x [((1.08)^10 - 1) / 0.08] = 1,000 x 14.4866 = Rs.14,486.56. This amount then compounds for 40 more years: 14,486.56 x (1.08)^40 = 14,486.56 x 21.7245 = Rs.314,706 (approximately). Person B: Value at the end of year 50 = 1,000 x [((1.08)^40 - 1) / 0.08] = 1,000 x 259.0565 = Rs.259,057 (approximately). Person A's program will be worth more (about Rs.314,706 against Rs.259,057), even though Person A invests only Rs.10,000 in total against Person B's Rs.40,000. This is the power of compounding: money invested early gets many more years to grow.

  6. Differentiate between a bond's coupon rate and its yield to maturity. (Marks: 5)
    Topic: Bonds & Stock Valuation

    The coupon rate is the amount of interest paid every year, expressed as a percentage of the face value of the bond. It is fixed at the time of issue and determines the income that will be earned from the bond; it is the rate the bond issuer promises to pay the holder. Yield-to-maturity (YTM) is the single discount rate applied to all future interest and principal payments that produces a present value equal to the current price of the security. It is the rate of return estimated on a bond if it is held until the maturity date. In short: (1) YTM is the rate of return estimated on a bond held until maturity, while the coupon rate is the amount of interest paid per year as a percentage of face value. (2) YTM includes the coupon rate in its calculation and also reflects any capital gain or loss between the purchase price and face value.

  7. How much should you pay for a bond with $1,000 face value, a 14 percent coupon rate, and five years to maturity if your appropriate discount rate is 10 percent and interest is paid annually? (Marks: 5)
    Topic: Bonds & Stock Valuation

    Annual coupon payment = 14% x 1,000 = $140. Bond Price = 140/(1.10)^1 + 140/(1.10)^2 + 140/(1.10)^3 + 140/(1.10)^4 + 140/(1.10)^5 + 1,000/(1.10)^5 = 140 x [annuity factor at 10% for 5 years] + 1,000 x [PV factor at 10% for 5 years] = 140 x 3.7908 + 1,000 x 0.6209 = 530.71 + 620.92 = $1,151.63. You should pay about $1,151.63. The bond sells at a premium because its coupon rate (14%) is higher than the required rate of return (10%).

  8. A public limited company is expected to pay a Rs.0.50 per share dividend at the end of the year. The dividend is expected to grow at a constant rate of 7% per year. The required rate of return on the stock is 15%. What value per share of the company's stock is expected one year from now? (Marks: 5)
    Topic: Bonds & Stock Valuation

    Dividend at the end of year 2: D2 = D1 x (1 + g) = 0.50 x (1.07) = Rs.0.535. Price at the end of year 1: P1 = D2 / (r - g) = 0.535 / (0.15 - 0.07) = 0.535 / 0.08 = Rs.6.69 per share.

  9. Suppose you are a capital budgeting manager of a company. For the current year you have a total capital budget of Rs.6,000,000. The following projects are available for investment: Project A: Initial investment Rs.3 million; Annual cash flows Rs.1 million; Life 5 years; Discount rate 10% Project B: Initial investment Rs.3 million; Annual cash flows Rs.1.5 million; Life 3 years; Discount rate 8% Project C: Initial investment Rs.3 million; Annual cash flows Rs.1 million; Life 6 years; Discount rate 12% Which project(s) should be selected for investment within the given budget? (Assume the projects are divisible and not mutually exclusive.) (Marks: 5)
    Topic: Capital Budgeting & Project Evaluation

    Project A: PV of inflows = 1 x annuity factor (10%, 5 years) = 1 x 3.7908 = Rs.3.79 million. NPV = 3.79 - 3 = Rs.0.79 million. PI (profitability index as NPV/Investment) = 0.79 / 3 = 0.263. Project B: PV of inflows = 1.5 x annuity factor (8%, 3 years) = 1.5 x 2.5771 = Rs.3.86 million. NPV = 3.86 - 3 = Rs.0.86 million. PI = 0.86 / 3 = 0.287. Project C: PV of inflows = 1 x annuity factor (12%, 6 years) = 1 x 4.1114 = Rs.4.11 million. NPV = 4.11 - 3 = Rs.1.11 million. PI = 1.11 / 3 = 0.370. Ranking on the basis of PI: C first (0.370), B second (0.287), A third (0.263). With a budget of Rs.6,000,000: invest Rs.3,000,000 in Project C and Rs.3,000,000 in Project B. Remaining funds = Nil. Projects C and B should be selected.

  10. Following are the forecasted cash flows (in millions) of two projects: Year 0: Project A = -36, Project B = -50 Year 1: Project A = 20, Project B = 25 Year 2: Project A = 20, Project B = 25 Year 3: Project A = 20, Project B = 25 If both projects are mutually exclusive, which project should be chosen on the basis of the Profitability Index criterion? Assume a discount rate of 10%. (Marks: 5)
    Topic: Capital Budgeting & Project Evaluation

    Project A: NPV = -36 + 20/(1.10) + 20/(1.10)^2 + 20/(1.10)^3 = -36 + 18.18 + 16.53 + 15.03 = 13.74. PI = (NPV + Initial Investment) / Initial Investment = (13.74 + 36) / 36 = 1.381. Project B: NPV = -50 + 25/(1.10) + 25/(1.10)^2 + 25/(1.10)^3 = -50 + 22.73 + 20.66 + 18.78 = 12.14 (approximately). PI = (12.14 + 50) / 50 = 1.243. Project A should be selected because it has a positive NPV and a greater NPV and PI than Project B.

  11. How is the Profitability Index (PI) used in capital budgeting decisions? (Marks: 3)
    Topic: Capital Budgeting & Project Evaluation

    The Profitability Index method compares the present value of future cash inflows with the initial investment on a relative basis. PI = Present Value of Cash Flows / Initial Investment. A project with a PI greater than 1 is accepted, and a project is rejected when its PI is less than 1. If the present value of cash flows exceeds the initial investment, there is a positive net present value and a PI greater than 1, indicating that the project is acceptable. PI is especially useful for ranking projects when capital is rationed.

  12. How does probability analysis evaluate the financial feasibility of a project? (Marks: 5)
    Topic: Capital Budgeting & Project Evaluation

    In probability analysis, instead of taking a single estimate of future cash flows, the analyst assigns probabilities to the possible cash flows (or NPVs) of the project under different conditions. The expected NPV is then calculated as the probability-weighted average of the possible NPVs, and the dispersion (variance/standard deviation) of the possible outcomes measures the project's risk. A project is considered financially feasible if its expected NPV is positive at the firm's cost of capital; between competing projects, the one with the higher expected NPV and lower dispersion of outcomes is preferred. Probability analysis therefore evaluates feasibility not only by the expected return but also by the likelihood of unfavourable outcomes.

  13. What is capital rationing, and why does it act as a hurdle for a business to invest in the optimum investment? (Marks: 5)
    Topic: Capital Budgeting & Project Evaluation

    Capital rationing occurs when a company has more capital budgeting projects with positive net present values than it has money to invest in them, so some projects that should be accepted are excluded because financial capital is limited. It is an artificial constraint because management may dictate the amount to be invested for project purposes, and the amount is not based on marginal analysis in which the return of each proposal is related to the cost of capital and all positive-NPV projects are accepted. A company may adopt capital rationing because it is fearful of too much growth or hesitant to use external sources of financing. As a result, the firm cannot undertake all wealth-maximizing projects and its investment is below the optimum level.

  14. Differentiate between the single-period capital rationing and multi-period capital rationing. (Marks: 3)
    Topic: Capital Budgeting & Project Evaluation

    Single-period capital rationing is a situation where the company has a limited amount of funds in one investment period only. After that period, the company can access funds from various sources, e.g. issuing shares, borrowing from banks or issuing bonds. Multi-period capital rationing is the situation where capital is in limited availability in more than one period and the selection of projects cannot be made simply by ranking projects according to PI; techniques such as linear programming are used to select the combination of projects.

  15. Differentiate between soft and hard capital rationing. (Marks: 3)
    Topic: Capital Budgeting & Project Evaluation

    Capital rationing can be classified into hard and soft, based on whether the factors are external or internal. Hard capital rationing is when the constraints that affect business decisions are externally determined; hard capital rationing does not occur under perfect market conditions. Soft capital rationing occurs when investment expenditure is controlled and limited internally, by restrictions imposed by management itself.

  16. What is the difference between simple payback period and discounted payback period? (Marks: 3)
    Topic: Capital Budgeting & Project Evaluation

    The only difference between simple and discounted payback is discounting. The simple payback method does not care about the time value of money principle, while the discounted payback period takes care of this principle in its calculation, i.e. it measures the time needed for the discounted cash inflows to recover the initial investment.

  17. Differentiate between accounting break-even point and economic break-even point. (Marks: 5)
    Topic: Break-Even & Operating Leverage

    The difference between the accounting and economic break-even is a cost factor known as the opportunity cost of capital. In accounting break-even we calculate the accounting earnings first and then deduct all the costs from earnings to reach break-even, except the opportunity cost of capital that is invested in the project. A project that just breaks even in accounting terms will have a negative NPV. Economic break-even suggests that when you deduct other costs from accounting earnings you should also deduct the cost of capital employed. A project having a positive EVA (Economic Value Added) adds value to the firm and a negative EVA reduces the firm's value; economic break-even is the level at which EVA is zero.

  18. Explain break-even analysis and sensitivity analysis. (Marks: 3)
    Topic: Break-Even & Operating Leverage

    Break-even analysis is used to determine how much sales volume a business needs to start making a profit. It is especially useful when developing a pricing strategy. Break-even quantity = Fixed Costs / (Revenue per unit - Variable cost per unit). Sensitivity analysis is used to determine how sensitive a model (such as a project's NPV) is to changes in the value of its parameters. It is performed as a series of tests in which different parameter values are set to see how a change in a parameter causes a change in the outcome. By showing how the model behaviour responds to changes in parameter values, sensitivity analysis is a useful tool in evaluating a capital project.

  19. What is the difference between systematic and unsystematic risk? (Marks: 3)
    Topic: Risk, Return & Portfolio Management

    Systematic risk consists of unanticipated events that affect all assets to some degree. It is non-diversifiable, influences a large number of assets, and is also known as market risk. Systematic risk is measured by the beta coefficient, which measures the systematic risk inherent in an asset relative to the market as a whole. Unsystematic risk (unique risk) affects only specific assets or a firm. It is also known as diversifiable, unique or asset-specific risk. It can be eliminated by diversification; therefore a portfolio with many assets has almost zero unsystematic risk.

  20. Suppose you have 40% of your portfolio invested in firm A, 30% in firm B, 20% in firm C, and 10% in firm D. The betas for these firms are, respectively, 1.2, 1.4, 0.8, and 1.1. Calculate your portfolio beta. (Marks: 3)
    Topic: Risk, Return & Portfolio Management

    Portfolio beta = (Weight of A x Beta of A) + (Weight of B x Beta of B) + (Weight of C x Beta of C) + (Weight of D x Beta of D) = (40% x 1.2) + (30% x 1.4) + (20% x 0.8) + (10% x 1.1) = 0.48 + 0.42 + 0.16 + 0.11 = 1.17.

  21. What is the relationship between the market risk of a security and the rate of return that investors demand of that security? (Marks: 5)
    Topic: Risk, Return & Portfolio Management

    The extra return that investors require for taking risk is known as the risk premium. The Capital Asset Pricing Model states that the expected risk premium of an investment should be proportional to both its beta and the market risk premium. The expected rate of return from any investment is equal to the risk-free interest rate plus the risk premium: r = Rf + Beta x (Rm - Rf). The Security Market Line (SML) is the graphical representation of the CAPM equation; it relates the expected return investors demand of a security to its beta. The higher the market (systematic) risk of a security, the higher the return investors demand.

  22. Stock A and Stock B have betas of 1.5 and 0.75 respectively. The risk-free rate is 7% and the average market return is 13%. By how much is the required return of the higher-risk stock greater than that of the lower-risk stock? (Marks: 5)
    Topic: Risk, Return & Portfolio Management

    Using CAPM: Required return = Rf + Beta x (Rm - Rf). Stock A: Required return = 0.07 + 1.5 x (0.13 - 0.07) = 0.07 + 1.5 x 0.06 = 0.07 + 0.09 = 0.16 = 16%. Stock B: Required return = 0.07 + 0.75 x (0.13 - 0.07) = 0.07 + 0.75 x 0.06 = 0.07 + 0.045 = 0.115 = 11.5%. The required return of the higher-risk stock (A) is 16% - 11.5% = 4.5% greater than that of the lower-risk stock (B). Stock A is highly risky because its beta is greater than 1.

  23. Define aggressive stocks and defensive stocks. (Marks: 3)
    Topic: Risk, Return & Portfolio Management

    Aggressive stocks have high betas, greater than 1, meaning that their return moves more than one-to-one with changes in the return of the overall market; they are riskier than the market. Defensive stocks are less volatile to changes in market return and have a beta of less than one; they are less risky than the market.

  24. Why should the weighted average cost of capital (WACC) be used as the discount rate for analyzing the financial viability of a project? (Marks: 3)
    Topic: CAPM & Cost of Capital

    The discount rate used to find out the present value of future cash flows is normally the WACC. The WACC is the minimum return that a company must earn on its existing asset base to satisfy its creditors, owners, and other providers of capital, or they will invest elsewhere. Broadly speaking, a company's assets are financed by either debt or equity; WACC is the average of the costs of these sources of financing, each weighted by its respective use in the given situation. A project is financially viable only if it earns at least the WACC, so the WACC is the appropriate discount rate (hurdle rate) for evaluating it.

  25. Why is the Capital Asset Pricing Model (CAPM) more suitable to calculate the cost of equity as compared to the Dividend Growth Model? Discuss. (Marks: 5)
    Topic: CAPM & Cost of Capital

    The dividend growth model approach has limited application in practice because of its assumptions: (1) it assumes that the dividend per share will grow at a constant rate, g, forever; and (2) the expected dividend growth rate, g, should be less than the cost of equity to arrive at the simple growth formula. It also does not explicitly take risk into account and can only be applied to dividend-paying companies. CAPM, on the other hand, tells us that investors demand a higher rate of return for riskier shares. It is generally seen as a much better method of calculating the cost of equity than the dividend growth model in that it explicitly takes into account a company's level of systematic risk relative to the stock market as a whole, through the beta coefficient.

  26. What are geared and ungeared beta? Describe the difference between them. (Marks: 3)
    Topic: CAPM & Cost of Capital

    Geared beta is the beta attaching to the ordinary shares of a geared (levered) firm. These shares bear a risk higher than the firm's basic business activity, because financial leverage adds financial risk on top of business risk. Ungeared beta is the geared beta stripped of the effect of gearing. It corresponds to the asset (activity) beta in an equivalent ungeared firm — the inherent systematic riskiness of a firm's operations, before allowing for gearing.

  27. Why is the weighted average cost of capital (WACC) of a levered firm lesser than that of an unlevered firm? Explain briefly. (Marks: 3)
    Topic: Capital Structure & Dividend Policy

    Interest paid on debt is a tax-deductible expense, so debt financing creates an interest tax shield: the government effectively bears part of the cost of debt, making the after-tax cost of debt lower than its before-tax cost. When a firm uses debt in its capital structure, this tax saving pulls the overall weighted average cost of capital down. An unlevered firm has no debt and therefore no interest tax shield, so its WACC (equal to its cost of equity) remains higher.

  28. Differentiate between a levered firm and an unlevered firm. (Marks: 3)
    Topic: Capital Structure & Dividend Policy

    Leverage refers to the amount of debt used to finance a firm's assets. A firm with significantly more debt than equity is considered to be highly leveraged (a levered firm uses debt in its capital structure and its shareholders bear financial risk in addition to business risk). A firm with no debt in its capital structure is known as an unlevered firm; it is financed entirely by equity.

  29. In the year ending January 2008, Wal-Mart paid out Rs.1,326 million as debt interest. How much more tax would Wal-Mart have paid if the firm had been entirely financed by equity? What would be the present value of Wal-Mart's interest tax shield if the company planned to keep its borrowing permanently at the 2008 level? Assume an interest rate of 8% and a corporate tax rate of 35%. (Marks: 5)
    Topic: Capital Structure & Dividend Policy

    Additional tax if entirely equity financed = Interest x Tax rate = 1,326 million x 35% = Rs.464.1 million. (With debt, this amount of tax is saved every year through the interest tax shield.) Present value of the interest tax shield (permanent debt) = Annual tax shield / Interest rate = 464.1 / 0.08 = Rs.5,801.25 million. Equivalently, Debt = Interest / Rate = 1,326 / 0.08 = Rs.16,575 million, and PV of tax shield = Tax rate x Debt = 0.35 x 16,575 = Rs.5,801.25 million.

  30. A company has Rs.800,000 of 12% debt financing. Calculate the present value of the tax benefit if the tax rate is 40%. By how much would the tax benefit change if the tax rate decreases to 30%? (Marks: 3)
    Topic: Capital Structure & Dividend Policy

    Annual interest = 800,000 x 12% = Rs.96,000. Annual interest tax shield at 40% = 96,000 x 40% = Rs.38,400. Since the debt is permanent, PV of tax benefit = Tax rate x Debt = 0.40 x 800,000 = Rs.320,000 (equivalently 38,400 / 0.12). At a 30% tax rate: annual tax shield = 96,000 x 30% = Rs.28,800; PV of tax benefit = 0.30 x 800,000 = Rs.240,000. The PV of the tax benefit would decrease by 320,000 - 240,000 = Rs.80,000 (the annual shield falls by Rs.9,600).

  31. What level of debt financing (high or low) would you suggest for the following firms: (a) a firm paying high tax; (b) a firm paying no tax? (Marks: 5)
    Topic: Capital Structure & Dividend Policy

    (a) Firm paying high tax: high debt financing is suggested, because interest is tax deductible; the firm can reduce its tax liability through the interest tax shield, which is more valuable at a high tax rate. (b) Firm paying no tax: low debt financing is suggested, because the firm is already not paying any tax, so there is no tax benefit from debt, while debt would still add financial risk and fixed interest obligations.

  32. A public limited company dealing in foods and beverages is financed 80% by common stock and 20% by bonds. The expected return on the common stock is 12% and the rate of interest on bonds is 6%. Assume the bonds are default free and there are no taxes. Now assume the company issues more debt and uses the proceeds to retire equity; the new financing mix is 60% equity and 40% debt. If the debt is still default free, what happens to the expected rate of return on equity? What happens to the expected return on the overall mix of debt and equity? (Marks: 10)
    Topic: Capital Structure & Dividend Policy

    Initial overall return (WACC) = (0.80 x 12%) + (0.20 x 6%) = 9.6% + 1.2% = 10.8%. According to Modigliani and Miller Proposition I (no taxes), the value of the firm and its overall cost of capital are independent of capital structure, so the expected return on the overall mix of debt and equity remains unchanged at 10.8%. By MM Proposition II, the return on equity rises as gearing increases: rE = rA + (D/E) x (rA - rD) = 10.8% + (40/60) x (10.8% - 6%) = 10.8% + 3.2% = 14%. Check: (0.60 x 14%) + (0.40 x 6%) = 8.4% + 2.4% = 10.8%. So the expected rate of return on equity increases from 12% to 14%, while the expected return on the overall mix stays the same at 10.8%.

  33. What is dividend policy? Describe the types of dividends. (Marks: 5)
    Topic: Capital Structure & Dividend Policy

    Dividend policy is the policy a company uses to decide how much it will pay out to shareholders in dividends. Types of dividends: 1. Cash dividends (most common) are paid out in the form of real cash. They are a form of investment income and are taxable to the recipient in the year they are paid. This is the most common method of sharing corporate profits. 2. Stock (scrip) dividends are paid out in the form of additional shares of the issuing corporation. They are usually issued in proportion to shares owned (e.g. a 5% stock dividend gives 5 extra shares for every 100 shares owned). Like a stock split, this increases the total number of shares while lowering the price of each share, and does not change the market capitalization. 3. Property dividends (dividends in specie) are paid out in the form of assets of the issuing corporation, usually products or services provided by the corporation, or securities of other companies owned by the issuer.

  34. Compare and contrast the stable dividend per share policy and the constant dividend payout policy. (Marks: 5)
    Topic: Capital Structure & Dividend Policy

    Constant dividend payout policy: a fixed percentage of earnings is paid out as dividend. Under this policy the dividend amount will vary because net income is not constant; it may even drop to nil in case of a loss, which results in variability of return to investors and can lower the market price of the share. Stable dividend per share policy: a fixed amount of dividend per share is paid every year. It is looked upon favourably by investors and implies a low-risk firm; investors can easily forecast and predict their earnings, and it aids financial planning because the cash flow (dividend amount) can be ascertained with accuracy.

  35. How could a stable dividend policy increase the marketability of a firm's shares? (Marks: 3)
    Topic: Capital Structure & Dividend Policy

    Under a stable dividend policy a fixed dividend per share is paid regularly. This is looked upon favourably by investors and implies that the firm is low risk, which increases the marketability of the firm's shares. Cash flow can also be planned, as the dividend amount can be ascertained with accuracy, which aids investors' financial planning and makes the shares more attractive to buy.

  36. Differentiate between an Initial Public Offering (IPO) and private placement of securities. (Marks: 5)
    Topic: Capital Structure & Dividend Policy

    An initial public offering (IPO) is a private company's first sale of stock to the general public. IPOs are often offered by smaller and younger companies looking to grow rapidly; they are a relatively risky investment because the company has a limited history and the stock's future value is uncertain. An IPO requires registration with the securities regulator and involves expensive underwriting fees; the shares are then traded on public exchanges. A private placement is a non-public offering of securities to a small group of institutional and accredited investors (banks, mutual funds, insurance companies and pension funds). It is typically exempt from the usual registration requirements, so the issuing company avoids expensive underwriting fees. Private placements are not traded on public exchanges.
