  1. Define language and explain why language is regarded as the most effective means of communication.
    Topic: Language and Communication

    Language is the system of meaningful sounds, words and sentences through which human beings express their thoughts, feelings and experiences to one another. Language is regarded as the most effective means of communication because other means — signs and symbols, facial expressions and bodily gestures — are limited: they can convey only simple, concrete messages and cannot express complex, abstract feelings and ideas. Language, in its spoken and written forms, can communicate every kind of thought completely, and its written form also preserves knowledge and information for future generations.

  2. Describe the basic components of language as given in the course.
    Topic: Language and Communication

    The course describes four basic components of language. (1) Hurf (sound/letter): the smallest sound units from which words are formed. (2) Maaniyaat (semantics): the meanings of words, which reveal the different shades of meaning a word carries. (3) Nahv (syntax): the knowledge of how words are arranged in a particular order to form meaningful sentences, e.g. the arrangement of noun, verb and object. (4) Tanazuraat (context): the background or situation with reference to which words carry a particular meaning; without context the true meaning of words cannot be determined.

  3. Discuss the non-verbal means of communication and their limitations.
    Topic: Language and Communication

    Besides language, man communicates through (1) signs and symbols, e.g. traffic signals whose different colours give different directions; (2) facial expressions, through which the feelings of the heart are reflected on the face; and (3) bodily movements and gestures, which complete and support the act of communication. Their limitations are that the communication achieved through them is very limited and incomplete: feelings and experiences can be indicated only vaguely, complex ideas cannot be expressed, and there is a danger of misunderstanding. Therefore they can support language but can never replace it.

  4. Explain Hafiz Mahmood Shirani's Punjab theory of the origin of Urdu.
    Topic: Origin and Development of Urdu

    Hafiz Mahmood Shirani presented his theory in his book 'Punjab Mein Urdu' (1928). On historical grounds he argued that Urdu was born in Punjab, because Muslims settled in Punjab long before they reached Delhi, and the mixed language that arose there from the contact of the newcomers and the local people travelled to Delhi with them. On linguistic grounds he showed that Urdu and Punjabi share a great deal: their vocabulary, idioms and grammatical structure (e.g. word formation and sentence structure) are very close to each other, which proves that Urdu's foundations were laid in Punjab.

  5. Compare the Sindh theory of Syed Sulaiman Nadvi and the Deccan theory of Nasiruddin Hashmi regarding the origin of Urdu.
    Topic: Origin and Development of Urdu

    Syed Sulaiman Nadvi, in his research articles 'Naqosh-e-Sulaimani' (1939), held that Urdu originated in Sindh: Arab traders and preachers reached Sindh first, and the language born of their contact with the local people was the earliest form of Urdu, which later spread to other regions. Nasiruddin Hashmi, in 'Dakan Mein Urdu', presented the Deccan theory: Urdu took shape in the Deccan (South India) where it flourished under the Bahmani, Adil Shahi and Qutb Shahi courts and produced early literature; from the Deccan it travelled to North India. Both theories agree that Urdu arose from the contact of Muslims with local populations, but they differ about the region where this process first took place.

  6. Why is Urdu called a 'Lashkari' (camp) language?
    Topic: Origin and Development of Urdu

    The word 'Urdu' itself comes from Turkish, where it means a camp or an army. Urdu is called a Lashkari language because it was not created by any individual or academy; it developed gradually in the camps, bazaars and cities where Muslim soldiers, traders and settlers speaking Persian, Arabic and Turkish came into daily contact with the local people speaking Khari Boli and other local dialects. Out of this mutual contact a common language of communication emerged, which was later refined by poets and writers and became a full literary language.

  7. Describe the role of Fort William College in the development of Urdu prose.
    Topic: Origin and Development of Urdu

    Fort William College was established in 1800 at Calcutta to train British officers in Indian languages. Under Dr. John Gilchrist, head of its Hindustani department, Indian scholars were employed to write and translate simple, useful prose books in Urdu. Mir Amman's 'Bagh-o-Bahar' is the most famous product of this institution; works like 'Tota Kahani' and translations of history and ethics were also produced. These books gave Urdu a simple, clear prose style, created textbooks for learners, and laid the foundation of modern Urdu prose.

  8. Write a note on Maulvi Abdul Haq and the Anjuman Taraqqi-e-Urdu.
    Topic: Origin and Development of Urdu

    Anjuman Taraqqi-e-Urdu was founded in 1903 to promote and protect the Urdu language. Its most devoted secretary was Maulvi Abdul Haq, who served Urdu all his life: he published standard journals and dictionaries, collected rare manuscripts, wrote the rules of Urdu grammar, and defended Urdu at every forum. For these unmatched services he is honoured as 'Baba-e-Urdu' (the Father of Urdu). The Anjuman continues to work for the promotion of Urdu language and literature.

  9. Discuss the importance of Urdu as the national language of Pakistan.
    Topic: Importance of Urdu

    Urdu is the national language of Pakistan under Article 251 of the 1973 Constitution, and Quaid-e-Azam declared in 1948 that Urdu would be the state language of Pakistan. Although it is the mother tongue of a minority, it is understood and spoken in every province, so it serves as a lingua franca and a powerful bond of national unity among people of different regions and languages. A vast treasure of religious, historical and literary knowledge exists in Urdu, and it is the medium of instruction in most government schools; therefore its teaching is essential for national cohesion, education and cultural continuity.

  10. State and explain the main principles of language teaching.
    Topic: Principles of Language Teaching

    The main principles are: (1) Principle of natural order — language should be taught in the order listening, speaking, reading, writing, as a child learns his mother tongue. (2) Principle of oral work — at the primary stage maximum emphasis should be on listening and speaking, because up to Class 2 a child learns 60 to 80 percent through these two skills. (3) Principle of selection and gradation — teaching material should be selected according to the age, level and interest of students and arranged from simple to complex. (4) Principle of motivation — students' interest and willingness must be created and maintained. (5) Principle of practice — language is a skill, so maximum practice and use must be provided. (6) Principle of using teaching aids — suitable aids make learning effective and lasting. (7) Principle of individual differences — teaching should respect the differing abilities of learners.

  11. Why is oral work emphasised at the primary stage of language teaching?
    Topic: Principles of Language Teaching

    Oral work is emphasised because language learning naturally begins with listening and speaking: a child first understands and speaks his mother tongue long before he reads or writes. Research referred to in the course shows that up to Class 2 a child learns about 60 to 80 percent through listening and speaking, while the share of reading and writing is small at this stage. Oral work is also more interesting for young children, it is easier than reading and writing, children get tired less, and it lays the firm foundation on which reading and writing are later built. If the oral foundation is weak, progress in reading and writing also suffers.

  12. What is the natural order of acquiring the four language skills? Justify your answer.
    Topic: Principles of Language Teaching

    The natural order is: listening, speaking, reading and writing. A child first listens to the sounds around him and understands them; then he imitates and begins to speak; reading is taught after he can speak the language; and writing, which needs both mental and muscular control, comes last. This is the order in which every child learns his mother tongue without any formal teaching, and language teaching in school succeeds best when it follows the same natural order instead of beginning with reading and writing.

  13. Describe the Grammar-Translation Method with its merits and demerits.
    Topic: Teaching Methods

    The Grammar-Translation Method is the oldest method of language teaching. The mother tongue is the medium of instruction; grammar rules are taught and memorised, and sentences are translated from the mother tongue into the target language and back. Reading and writing receive the main attention. Merits: it is easy for the teacher, works with large classes, explains difficult words and rules clearly, and helps in examinations. Demerits: speaking and listening are neglected, pronunciation suffers, students cannot use the language in real life, and learning becomes dull and rule-bound rather than a living skill.

  14. Explain the Direct Method and show how it differs from the Grammar-Translation Method.
    Topic: Teaching Methods

    In the Direct Method the target language is taught directly, without the help of the mother tongue. Meanings are explained through objects, pictures, actions and situations; oral work, correct pronunciation and conversation are emphasised, and grammar is learned indirectly through use. It differs from the Grammar-Translation Method in every respect: the medium is the target language instead of the mother tongue; speaking and listening come first instead of reading and writing; grammar is picked up through practice instead of memorised rules; and translation is completely avoided.

  15. Describe the Audio-Lingual Method. Why are drills and the language laboratory important in it?
    Topic: Teaching Methods

    The Audio-Lingual Method treats language as a set of habits. It is based on structural patterns of the language and on behaviourist psychology: the learner hears model sentences, repeats them, and practises them through drills until correct responses become automatic habits. Listening and speaking are taught before reading and writing, and errors are corrected immediately so that wrong habits do not form. Drills are important because habit formation needs repeated practice of patterns; the language laboratory is important because it gives every learner individual listening and speaking practice with model pronunciation, recordings and self-correction, which an ordinary classroom cannot provide to all students at once.

  16. What is meant by the Communicative Approach to language teaching?
    Topic: Teaching Methods

    The Communicative Approach aims at developing communicative competence — the ability to use the language appropriately and effectively in real-life situations — rather than merely knowing its grammar rules. Classroom activities are built around genuine communication: dialogues, role play, pair and group work, discussions and tasks in which learners exchange real information and opinions. Fluency and meaningful use are valued along with accuracy, and the teacher acts as a facilitator of communication instead of a lecturer.

  17. Discuss the merits and demerits of the Lecture Method (Khitabiya Andaz) and give suggestions for its improvement.
    Topic: Teaching Methods

    Merits: a large amount of material can be presented in a short time; it is useful for introducing a new topic and for large classes; and it requires no costly aids. Demerits: students remain passive listeners and their interest and attention decline; the teacher stays unaware of the individual problems of students; there is a danger that much of the material is forgotten because students cannot keep attention on listening for long; and there is little exchange of ideas because the method rests on one-way telling. Suggestions for improvement: it should not be used in the early classes (up to about Class 3); it should be used only where suitable, and mixed with question-answer so that students participate; the lecture should be interesting, neither too long nor boring; and the teacher's delivery should be clear and well prepared.

  18. Compare the Kindergarten and Montessori methods of education.
    Topic: Teaching Methods

    Both methods are meant for young children and respect the child's nature, but they differ in origin and technique. The Kindergarten method was given by Froebel: the school is a 'children's garden' and the teacher a gardener; education proceeds through play, songs, gifts and occupations, with emphasis on social and group activities. The Montessori method was given by Dr. Maria Montessori: it stresses auto-education and individual work with specially designed didactic apparatus, training of the senses, freedom within a prepared environment, and the teacher as a quiet guide or directress. In short, Kindergarten emphasises guided group play, while Montessori emphasises individual self-activity and sense training.

  19. What is the Project Method? Describe its main features and advantages.
    Topic: Teaching Methods

    The Project Method, given by W. H. Kilpatrick on the basis of Dewey's pragmatism, is learning through a purposeful, real-life activity (a project) carried out by the students themselves, e.g. preparing a class magazine or organising an exhibition. Its features are: the problem arises from the students' own interest; they plan, execute and evaluate the work themselves; and knowledge of several subjects is gained in an integrated way. Advantages: learning is by doing, so it is lasting; it develops cooperation, responsibility, self-confidence and practical ability; and it connects school learning with real life. Its limitation is that systematic, sequential study of a subject may suffer, so it should supplement, not replace, regular teaching.

  20. Define teaching aids and classify them with examples.
    Topic: Teaching Aids

    Teaching aids are the devices and materials which a teacher uses to make teaching clear, interesting and effective, and to give learners direct or near-direct experience of the things taught. They are classified into three types. (1) Audio aids, which appeal to the sense of hearing: radio, tape recorder, record player and the listening side of the language laboratory. (2) Visual aids, which appeal to the sense of sight: blackboard, charts, maps, pictures, models, flashcards and real objects. (3) Audio-visual aids, which appeal to both hearing and sight: television, films, computer and multimedia projector.

  21. Discuss the importance and uses of teaching aids in the teaching of Urdu.
    Topic: Teaching Aids

    Teaching aids make abstract ideas concrete and easy to understand, especially for young children who learn through the senses. They create interest and attention, remove the dullness of verbal teaching, and make learning lasting because impressions received through more than one sense are retained longer. In Urdu teaching they help in explaining new words through pictures and real objects, in teaching correct pronunciation through recordings, in presenting stories and poems vividly, and in catering to individual differences, since slow and fast learners can all benefit. They also save the teacher's time and energy and make the classroom atmosphere lively.

  22. Why is the blackboard called the most useful teaching aid? How should a teacher use it effectively?
    Topic: Teaching Aids

    The blackboard is called the most useful teaching aid because it is available in every classroom, is the cheapest of all aids, needs no electricity or preparation of material, and can be used for every subject and every stage of the lesson — writing new words, drawing figures, summarising the lesson and noting home work. For effective use the teacher should write neatly, boldly and in straight lines so that the whole class can read; organise the board into sections; use coloured chalk for important points; write only the essential matter; and stand aside while writing so that students can see, and clean the board before leaving.

  23. What are the four basic language skills? Distinguish between receptive and productive skills.
    Topic: Language Skills

    The four basic language skills are listening, speaking, reading and writing. Listening and reading are called receptive skills because in them the learner receives and understands language produced by others; they are also called passive skills. Speaking and writing are called productive skills because in them the learner himself produces language to express his thoughts; they are also called active or expressive skills. The receptive skills normally develop before the productive ones and provide the input on which production is based.

  24. State the objectives of developing the speaking skill and the principles a teacher should follow for it.
    Topic: Language Skills

    Objectives of the speaking skill: (1) the child should speak correctly and purely; (2) the child should speak simply and in an understandable manner, so that he not only speaks but also makes his meaning clear; and (3) the child should speak in a pleasant and interesting style so that his conversation is attractive and effective. Principles for the teacher: he should present himself as a model — his own pronunciation must be correct and his tone standard, because children imitate the teacher; he should create such an environment that children speak without hesitation and in simple, understandable language; he should give maximum practice in speaking, encouraging children more and more; and during practice he should correct mistakes with affection, never discouraging or punishing weak children, and pay special attention to slow and shy learners.

  25. Differentiate between oral (loud) reading and silent reading.
    Topic: Language Skills

    In oral reading the student reads aloud, while in silent reading he reads quietly with the eyes and mind only. Oral reading is essential at the primary stage: it enables the teacher to check and correct pronunciation, teaches proper stress and pause, and builds the child's confidence to read before others; but it is slow and not suitable for deep study. Silent reading is faster, directs full attention to meaning and comprehension, and is the method of study at higher stages and in practical life; but the teacher cannot directly check the child's reading in it. Both are necessary, oral reading dominating in the early classes and silent reading in the later ones.

  26. How should a poem be taught at the primary level? Also state the aims of teaching poetry.
    Topic: Teaching of Poetry, Prose, Grammar and Composition

    Aims of teaching poetry are to develop the aesthetic sense and love of beauty, to enrich the imagination and emotions, to give the pleasure of rhythm and music in language, and to refine the child's taste. Method: the teacher should first give a brief, interesting introduction; then present a model recitation of the poem with correct pronunciation, rhythm and feeling, because children learn poetry by imitation; difficult words should be explained briefly so that the flow is not broken; the poem should then be read by the students in imitation, in chorus and individually; simple questions should confirm understanding; and memorisation should come naturally through enjoyment, not by force. Heavy grammatical analysis and lengthy explanation must be avoided, as they kill the beauty of the poem.

  27. Compare the inductive and deductive methods of teaching grammar. Which one is better for young learners and why?
    Topic: Teaching of Poetry, Prose, Grammar and Composition

    The inductive method proceeds from examples to the rule: students are given several sentences, they observe the common feature, and with the teacher's help they themselves arrive at the grammatical rule. The deductive method proceeds from the rule to examples: the rule is stated and memorised first, and then applied to given examples. For young learners the inductive method is better, because it follows the natural way of learning, keeps students active and interested, develops their power of observation and reasoning, and the rule discovered by the learners themselves is remembered far longer than a rule imposed on them.

  28. What is composition (Insha)? Differentiate between guided and free composition.
    Topic: Teaching of Poetry, Prose, Grammar and Composition

    Composition (Insha) is the art of expressing one's own thoughts in connected, organised written language. In guided (controlled) composition the teacher provides full support: the topic, outline, vocabulary and even model sentences are given, and students write within that framework; it is used at the primary and middle stages. In free composition students choose or are given only a topic and express their own ideas in their own words and style without detailed guidance; it is suitable for higher classes. Guided composition builds accuracy and confidence step by step, while free composition develops originality and independent expression.

  29. What is meant by active and passive vocabulary? How can a teacher enrich the vocabulary of his students?
    Topic: Vocabulary and Curriculum

    The words which a person actually uses in his own speaking and writing are his active vocabulary, while the words which he understands when he hears or reads them but does not himself use are his passive vocabulary. A teacher can enrich students' vocabulary by teaching new words in meaningful sentences and real contexts rather than as isolated lists; by using pictures, objects and actions to fix meanings; by encouraging wide reading of stories and library books; by word games, synonyms and antonyms exercises; and by giving students frequent chances to use new words in speaking and writing, so that passive words gradually become active.

  30. Describe the general objectives of teaching Urdu in Classes 1 to 3.
    Topic: Vocabulary and Curriculum

    In Class 1 the objectives are that the child should listen attentively and understand simple talk, speak in simple words and short sentences, recognise the Urdu alphabets and simple words through the Qaida (primer) and picture reading, and begin to form letters. In Class 2 the child should join letters to form words, read simple sentences and short passages aloud with correct pronunciation, speak about familiar things, and write words and simple sentences neatly. In Class 3 the child should read short stories and paragraphs with understanding and reasonable speed, express his experiences orally in connected sentences, write simple sentences and short paragraphs, and develop a basic stock of vocabulary for daily use. Throughout these classes the emphasis gradually shifts from oral work towards reading and writing, while interest in the language is kept alive through stories, poems and play.
