  1. What is dream analysis? Who gave this technique?
    Topic: Freud and Psychoanalysis

    Dream analysis is a psychoanalytic technique in which the patient's dreams are interpreted to uncover repressed unconscious wishes, conflicts and desires. Sigmund Freud gave this technique and described dreams as the 'royal road to the unconscious'. Freud distinguished the manifest content (the dream as remembered) from the latent content (its hidden symbolic meaning); through free association on the dream elements, the analyst uncovers the latent content, because during sleep the ego's defenses relax and repressed material expresses itself in disguised, symbolic form.

  2. What is libido?
    Topic: Freud and Psychoanalysis

    Libido is the psychic energy of the id, derived mainly from the sexual (life) instinct, Eros. For Freud it is the driving force behind the pleasure-seeking behavior of the id, and it becomes invested (cathected) in different erogenous zones during the psychosexual stages — mouth in the oral stage, anus in the anal stage, genitals in the phallic stage. Jung used the term more broadly for a general creative life force or psychic energy essential for biological, psychological and spiritual development.

  3. Describe the defense mechanisms of the ego, with the help of a case/example.
    Topic: Freud and Psychoanalysis

    Defense mechanisms are the ego's unconscious, self-deceptive techniques for reducing anxiety arising from conflict between the id, superego and reality. Important ones are: repression (pushing threatening memories/desires into the unconscious — the basic defense), denial (refusing to accept reality), projection (attributing one's own unacceptable impulses to others), displacement (redirecting an impulse to a safer target), rationalization (giving logical excuses for unacceptable behavior), regression (retreating to an earlier stage of life), reaction formation (expressing the opposite of the true impulse) and sublimation (channeling impulses into socially valued activities). Case example: a student who is heart-broken channels his feelings into writing poetry — this is sublimation; if the same student, when scolded by his father, goes home and shouts at his younger brother, that is displacement.

  4. Describe Freud's structural model of personality (id, ego and superego).
    Topic: Freud and Psychoanalysis

    Freud divided personality into three interacting structures. (1) Id: present at birth, totally unconscious, a reservoir of libido that works on the pleasure principle and demands immediate gratification through primary-process thinking. (2) Ego: develops from the id in the first years of life, works on the reality principle, and mediates between the id's demands, the superego's restrictions and the demands of reality; it uses defense mechanisms to control anxiety. (3) Superego: the last to develop (around age 5), it is the internalized moral code of parents and society, consisting of the conscience and the ego-ideal; it works on the morality principle. A healthy personality reflects a strong ego balancing id and superego; imbalance produces anxiety and neurosis.

  5. Briefly describe Freud's psychosexual stages of development.
    Topic: Freud and Psychoanalysis

    Freud proposed five stages in which the libido focuses on a different erogenous zone; fixation (from unresolved conflict or over-indulgence) at any stage leaves permanent traits. (1) Oral (birth–1 year): pleasure from the mouth — sucking, eating; fixation gives oral traits like dependency, smoking, overeating. (2) Anal (1–3 years): pleasure from bowel control/toilet training; fixation gives stinginess, stubbornness and excessive neatness (anal retentive) or messiness (anal expulsive). (3) Phallic (3–6 years): pleasure from the genitals; the Oedipus complex in boys and Electra complex in girls occur and are resolved by identification with the same-sex parent. (4) Latency (6 years–puberty): libido is dormant/sublimated into school, peers and nonsexual activities. (5) Genital (puberty onward): mature sexual interests directed toward the opposite sex.

  6. Write a brief biography of Carl Jung.
    Topic: Jung, Adler and Horney

    Carl Gustav Jung (1875–1961) was a Swiss psychiatrist and the founder of analytical psychology. He studied medicine at Basel and worked at the Burgholzli psychiatric hospital in Zurich under Eugen Bleuler, where he developed the word association test to explore unconscious complexes. In 1907 he met Freud and became his close collaborator and expected successor, but he broke with Freud around 1912–13 because he rejected Freud's purely sexual definition of libido and his view of the unconscious. Jung then developed his own system: the personal unconscious and the deeper collective unconscious containing universal archetypes (persona, shadow, anima, animus, self); he also introduced the psychological types introversion and extraversion and the goal of individuation — becoming a unified self. His major work of the break period was 'Psychology of the Unconscious' (1912), and he remained one of the most influential personality theorists of the twentieth century.

  7. Explain Jung's collective unconscious and archetypes.
    Topic: Jung, Adler and Horney

    Jung divided the psyche into the ego, the personal unconscious (repressed/forgotten personal experiences and complexes) and the collective unconscious — the deepest layer, shared by all human beings, containing the accumulated memories and patterns of the whole human species. Its contents are called archetypes: universal, inherited predispositions to experience and represent basic human themes, seen in myths, dreams and symbols across cultures. Major archetypes are the persona (the social mask we present to others), the shadow (the dark, animal side of the psyche), the anima (feminine component of the male psyche), the animus (masculine component of the female psyche), the mother archetype, and the self — the central archetype of wholeness whose realization is the goal of individuation.

  8. What did Adler mean by inferiority complex and fictional finalism?
    Topic: Jung, Adler and Horney

    For Adler, every child experiences feelings of inferiority from being small and dependent; striving to overcome this inferiority and move toward superiority/perfection is the master motive of life. When feelings of inferiority become excessive and block normal striving — due to pampering, neglect or organ inferiority — an inferiority complex results. Fictional finalism is Adler's idea (borrowed from Vaihinger) that behavior is guided not only by the past but by fictional goals we create for the future — subjective final goals (e.g. 'I will be secure only if I am perfect') that may not exist in reality yet pull our behavior forward, just as Jung's teleology holds that future goals pull us ahead.

  9. Describe Karen Horney's concept of basic anxiety and the three neurotic trends.
    Topic: Jung, Adler and Horney

    Horney held that the core of neurosis is basic anxiety: the child's feeling of being isolated and helpless in a potentially hostile world, produced by disturbed parent–child relations (lack of genuine warmth and affection). To cope with basic anxiety the child develops one of three neurotic trends (interpersonal orientations): (1) moving toward people — the compliant type, seeking love and approval; (2) moving against people — the hostile/aggressive type, seeking power and control; (3) moving away from people — the detached type, seeking independence and withdrawal. In the healthy person all three are used flexibly; in the neurotic one trend dominates rigidly. Horney also listed ten neurotic needs, e.g. the need for affection and approval, for power, and for perfection.

  10. According to Maslow's theory, write the names of five self-actualized persons and justify your answer.
    Topic: Humanistic Theories: Maslow and Rogers

    Maslow studied healthy, fully functioning people and listed as self-actualizers historical figures such as Abraham Lincoln, Thomas Jefferson, Albert Einstein, Eleanor Roosevelt and Baruch Spinoza. Justification: these persons show Maslow's characteristics of self-actualizers — they perceive reality accurately, accept themselves and others, are spontaneous, problem-centered rather than self-centered, autonomous, creative, democratic in attitudes, and capable of peak experiences; having satisfied their lower deficiency needs (physiological, safety, belongingness and esteem), they were motivated by being (B) needs — truth, goodness, beauty and justice — and realized their own potential, which is exactly Maslow's definition of self-actualization.

  11. Describe Maslow's hierarchy of needs.
    Topic: Humanistic Theories: Maslow and Rogers

    Maslow arranged human needs in a hierarchy of five levels, in which lower needs must be at least partly satisfied before higher needs emerge. (1) Physiological needs — food, water, sleep, sex; the most basic. (2) Safety needs — security, stability, freedom from fear. (3) Belongingness and love needs — affection, family, group membership. (4) Esteem needs — achievement, competence, confidence and the respect of others. The first four are deficiency (D) needs. (5) Self-actualization needs — realizing one's full potential and becoming everything one is capable of becoming; this is a growth/being (B) need. Later Maslow also discussed cognitive, aesthetic needs and peak experiences of self-actualizers.

  12. What is Rogers' concept of unconditional positive regard?
    Topic: Humanistic Theories: Maslow and Rogers

    Unconditional positive regard is Carl Rogers' term for accepting, prizing and valuing a person completely, without conditions or judgment — loving the person for who he is, not for behaving in particular ways. Rogers contrasted it with conditions of worth, in which parents/others give love only when the child meets their standards, forcing the child to deny parts of his real self and producing incongruence between self and experience. In Rogers' person-centered therapy the therapist provides unconditional positive regard (with genuineness and empathy), so the client can drop defensiveness, accept his organismic experience and move toward becoming a fully functioning person.

  13. Explain Rogers' concept of the self and incongruence.
    Topic: Humanistic Theories: Maslow and Rogers

    Rogers views personality in terms of the self: an organized, consistent pattern of perceptions of 'I' and 'me' together with the values attached to them. The infant develops a self-concept through interaction with significant others and has two inborn needs — positive regard from others and positive self-regard. When others impose conditions of worth, the person introjects their values and denies experiences inconsistent with the self-concept; the gap between the self-concept and actual organismic experience is called incongruence, which produces anxiety and defensive distortion or denial. Congruence — harmony between self and experience — marks the fully functioning, psychologically healthy person.

  14. What is the difference between source traits and surface traits?
    Topic: Trait Theories: Allport and Cattell

    These are Raymond Cattell's terms. Surface traits are clusters of observable behaviors that appear to go together but do not necessarily share a common cause — they are correlations of manifest responses (e.g. a person who is talkative, cheerful and energetic shows the surface trait of sociability); they are less stable and less important for understanding personality. Source traits are the deeper, underlying basic factors that cause the surface clusters; they are discovered through factor analysis, are fewer in number, stable and truly structural units of personality. Cattell identified 16 source traits (measured by the 16 PF questionnaire), such as warmth, dominance, emotional stability and vigilance.

  15. Who gave factor analysis, and how did Cattell use it in personality study?
    Topic: Trait Theories: Allport and Cattell

    Factor analysis is a statistical technique (originated by Charles Spearman) that reduces a large number of correlated variables to a few underlying factors. Raymond Cattell applied it systematically to personality: starting from Allport and Odbert's list of about 4,500 trait words, he reduced them to about 171 surface traits and, by factor-analyzing ratings and questionnaire data (L-data, Q-data and T-data), extracted 16 fundamental source traits of personality, on which he built the Sixteen Personality Factor (16 PF) Questionnaire. Eysenck used the same technique to derive his superfactors (extraversion, neuroticism, psychoticism).

  16. Describe Allport's classification of traits (cardinal, central and secondary).
    Topic: Trait Theories: Allport and Cattell

    Gordon Allport, the founder of trait theory, defined a trait as a neuropsychic structure that renders many stimuli functionally equivalent and guides consistent forms of adaptive behavior. He classified personal dispositions into three levels. (1) Cardinal traits: a single, all-pervading disposition that dominates a person's whole life (e.g. Machiavellianism in Machiavelli); rare, and not everyone has one. (2) Central traits: the 5–10 broad, major characteristics by which a person is usually described (e.g. honesty, sociability, punctuality) — the traits we would mention in a letter of recommendation. (3) Secondary traits: narrow, situation-specific preferences and attitudes (e.g. food or dress preferences) that are least consistent and least important. Allport also stressed the uniqueness of the individual (idiographic approach) and the concept of functional autonomy of motives.

  17. Write Dollard and Miller's higher mental processes.
    Topic: Learning Theories: Dollard and Miller, Skinner and Bandura

    Dollard and Miller held that, beyond simple learned stimulus–response habits, humans develop higher mental processes that are themselves learned cue-producing responses, largely mediated by language. These include: (1) labeling — attaching words/labels to stimuli and situations, which lets the person respond to the label rather than the whole situation; (2) reasoning — using internal (covert) responses and language to work out solutions before acting; (3) planning — arranging a sequence of responses in advance toward a goal; and related processes such as foresight/anticipation and self-instruction. Because these processes are learned, they can be unlearned and re-taught in therapy, which is the basis of Dollard and Miller's application of learning theory to psychotherapy.

  18. Describe Dollard and Miller's theory of habit and the stimulus–response learning process.
    Topic: Learning Theories: Dollard and Miller, Skinner and Bandura

    Dollard and Miller translated psychoanalytic concepts into Hull's stimulus–response (S–R) learning theory. Learning means forming a habit — a learned connection between a stimulus (cue) and a response. Four elements are necessary for learning: (1) Drive — a strong stimulus (e.g. hunger, pain, sex) that impels the organism to act; (2) Cue — a stimulus that determines when, where and which response to make; (3) Response — the behavior performed; (4) Reward (reinforcement) — an outcome that reduces the drive, which stamps in the S–R connection (the law of effect). Responses are organized in hierarchies: the initial hierarchy is the order in which responses naturally occur, and learning rearranges it into the resultant hierarchy. Personality, for them, is essentially the individual's set of learned habits.

  19. Describe the S–R theory and its therapeutic procedure.
    Topic: Learning Theories: Dollard and Miller, Skinner and Bandura

    The stimulus–response theory of Dollard and Miller explains normal and neurotic behavior alike as learned habits: a cue (stimulus) becomes connected with a response when the response is followed by drive reduction (reward). Neurosis is a learned pattern — e.g. a conflict between strong drives (sex/aggression) and fear learned from parental punishment produces symptoms, which persist because avoidance of the feared cue is itself rewarded by anxiety reduction. The therapeutic procedure therefore applies learning principles: the patient is helped, through free expression and a non-punishing therapeutic relationship, to verbalize and label the feared cues and conflicts, so that extinction can occur — the feared response is repeatedly evoked without punishment and so weakens — while new, healthier cue–response habits are reinforced. Insight here means learning new labels and higher mental processes for old conflicts.

  20. Define imitation.
    Topic: Learning Theories: Dollard and Miller, Skinner and Bandura

    Imitation is learning by copying the behavior of another person (a model) — the learner performs a response similar to the one observed. Dollard and Miller explained it through their three imitation paradigms: same behavior (two people independently make the same response to the same cue), copying behavior (one person guides/corrects another until his response matches), and matched-dependent behavior (the follower matches the leader's behavior in order to obtain the same reward the leader gets). Bandura later showed that imitation/observational learning can occur simply by watching a model, without the observer himself being reinforced (vicarious reinforcement), and depends on attention, retention, reproduction and motivation.

  21. How do Skinner and Bandura explain personality?
    Topic: Learning Theories: Dollard and Miller, Skinner and Bandura

    Skinner: personality is nothing but a collection of learned behavior patterns acquired through operant conditioning — behavior followed by reinforcement (positive or negative) increases, and behavior followed by punishment decreases; schedules of reinforcement shape stable habits, and there is no need to postulate inner traits or structures. Bandura's social learning theory adds cognition and observation: most behavior is learned by observing models (observational learning), and personality emerges from reciprocal determinism — the continuous interaction of behavior, personal/cognitive factors (beliefs, expectations, self-efficacy) and the environment. His concept of self-efficacy — belief in one's ability to perform a task — is central to how personality functions.

  22. Explain Albert Ellis' ABC model of personality.
    Topic: Ellis, Murray, Sullivan and Erikson

    Ellis' A-B-C model is the core of his Rational Emotive Behavior Therapy (REBT). A is the Activating event (e.g. failing an exam); B is the person's Belief system about the event — rational beliefs ('I failed, that is unfortunate, I will work harder') or irrational beliefs ('I must succeed in everything; failing makes me a worthless person'); C is the emotional and behavioral Consequence (sadness and renewed effort, or depression and withdrawal). Ellis' key point is that A does not cause C — B causes C. Therapy extends the model to A-B-C-D-E: D is Disputing/debating the irrational beliefs, and E is the new Effective philosophy that results, replacing self-disturbing beliefs with rational ones.

  23. Write Albert Ellis' assumptions about human beings (human nature).
    Topic: Ellis, Murray, Sullivan and Erikson

    Ellis (1979) listed key assumptions about human nature, including: (1) People have a biological and cultural tendency to think irrationally and to disturb themselves — humans are born with the potential for both rational (straight) thinking and irrational (crooked) thinking, self-helping and self-defeating behavior. (2) Humans are unique in the way they invent and create their irrational beliefs and keep re-indoctrinating themselves with them, so they continue to disturb themselves. (3) People largely condition and disturb themselves through their own beliefs and self-talk, rather than being disturbed by the environment or external events. Ellis also stressed the hopeful side: humans have the capacity, through changing their cognition, emotion and behavior, to understand their limitations and change themselves so as to remain minimally disturbed.

  24. Describe the REBT therapeutic process.
    Topic: Ellis, Murray, Sullivan and Erikson

    In Rational Emotive Behavior Therapy the therapist actively and directly helps the client: (1) identify the activating event (A) and the emotional/behavioral consequences (C); (2) uncover the irrational beliefs (B) — the 'musts' and absolutistic demands (e.g. 'I must be approved by everyone') — that produce the disturbance; (3) dispute (D) those beliefs by detecting them, discriminating rational from irrational beliefs, and debating them logically, empirically and pragmatically ('Where is the evidence that you must succeed?'); (4) arrive at an effective new philosophy (E) of rational beliefs, supported by cognitive homework, emotive techniques (e.g. shame-attacking exercises, rational-emotive imagery) and behavioral assignments, until the client practices and internalizes the new philosophy in daily life.

  25. Explain Murray's concepts of ability and achievement.
    Topic: Ellis, Murray, Sullivan and Erikson

    In Henry Murray's personology, ability and achievement are of central importance for the individual: in almost all his research he appraised subjects in terms of their abilities, achievements and leadership. Murray held that a person needs to be creative and imaginative — to compose and construct — in order to remain psychologically healthy, and he considered creative imagination possibly the strongest feature of personality. Within his need system this is expressed chiefly through the need for Achievement (n Ach): the need to accomplish something difficult, to master, manipulate or organize objects, people or ideas, to overcome obstacles and attain a high standard, and to excel oneself. Ability (what a person can do) and achievement (what he actually accomplishes, the outcome of need interacting with environmental press) together indicate the directionality of the person's striving, which Murray regarded as the key to understanding behavior.

  26. Briefly describe Sullivan's interpersonal theory of personality.
    Topic: Ellis, Murray, Sullivan and Erikson

    Harry Stack Sullivan defined personality as the relatively enduring pattern of recurrent interpersonal situations that characterize a person's life — personality cannot be studied apart from relations with others ('significant others'). Anxiety, transmitted first from mother to infant through empathy, is the central disruptive force, and the self-system develops as a system of security operations to avoid anxiety. Sullivan described modes of experience (prototaxic, parataxic and syntaxic — the highest, consensually validated thinking) and personifications (images of self and others, e.g. good-me, bad-me, not-me). Development passes through epochs — infancy, childhood, juvenile era, preadolescence, early and late adolescence, and adulthood — each defined by new interpersonal needs (tenderness, peer companionship, intimacy). His therapy is a participant-observer exploration of the patient's interpersonal relations.

  27. Describe Erik Erikson's stages of human (psychosocial) development.
    Topic: Ellis, Murray, Sullivan and Erikson

    Erikson proposed eight psychosocial stages across the whole life span; at each stage the person faces a crisis between two opposite outcomes, and successful resolution builds a basic virtue. (1) Infancy: Trust vs Mistrust (hope). (2) Early childhood: Autonomy vs Shame and Doubt (will). (3) Play age: Initiative vs Guilt (purpose). (4) School age: Industry vs Inferiority (competence). (5) Adolescence: Identity vs Role Confusion (fidelity). (6) Young adulthood: Intimacy vs Isolation (love). (7) Middle adulthood: Generativity vs Stagnation (care). (8) Old age: Ego Integrity vs Despair (wisdom). Unlike Freud, Erikson emphasized social/cultural influences and development continuing throughout life, and viewed personality as a function of the outcomes of these crises.

  28. What is meant by a case study?
    Topic: Foundations: Measurement, Heredity and Adjustment

    A case study is an intensive, in-depth investigation of a single individual (sometimes a small group or institution) using all available sources — life history, interviews, records, tests and observations. Freud used the case study method to build his theory from patients such as Little Hans and Anna O. Its strengths are richness of detail, study of rare phenomena and generation of hypotheses about the whole person; its weaknesses are lack of control, subjective interpretation, and results that cannot be generalized to others or easily replicated.

  29. Analyze the role of heredity and environment in personality development.
    Topic: Foundations: Measurement, Heredity and Adjustment

    Personality is the product of heredity interacting with environment, not of either alone. Heredity provides the raw material: genes determine physique, temperament, basic intellectual potential and predispositions (twin studies show identical twins are more similar in traits like extraversion and neuroticism than fraternal twins). Environment — family upbringing, culture, society, schooling, peer relations and life experiences — molds this raw material into a unique personality; the same trait potential develops differently under different rearing and cultural press. Modern personality psychology therefore studies gene–environment interaction: heredity sets limits and tendencies, environment decides how far and in what direction they are expressed.

  30. What is the role of genetics in human behavior?
    Topic: Foundations: Measurement, Heredity and Adjustment

    Genetics (heredity) contributes to behavior in several ways: (1) it determines physical characteristics and the nervous/endocrine systems on which temperament and activity level rest; (2) twin and adoption studies show moderate heritability for major traits (extraversion, neuroticism, conscientiousness), intelligence and even predispositions to some disorders such as schizophrenia; (3) constitutional theories (e.g. Sheldon's somatotypes) linked body build, which is largely inherited, with temperament; (4) genetics sets reaction ranges — the limits within which environment can shape a characteristic. Genetics never acts alone: genes express themselves through environments, so behavior is always a heredity × environment product.

  31. Briefly describe adjustment.
    Topic: Foundations: Measurement, Heredity and Adjustment

    Adjustment is the process by which an individual maintains a harmonious relationship between his needs/desires and the demands of his environment. A well-adjusted person satisfies his needs in socially acceptable ways, copes realistically with frustration and conflict (using mature defenses rather than distorting reality), has a realistic self-concept and stable emotional life. Maladjustment appears when needs are chronically blocked or met through neurotic patterns. Personality theories treat adjustment as a central outcome: Freud saw it as ego strength, Rogers as congruence, Maslow as movement toward self-actualization, and learning theorists as acquiring adaptive habits.

  32. Describe the main methods of personality measurement.
    Topic: Foundations: Measurement, Heredity and Adjustment

    The main methods are: (1) Personality inventories — self-report questionnaires of straightforward statements (e.g. MMPI, with 550 items, 10 clinical scales and validity scales; Cattell's 16 PF; EPPS), which give an elaborate picture of the person's traits and styles. (2) Projective techniques — ambiguous stimuli onto which the person projects his inner world: Rorschach Ink Blot Test, Thematic Apperception Test (cards depicting human figures in ambiguous situations, about which the subject tells stories), Rotter's Incomplete Sentence Blank and House-Tree-Person. (3) Behavioral assessment — direct observation, behavioral coding systems (which focus on the frequency of behavior), rating scales and self-monitoring (keeping track of one's own behavior). (4) Interviews and case history methods. Each method has its own reliability, validity and limitations, so assessment usually combines several.

  33. What criteria are used to evaluate a personality theory?
    Topic: Foundations: Measurement, Heredity and Adjustment

    Six criteria are used: (1) Verifiability — the theory's concepts are clearly defined and can be empirically tested and confirmed or disconfirmed by independent investigators. (2) Heuristic value — the degree to which the theory directly stimulates research. (3) Internal consistency — the theory does not contradict itself; its parts fit together logically. (4) Parsimony — it explains phenomena with the fewest possible concepts (the law of parsimony prefers the most economical explanation). (5) Comprehensiveness — the range and diversity of phenomena (biological, emotional, cognitive, social, cultural) it covers. (6) Functional significance — its usefulness in helping people understand everyday human behavior. No existing theory satisfies all six fully.
