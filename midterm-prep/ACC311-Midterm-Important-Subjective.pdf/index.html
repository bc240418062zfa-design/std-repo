  1. Mr. Kamran is a partner of an audit firm. He bought shares of a company for which his firm is also conducting audit. However, he sold out all these shares within thirty days and immediately informed the company secretary about the whole transaction. Is the company secretary required to take any step under the Companies Law, 1984?
    Topic: Companies Ordinance 1984 — Auditor Appointment, Disqualifications & Books of Accounts • 5 marks marks

    Yes, a step is required. An auditor must be independent of the company he audits, and under the Companies Ordinance, 1984 a person holding any security (shares) of a company is disqualified from being appointed as its auditor (Section 253). A partner of the audit firm who buys shares in an audit client therefore acquires a direct financial interest in the client and, for the period he holds those shares, he and his firm cease to be independent and stand disqualified. The sale of the shares within thirty days does not remove the breach, because the disqualification existed while the shares were held. On being informed, the company secretary is required to bring the transaction to the notice of the Board of Directors and have it recorded, so that the company's statutory record reflects the matter and the position of the auditors can be regularised as required by law. The disclosure to the secretary also does not cure the defect by itself; it is the starting point for the action the company must take.

  2. Why are books of accounts required to be kept by companies? Discuss the types of books of accounts with examples.
    Topic: Companies Ordinance 1984 — Auditor Appointment, Disqualifications & Books of Accounts • 10 marks marks

    Why books of accounts are kept: (1) Legal requirement — Section 230 of the Companies Ordinance, 1984 requires every company to keep proper books of account, and failure to do so is an offence. (2) True and fair view — the financial statements (balance sheet and profit and loss account) are prepared from these books and must give a true and fair view of the state of affairs of the company. (3) Facilitation of audit — the auditor examines the books to obtain evidence and to form his opinion. (4) Management control and decisions — management relies on the books for planning, control and evaluation of performance. (5) Taxation and regulation — tax authorities, the SECP, lenders and other agencies rely on them. (6) Detection of errors and frauds — systematic records help prevent and detect errors and frauds and fix responsibility. (7) Evidence and valuation — they serve as evidence in disputes and for valuing shares and the business. Types of books of accounts (Section 230 requires books in respect of): (i) all sums of money received and expended by the company and the matters to which the receipt and expenditure relate — e.g. the cash book; (ii) all sales and purchases of goods by the company — e.g. the sales day book and the purchases day book; (iii) all assets and liabilities of the company — e.g. the journal, the general ledger and the fixed assets register; and (iv) in the case of companies engaged in production, processing, manufacturing or mining activities, particulars relating to the utilisation of material, labour and other items of cost — e.g. cost records. The books must be kept at the registered office of the company (or at another place approved by the directors, with intimation to the Registrar) and must be preserved for ten years.

  3. What are the disqualifications of an auditor under Section 253 of the Companies Ordinance, 1984?
    Topic: Companies Ordinance 1984 — Auditor Appointment, Disqualifications & Books of Accounts

    Under Section 253 of the Companies Ordinance, 1984, the following persons are disqualified from being appointed as auditor of a company: (1) a body corporate; (2) an officer or employee of the company; (3) a person who is a partner of, or in the employment of, an officer or employee of the company; (4) a person who is indebted to the company; and (5) a person holding any security (shares) of the company. In addition, the auditor of a listed company must be a chartered accountant (or a firm of chartered accountants) within the meaning of the Chartered Accountants Ordinance, 1961, and must hold a satisfactory rating under the Quality Control Review programme of the Institute of Chartered Accountants of Pakistan. The object of these disqualifications is to protect the independence of the auditor.

  4. What is the audit process? What is its importance?
    Topic: The Audit Process, Types of Audit & Audit Risk

    The audit process is the sequence of stages through which an audit is conducted: (1) acceptance of the engagement and issue of the engagement letter; (2) audit planning — obtaining knowledge of the client's business, assessing risk and materiality and preparing the audit plan and programme; (3) evaluation of the internal control system and tests of controls; (4) substantive procedures — vouching and verification of transactions, assets and liabilities; (5) collection and evaluation of audit evidence, documented in the working papers; (6) review of the financial statements; and (7) formation of the opinion and issue of the audit report. Importance: following a proper process ensures that the audit is performed systematically and in accordance with the auditing standards; that sufficient appropriate evidence is obtained to support the opinion; that material misstatements, errors and frauds are likely to be detected; and that the resulting report is reliable for shareholders, lenders, tax authorities and other users. It also evidences the due care exercised by the auditor if his work is later questioned.

  5. What is audit risk assessment?
    Topic: The Audit Process, Types of Audit & Audit Risk

    Audit risk assessment is the process by which the auditor identifies and assesses the risks of material misstatement in the financial statements. The auditor obtains an understanding of the entity and its environment, including its internal control, by performing risk assessment procedures — inquiries of management and others, analytical procedures, and observation and inspection. He then assesses inherent risk (the susceptibility of an assertion to a material misstatement before considering controls) and control risk (the risk that the client's internal control will not prevent, or detect and correct, a material misstatement) at the financial statement level and at the assertion level. The combined assessment determines the nature, timing and extent of the further audit procedures needed to reduce audit risk to an acceptably low level.

  6. Enlist the categories of risk assessment procedures.
    Topic: The Audit Process, Types of Audit & Audit Risk • 3 marks marks

    Risk assessment procedures fall into three categories: (1) Inquiries of management and of others within the entity (and of persons outside the entity where relevant); (2) Analytical procedures — the use of ratio analysis, trend analysis and common-size analysis of financial and non-financial information to identify unusual changes; and (3) Observation and inspection — inspection of documents, records, internal control manuals and management reports, observation of the entity's activities, operations and procedures, and visits to the entity's premises and plant facilities.

  7. What is the risk of material misstatement? Discuss its components and levels.
    Topic: The Audit Process, Types of Audit & Audit Risk

    The risk of material misstatement is the risk that the financial statements are materially misstated prior to the audit. It exists at two levels: (a) the financial statement level — risks that relate pervasively to the financial statements as a whole, such as a weak control environment or management override of controls; and (b) the assertion level — risks relating to classes of transactions, account balances and disclosures. At the assertion level it consists of two components: inherent risk, the susceptibility of an assertion to a misstatement that could be material, before consideration of any related controls; and control risk, the risk that a material misstatement will not be prevented, or detected and corrected, on a timely basis by the entity's internal control. The auditor assesses this risk in order to design further audit procedures: the higher the assessed risk of material misstatement, the lower the detection risk the auditor can accept and the more persuasive the evidence required.

  8. What is the primary difference between an operational audit and a compliance audit?
    Topic: The Audit Process, Types of Audit & Audit Risk

    An operational audit is a review of any part of an entity's operating procedures and methods for the purpose of evaluating efficiency and effectiveness; its primary objective is to make recommendations for future improvements so that the goals of management can be accomplished. A compliance audit determines whether the entity is following specific procedures, rules or regulations set down by some higher authority, such as laws, policies or the terms of a contract. In short, an operational audit focuses on business efficiencies and effectiveness, while a compliance audit focuses on whether laws and other requirements are being followed.

  9. If you are hired by a government bank as an auditor, what would you do to promote the bank's credibility?
    Topic: The Audit Process, Types of Audit & Audit Risk

    As auditor of a government bank I would promote the bank's credibility by: (1) performing the audit completely independently and in accordance with the auditing standards and the requirements of the State Bank of Pakistan; (2) thoroughly evaluating the internal control and compliance systems, including compliance with the Banking Companies Ordinance, 1962 and the SBP prudential regulations; (3) carefully verifying advances, deposits and non-performing loans and reporting irregularities without fear or favour; (4) ensuring that the financial statements give a true and fair view and that all required disclosures are made; (5) issuing an honest and unbiased audit report within the prescribed time; and (6) reporting weaknesses and irregularities to management and those charged with governance through the management letter so that they are corrected. A transparent, standards-based audit strengthens the confidence of depositors, the public and the regulator in the bank.

  10. Define internal control and enlist the procedure of audit.
    Topic: Internal Control, Tests of Controls & Substantive Procedures • 3 marks marks

    Internal control is the process designed, implemented and maintained by those charged with governance, management and other personnel to provide reasonable assurance about the achievement of the entity's objectives with regard to the reliability of financial reporting, the effectiveness and efficiency of operations, and compliance with applicable laws and regulations. It comprises the control environment, the entity's risk assessment process, control activities, information and communication, and monitoring of controls. The procedure of audit (main steps) is: acceptance of the engagement and the engagement letter; audit planning; evaluation of the internal control system; tests of controls; substantive procedures (vouching and verification); collection and evaluation of audit evidence; and finally the audit opinion and report.

  11. What is the purpose of substantive procedures? Also define the term “timing”.
    Topic: Internal Control, Tests of Controls & Substantive Procedures • 5 marks marks

    Substantive procedures are audit procedures designed to detect material misstatements at the assertion level. Their purpose is to obtain evidence about the completeness, accuracy and validity of the accounting data and of the amounts and disclosures in the financial statements. They consist of (a) tests of details of classes of transactions, account balances and disclosures, and (b) substantive analytical procedures. “Timing” refers to when the audit procedures are to be performed, or the period or date to which the audit evidence applies — for example, whether a procedure is performed at an interim date or at the period end. (It is one of the three dimensions of an audit procedure along with its nature and extent.)

  12. What are tests of controls? When is an auditor required to perform them?
    Topic: Internal Control, Tests of Controls & Substantive Procedures

    Tests of controls are audit procedures designed to evaluate the operating effectiveness of controls in preventing, or detecting and correcting, material misstatements at the assertion level. They test the design, implementation and operating effectiveness of a control, using procedures such as inquiry, observation, inspection of documents and re-performance. The auditor performs tests of controls when his assessment of the risk of material misstatement at the assertion level includes an expectation that the controls are operating effectively (so that he plans to rely on them), or when substantive procedures alone do not provide sufficient appropriate audit evidence at the assertion level.

  13. Suppose an auditor makes the assessment that there is a low level of control risk and inherent risk for a client. What would you expect the auditor to do in this situation?
    Topic: Internal Control, Tests of Controls & Substantive Procedures

    If inherent risk and control risk are both assessed as low, the risk of material misstatement is low, so the auditor can accept a relatively higher detection risk. He would therefore perform a relatively small number of substantive procedures (a reduced extent of tests of details). He cannot omit substantive procedures altogether, because some substantive work is always required for material classes of transactions, account balances and disclosures; and tests of controls would still be performed to support the low control risk assessment.

  14. What is an information system? What is its importance in the accounting system?
    Topic: Information Systems & the Accounting System • 5 marks marks

    An information system is the combination of infrastructure (hardware and software), personnel, procedures and data that an entity uses to initiate, record, process and report transactions and events, and to maintain accountability for the related assets, liabilities and equity. The part of it that deals with financial data is the accounting information system, which captures data from source documents, processes it through journals and ledgers, and produces the financial statements and other reports. Its importance in the accounting system is that it: (1) ensures transactions are recorded completely, accurately and on a timely basis; (2) provides a complete audit trail from source document to financial statement; (3) supports internal control through authorisation, segregation of duties and built-in checks; (4) supplies reliable information for management decisions and for statutory financial reporting; and (5) enables the auditor to understand how transactions flow through the system so that the risks of material misstatement can be properly assessed.

  15. What is negligence? Explain the tort of negligence in relation to an auditor.
    Topic: Auditor Liability

    Negligence is the breach of a legal duty of care: it arises when a person fails to exercise the reasonable care, skill and caution that a reasonably competent person in his position would exercise, and loss results from that failure. A tort is a civil wrong, independent of contract, for which the injured party may claim damages; negligence is a tort. An auditor therefore owes a duty of care to his client, and if he performs the audit negligently — for example by failing to carry out the work in accordance with the auditing standards — and the client suffers loss as a result, the auditor is liable in damages both for breach of contract and in tort for negligence. To succeed, the claimant must prove that a duty of care existed, that the duty was breached, and that the loss was caused by the breach. An auditor who has exercised reasonable care and skill in accordance with the auditing standards is not liable merely because an error or fraud remained undetected.

  16. Define tolerable error. What is the difference between sampling and tolerable error?
    Topic: Audit Sampling & Audit Planning • 3 marks marks

    Tolerable error is the maximum error (misstatement) in a population that the auditor is willing to accept and still conclude that the result obtained from the sample has achieved the audit objective. In tests of controls it is expressed as the maximum rate of deviation from a prescribed control procedure that the auditor is prepared to tolerate. Difference: audit sampling is a technique or process — it is the application of audit procedures to less than 100% of the items within a population, in such a way that every sampling unit has a chance of selection, so that the auditor can draw a conclusion about the entire population. Tolerable error, on the other hand, is a benchmark or limit fixed at the planning stage which is used within sampling: the misstatement projected from the sample is compared with tolerable error, and if the projected misstatement exceeds tolerable error, the population cannot be accepted as fairly stated. Sampling is the method; tolerable error is the yardstick against which the results of the method are judged.

  17. Discuss the factors which influence the auditor's judgment regarding sample size for tests of controls.
    Topic: Audit Sampling & Audit Planning • 5 marks marks

    The sample size for tests of controls is influenced by: (1) the auditor's assessment of the risk of material misstatement — the higher the risk, the larger the sample; (2) the degree of reliance the auditor plans to place on the operating effectiveness of the control — greater reliance requires a larger sample; (3) the tolerable rate of deviation — the lower the deviation rate the auditor is willing to accept, the larger the sample; (4) the expected rate of deviation in the population — the higher the deviations expected, the larger the sample; (5) the level of assurance (confidence) the auditor requires from the test — the more assurance required, the larger the sample; and (6) the size of the population — for large populations this has only a negligible effect on sample size.

  18. A company has hired auditors at the year end. The auditors used audit sampling techniques which enabled them to gather audit evidence efficiently. State the objectives the auditors might have defined first to achieve by using sampling techniques, and also briefly explain the further steps the auditors might undertake to complete the sampling process.
    Topic: Audit Sampling & Audit Planning • 5 marks marks

    Objectives: before applying sampling, the auditor first defines the objective of the test, for example (a) to obtain evidence that a particular control is operating effectively throughout the period, or (b) to estimate the amount or rate of misstatement in an account balance or class of transactions, so that a conclusion can be drawn about the whole population from the sample examined. Further steps of the sampling process: (1) define the population and the sampling unit, ensuring the population is complete and appropriate for the objective; (2) determine the sample size, considering tolerable error, the expected error/deviation and the required level of confidence; (3) select the sample by an appropriate method (random, systematic, stratified or haphazard selection) so that it is representative of the population; (4) perform the planned audit procedures on each item selected; (5) evaluate the results — project the misstatements or deviations found to the population and compare them with tolerable error; and (6) draw the conclusion and consider the effect of the results on the rest of the audit and on the audit opinion.

  19. What matters should the auditor consider for developing an overall audit plan for the expected scope and conduct of an audit?
    Topic: Audit Sampling & Audit Planning • 5 marks marks

    In developing the overall audit plan, the auditor should consider: (1) knowledge of the client's business — the industry, the organisation, its products and services, and the accounting policies adopted; (2) an understanding of the accounting system and the internal control system; (3) the assessment of risk and materiality; (4) the nature, timing and extent of the audit procedures to be performed; (5) the coordination, direction, supervision and review of the work, including the involvement of other auditors and experts and work at branches and subsidiaries; (6) staffing, time budgeting and reporting deadlines; and (7) other matters such as the terms of the engagement, statutory requirements, going-concern considerations and related parties.

  20. What steps should an auditor take in order to ensure that the reserves are properly classified and described in the accounts in accordance with the Companies Ordinance, 1984?
    Topic: Internal Control over Specific Cycles & Accounts • 3 marks marks

    The auditor should take the following steps: (1) examine the memorandum and articles of association and the relevant provisions of the Companies Ordinance, 1984 governing the creation and use of each reserve (for example the share premium account and the capital redemption reserve); (2) verify the resolutions of the directors and, where required, of the shareholders authorising transfers to and from reserves; (3) agree the opening balances of reserves with the previous year's audited financial statements and check that all movements during the year are properly recorded and authorised; (4) ensure that capital reserves and revenue reserves are separately and correctly classified, and that amounts which are not available for distribution as dividend are not described as free reserves; and (5) check that the reserves are presented and described under the proper headings in the balance sheet as required by the Fourth Schedule to the Ordinance, and that any utilisation of a reserve during the year is properly disclosed.

  21. Enlist the typical control procedures at key stages of the sales cycle.
    Topic: Internal Control over Specific Cycles & Accounts • 3 marks marks

    The typical control procedures at the key stages of the sales cycle are: (1) Orders — orders are accepted only from approved customers after a credit check and authorisation, and are recorded on pre-numbered order forms. (2) Dispatch — goods are dispatched only against authorised orders; dispatch notes/gate passes are prepared and matched with the orders. (3) Invoicing and credit notes — invoices are raised from dispatch notes using authorised price lists, are arithmetically checked and sequentially numbered; credit notes are authorised by a responsible official. (4) Returns inwards — goods returned are inspected, recorded in a goods inwards register and matched with the credit notes issued. (5) Receivables — the sales ledger control account is reconciled with the individual customer balances, statements are sent to customers regularly, and the ageing of receivables is reviewed. (6) Bad debts — write-offs are authorised by a responsible official, and subsequent recoveries are monitored and recorded.

  22. Suppose that your supervisor has asked you to carry out an internal control review of the inventory system of a manufacturing company. What internal control procedures would you examine in respect of arithmetical accuracy?
    Topic: Internal Control over Specific Cycles & Accounts • 5 marks marks

    In reviewing arithmetical accuracy in the inventory system, I would examine whether: (1) the inventory records (bin cards and stores ledger) are regularly added/cast accurately and agreed with one another; (2) receipts, issues and balances are correctly computed and priced, with a consistent costing method (such as FIFO or weighted average) being applied; (3) the extensions on stock sheets (quantity × price) are independently checked by a second person; (4) periodic reconciliations are carried out between the physical inventory counts and the book records, and differences are investigated; (5) all adjustments arising from discrepancies are authorised by a responsible official and properly recorded; and (6) the calculations of work-in-progress quantities and values, including overhead absorption, are arithmetically verified.

  23. What are the essential features of auditing? Distinguish between accounting and auditing.
    Topic: Introduction, Types of Audit & the Audit Process • 3 marks marks

    Essential features of auditing: (1) it is the examination of the financial statements of an entity; (2) it is carried out by an independent person (the auditor); (3) it involves the collection and evaluation of evidence; and (4) its end result is an opinion (report) that assists the users of the financial statements. The preparation of the financial statements is NOT a feature of auditing — that is the responsibility of management. Accounting versus auditing: accounting is the process of recording, classifying and summarising business transactions and of preparing the financial statements; it is performed by the entity's own accountants on a continuous basis. Auditing begins where accounting ends: it is the critical and independent examination of the financial statements already prepared, to determine whether they give a true and fair view. In short, accounting constructs the accounts; auditing scrutinises and reports on them.

  24. Distinguish between internal audit and external audit.
    Topic: Introduction, Types of Audit & the Audit Process • 5 marks marks

    Internal audit and external audit differ in the following respects: (1) Appointment — internal auditors are appointed by the management/board of the company; external auditors are appointed by the shareholders (members) in the annual general meeting. (2) Status — an internal auditor is an employee of the company and needs no licence to practise; an external auditor is an independent professional, who for a listed company must be a chartered accountant. (3) Scope — the scope of internal audit is determined by management and covers operational as well as financial matters; the scope of external audit is determined by law and auditing standards and is primarily in respect of the financial statements. (4) Objective — internal audit aims to check that the accounting system and internal controls are operating efficiently and to help management improve them; external audit aims to express an opinion on whether the financial statements give a true and fair view. (5) Reporting — the internal auditor reports to management/the audit committee; the external auditor reports to the shareholders. (6) Compulsion — an external (statutory) audit is compulsory for companies; an internal audit department is set up voluntarily (or as required by the code of corporate governance for listed companies).

  25. How are the auditors of a company appointed under the Companies Ordinance, 1984? What happens if the company fails to appoint auditors?
    Topic: Auditor Appointment, Rights, Duties & Remuneration • 5 marks marks

    Appointment: (1) The first auditors of a company are appointed by the directors and hold office until the conclusion of the first annual general meeting; if the directors fail to appoint them, the members may appoint the first auditors in a general meeting. (2) Subsequent auditors are appointed by the members at each annual general meeting and hold office from the conclusion of that meeting until the conclusion of the next annual general meeting. (3) A retiring auditor is normally re-appointed unless he is disqualified, has given notice in writing of his unwillingness to be re-appointed, or the members decide otherwise. (4) The company must obtain the written consent of the proposed auditor before appointment and must intimate the appointment to the Registrar within the prescribed period (fourteen days). If the company fails to appoint auditors — for example, if neither the directors nor the members appoint the first auditors within four months of incorporation, or no auditor is appointed at an annual general meeting — the Commission (SECP) is empowered to appoint the auditors of the company.

  26. Discuss the rights and duties of an auditor of a company.
    Topic: Auditor Appointment, Rights, Duties & Remuneration • 5 marks marks

    Rights of an auditor: (1) right of access at all times to the books, accounts and vouchers of the company; (2) right to obtain from the officers of the company such information and explanations as he considers necessary for the performance of his duties; (3) right to receive notices of, and to attend, general meetings and to be heard at them on matters concerning him as auditor; (4) right to visit branch offices and have access to their books; (5) right to sign the audit report; (6) right to seek legal and technical advice; and (7) right to be indemnified out of the assets of the company against liabilities incurred in defending proceedings in which he is acquitted. Duties of an auditor: (1) to make a report to the members on the accounts examined by him and on the balance sheet and profit and loss account; (2) to state in the report whether, in his opinion, the financial statements give a true and fair view and comply with the Companies Ordinance, 1984; (3) to inquire into the matters specified in the Ordinance (for example, whether loans and advances are properly secured, whether transactions are prejudicial to the company's interests, and whether shares allotted for cash have actually been paid for in cash); (4) to exercise reasonable care, skill and caution and to comply with the auditing standards; and (5) to report fraud or irregularities to the Commission in the cases prescribed by law.

  27. How is the remuneration of an auditor fixed, and how can an auditor be removed before the expiry of his term?
    Topic: Auditor Appointment, Rights, Duties & Remuneration • 3 marks marks

    Remuneration: the remuneration of the auditor is fixed by the authority that appoints him — where the members appoint the auditor at the annual general meeting, the members fix the remuneration; the remuneration of the first auditors appointed by the directors is fixed by the directors; and where the Commission appoints the auditor, the Commission fixes it. The company secretary has no authority to fix the auditor's remuneration. Removal: an auditor appointed at an annual general meeting may be removed before the expiry of his term only by the members in a general meeting, and that too with the prior approval of the Commission. The auditor concerned must be given a reasonable opportunity of being heard, and he has the right to make written representations to the members.

  28. Discuss the liabilities of an auditor under the Companies Ordinance, 1984.
    Topic: Auditor Liability, Removal & Professional Misconduct • 5 marks marks

    An auditor may face the following liabilities: (1) Civil liability for negligence — if the auditor fails to exercise reasonable care and skill and the client suffers loss, he is liable in damages for breach of contract and in tort. (2) Liability for misfeasance — if the auditor has been guilty of misfeasance (breach of trust or duty) in relation to the company, the court may, on the application of the company, a creditor or a contributory, compel him to repay or restore money or property or to contribute to the company's assets by way of compensation. (3) Criminal liability — an auditor is criminally liable, for example, for making a false statement in a report, balance sheet or prospectus, for destroying, mutilating or falsifying documents relating to the company, or for failing to perform duties imposed by the Ordinance (such as presenting his report after performing the audit in accordance with the auditing standards). (4) Liability to third parties — generally an auditor is not liable to third parties with whom he has no contract, except in limited circumstances where he knew his report would be relied upon by an identified third party. In addition, a chartered accountant auditor may face disciplinary proceedings for professional misconduct before the Institute of Chartered Accountants of Pakistan.

  29. What is an audit engagement letter? State its purposes and main contents.
    Topic: Regulation, Ethics & Professional Bodies • 3 marks marks

    An audit engagement letter is the written agreement between the auditor and the client that records the terms of the audit engagement; it is prepared by the auditor and sent to the client before the audit begins. Purposes: it defines the auditor's responsibilities clearly; it minimises misunderstandings between the client and the auditor regarding the scope of the audit; and it confirms the auditor's acceptance of the appointment and the terms agreed. (It is not meant to educate the client or to help the auditor in preparing the audit report.) Main contents: the objective and scope of the audit; the responsibilities of the auditor; the responsibilities of management for the preparation of the financial statements and for internal control; identification of the applicable financial reporting framework; reference to the expected form and content of the auditor's report; the basis of fee computation and billing arrangements; and, where relevant, arrangements concerning the involvement of internal auditors, other auditors and experts.

  30. Discuss the role of ICAP and the SECP in the regulation of the audit profession in Pakistan.
    Topic: Regulation, Ethics & Professional Bodies • 3 marks marks

    The audit profession in Pakistan is regulated by its own professional body as well as by outside bodies. The Institute of Chartered Accountants of Pakistan (ICAP) is an autonomous body established under the Chartered Accountants Ordinance, 1961; its affairs are run by a council elected by its members (chartered accountants), and it is a member of the International Federation of Accountants (IFAC). ICAP formulates and issues the auditing standards applicable in Pakistan (adopting the International Standards on Auditing), prescribes the code of ethics, conducts the examinations and training of chartered accountants, runs the Quality Control Review programme for audit firms, and takes disciplinary action against members for professional misconduct. The Securities and Exchange Commission of Pakistan (SECP/the Commission), as the regulator of companies under the Companies Ordinance, 1984, also becomes involved: it prescribes the qualifications for appointment as auditor of listed and public interest companies, may appoint auditors where a company fails to do so, and enforces compliance with the Ordinance. The profession is therefore neither wholly unregulated nor regulated only by the government.

  31. What are the different types of company meetings? When must the first annual general meeting and the first annual accounts be presented?
    Topic: Companies Ordinance, Meetings & Books of Accounts • 3 marks marks

    Company meetings are of three broad types: (1) meetings of the members (shareholders) — these are called general meetings, and comprise the annual general meeting (AGM) and extraordinary general meetings; (2) meetings of the directors — board meetings; and (3) meetings of management and employees, which are internal working meetings. Only a general meeting is a “meeting of members”. The first annual general meeting of a company must be held within eighteen months of its incorporation; thereafter an AGM must be held in every calendar year, within four months of the close of the financial year. The first auditors hold office until the conclusion of the first AGM. Similarly, a company that has just been converted from a private limited company into a public limited company must present its first annual accounts before an annual general meeting within eighteen months.

  32. What are the components of an internal control system? Explain each briefly.
    Topic: Internal Control Systems & Their Evaluation • 5 marks marks

    An internal control system has five components: (1) Control environment — the overall attitude, awareness and actions of management and those charged with governance regarding internal control, including integrity, ethical values, organisational structure and assignment of authority and responsibility; it is the foundation of all other components. (2) The entity's risk assessment process — the process by which management identifies business risks relevant to the preparation of financial statements and decides how to manage them. (3) Control activities — the policies and procedures that help ensure that management directives are carried out, such as authorisation, segregation of duties, performance reviews, information processing controls and physical controls. (4) Information and communication — the information system (including the accounting system) that captures and exchanges the information needed to conduct, manage and control operations, and the communication of roles and responsibilities within the entity and with external parties. (5) Monitoring of controls — the process of assessing the quality of internal control performance over time, through ongoing monitoring activities, separate evaluations (such as internal audit) or a combination of both.

  33. Explain the SOAP MAPS categories of internal control, giving one application of each.
    Topic: Internal Control Systems & Their Evaluation • 5 marks marks

    The main categories of internal control are conveniently remembered as SOAP MAPS: (1) Segregation of duties — incompatible duties (such as handling cash and recording it) are divided among different persons, e.g. the cashier of a bank must not also maintain the books of account. (2) Organisational controls — a defined organisational structure with clear lines of authority and responsibility for decisions and transactions. (3) Authorisation and approval — transactions are executed only with proper authority, e.g. authorisation of purchases, cash and bank payments, sales on credit and bad debt write-offs. (4) Personnel controls — employment of competent and trustworthy personnel, with proper training and supervision. (5) Management and monitoring controls — supervision and review by management, including internal audit, so that management can effectively control the business operations. (6) Arithmetic and accounting controls — the use of standard accounting procedures such as control accounts, reconciliation procedures and arithmetic checks on accounting records, to ensure the completeness and accuracy of the financial records. (7) Physical controls — safeguarding of assets and records through locks, safes, restricted access and periodic physical verification. (8) Supervision — a supervisor or manager reviews and checks the work of subordinates to check the quality of work.

  34. What methods are used to record and evaluate an internal control system? Discuss the advantages and disadvantages of flowcharts.
    Topic: Internal Control Systems & Their Evaluation • 5 marks marks

    The methods commonly used to record and evaluate an internal control system are: (1) Narrative notes — a written description of the system and the procedures followed. (2) Internal Control Questionnaires (ICQs) — a standard list of questions designed so that a “no” answer indicates a control weakness. (3) Internal Control Evaluation Checklists (ICECs) — checklists used to evaluate whether the controls actually address the control objectives. (4) Flowcharts — a diagrammatical representation of the accounting system showing the flow of documents and the controls at each stage; the design of the controls can also be tested through ICQs and walk-through tests. Advantages of flowcharts: they present the system clearly and concisely; weaknesses and missing controls are easy to spot; and they force the auditor to understand how the company controls its operations. Disadvantages of flowcharts: preparing them is a tedious and time-consuming procedure, as flowchart symbols cannot simply be typed; any modification in the process may require the flowchart to be drawn again; and where the logic of the system is very complex, the flowchart may become difficult to comprehend.

  35. What are the limitations of an internal control system?
    Topic: Internal Control Systems & Their Evaluation • 3 marks marks

    Internal control provides only reasonable, not absolute, assurance, because of the following limitations: (1) Human error — controls may fail through simple mistakes, carelessness or misunderstanding of instructions. (2) Collusion — two or more employees acting together can circumvent controls such as segregation of duties. (3) Management override — management is in a position to override the controls it has itself established. (4) Cost–benefit consideration — the cost of a control should not exceed the benefit expected from it, so some risks are consciously left uncontrolled. (5) Unusual transactions — controls are generally designed for routine transactions and may not operate for non-routine or unusual ones. (6) Changes in conditions — a control that was adequate may become inadequate as conditions change. Internal control is also affected by the people working in the organisation, on whom its effectiveness ultimately depends.

  36. What are financial statement assertions? Explain the main assertions used by an auditor.
    Topic: Audit Evidence, Assertions & Audit Procedures • 5 marks marks

    Assertions are the representations made by management, explicitly or otherwise, in the financial statements, which the auditor uses to consider the different types of potential misstatements that may occur. The main assertions are: (1) Occurrence — transactions and events that have been recorded have actually occurred and pertain to the entity (i.e. there is no false transaction recorded). (2) Completeness — all transactions, events, assets, liabilities and disclosures that should have been recorded have been recorded; there are no unrecorded items. (3) Accuracy — amounts and other data relating to recorded transactions have been recorded appropriately. (4) Cut-off — transactions have been recorded in the correct accounting period. (5) Classification — transactions have been recorded in the proper accounts. For account balances, the corresponding assertions are: Existence (assets and liabilities exist at the given date), Rights and obligations (the entity holds the rights to its assets and its liabilities are its own obligations), Completeness, and Valuation and allocation (assets, liabilities and equity interests are included at appropriate amounts, with valuation adjustments properly recorded).

  37. Enlist and briefly explain the types of audit procedures used to obtain audit evidence.
    Topic: Audit Evidence, Assertions & Audit Procedures • 5 marks marks

    The auditor obtains evidence through the following procedures: (1) Inspection — examining records, documents or tangible assets. (2) Observation — looking at a process or procedure being performed by others, in order to determine the manner of its performance (for example, observing the counting of inventory). (3) Inquiry — seeking relevant information from knowledgeable persons inside or outside the enterprise, formally or informally; inquiry alone does not provide sufficient evidence and is corroborated by other procedures. (4) Confirmation — obtaining a direct written response from a third party, e.g. direct bank confirmation of balances. (5) Recalculation — checking the mathematical accuracy of documents or records. (6) Re-performance — the auditor's independent execution of procedures or controls that were originally performed by the entity. (7) Analytical procedures — evaluating financial information through analysis of plausible relationships among financial and non-financial data (ratio analysis, trend analysis, common-size analysis), which are used in planning (risk assessment), as substantive procedures and at the final review stage.

  38. What is meant by sufficient appropriate audit evidence? State the factors affecting the reliability of audit evidence.
    Topic: Audit Evidence, Assertions & Audit Procedures • 3 marks marks

    Audit evidence is sufficient when the quantity of evidence obtained is enough, and appropriate when its quality — its relevance and reliability — is adequate for the auditor to draw reasonable conclusions on which to base his opinion. Sufficiency and appropriateness are interrelated: the quantity needed is affected by the quality obtained. Reliability of evidence is influenced by its source and nature: (1) evidence obtained from independent sources outside the entity is more reliable than evidence obtained from within the entity; (2) evidence generated internally is more reliable when the related internal controls are effective; (3) evidence obtained directly by the auditor (e.g. by observation or recalculation) is more reliable than evidence obtained indirectly or by inference; (4) documentary evidence is more reliable than oral evidence; and (5) original documents are more reliable than copies. Internal sources of audit evidence include the accounting systems, records and documents, non-financial data and records, and management representations and discussions; an associated company of the entity is not an internal source.

  39. Explain the audit risk model and the relationship between its components.
    Topic: Audit Risk, Materiality & Planning • 5 marks marks

    Audit risk is the risk that the auditor expresses an inappropriate audit opinion when the financial statements are materially misstated. It is a function of the risk of material misstatement and detection risk, expressed in the audit risk model: Audit Risk = Inherent Risk × Control Risk × Detection Risk. (1) Inherent risk is the susceptibility of an account balance or class of transactions to a misstatement that could be material, individually or when aggregated with other misstatements, irrespective of the effects of internal controls. (2) Control risk is the risk that a material misstatement will not be prevented, or detected and corrected on a timely basis, by the entity's internal control system. Inherent risk and control risk together make up the risk of material misstatement, which exists independently of the audit. (3) Detection risk is the risk that the auditor's procedures will fail to detect a material misstatement. Relationship: the assessed level of inherent and control risk determines the acceptable level of detection risk — the relationship is inverse. If inherent and control risks are high, detection risk must be kept low, which the auditor achieves by performing more extensive substantive procedures; if they are low, a higher detection risk can be accepted and fewer substantive procedures are needed. The auditor cannot change inherent and control risks; he can only assess them and adjust detection risk through the nature, timing and extent of his procedures.

  40. What is materiality in auditing? How does the auditor use it in planning an audit?
    Topic: Audit Risk, Materiality & Planning • 3 marks marks

    Information is material if its omission or misstatement could influence the economic decisions of users taken on the basis of the financial statements. Materiality depends on the size of the item or error judged in the particular circumstances of its omission or misstatement; it provides a threshold or cut-off point rather than a qualitative characteristic. In planning the audit, the auditor makes a preliminary assessment of materiality at the financial statement level (and for particular account balances), because it helps him decide questions such as what items to investigate and whether to use sampling, and it affects the nature, timing and extent of audit procedures. Materiality and audit risk are inversely related: the higher the materiality level, the lower the audit risk, and vice versa. The assessment is reconsidered during the audit and at the evaluation stage, when the auditor judges whether the aggregate of uncorrected misstatements is material to the financial statements.

  41. What are the advantages of audit sampling? Enlist the methods available to an auditor for selecting sample items.
    Topic: Audit Sampling • 3 marks marks

    Advantages of audit sampling: (1) it is economical — examining every item would be too costly; (2) it is time-saving — conclusions can be reached within the reporting deadline; (3) it is more practical — a properly selected sample gives the auditor a reasonable basis for a conclusion about the whole population; and (4) it avoids damage to records/items that destructive 100% testing might cause. The auditor must select the sample so that all items in the population have an opportunity (a chance) of being selected. Sample selection methods: (1) simple random selection — every item has an equal chance of selection, using random number tables or computer generators; (2) systematic selection — items are selected at a fixed interval from a random start; (3) stratified sampling — the population is divided into sub-populations (strata) with homogeneous characteristics and a sample is selected from each stratum; (4) block selection — a block of contiguous items is selected; and (5) haphazard selection — items are selected without any structured technique but avoiding bias. Statistical sampling uses probability theory to measure sampling risk, whereas judgmental sampling relies on the auditor's judgment.

  42. State any five limitations of tests of control.
    Topic: Internal Control, Tests of Controls & Substantive Procedures • 5 marks marks

    Tests of control suffer from the following limitations: 1. Sampling risk: Tests of control are applied on a sample basis, so the sample may not be representative of the whole population and the auditor may reach a wrong conclusion about the effectiveness of a control. 2. Point-in-time evidence: A test shows that a control operated effectively at the time of testing; it does not guarantee that the control operated effectively throughout the whole period under audit. 3. Collusion: Controls can be circumvented by collusion between two or more employees, so a test may show apparent compliance even where a fraud has been committed. 4. Management override: Management can override the prescribed controls, which limits the reliance that the auditor can place on the results of tests of control. 5. Documentary evidence may be unreliable: The evidence examined may consist of documents that have been forged or altered, and employees may behave differently while they are being observed by the auditor.

  43. What is meant by the duty of care of an auditor? What may happen if an auditor does not exercise the duty of care?
    Topic: Auditor Liability • 5 marks marks

    The duty of care means that an auditor must carry out the audit with the reasonable care and skill that can be expected from a reasonably competent auditor in similar circumstances. The auditor must plan and perform the audit properly, follow the auditing standards, obtain sufficient appropriate audit evidence, apply professional judgment and maintain professional skepticism throughout the audit. If an auditor does not exercise the duty of care, he is guilty of negligence. As a result, he can be held liable to compensate the client (and in some cases third parties) for the loss suffered because of his negligence. He may also face disciplinary proceedings for professional misconduct by the Institute of Chartered Accountants of Pakistan (ICAP), suffer loss of reputation, and in cases of gross negligence or fraud he may even face criminal liability.
