  1. Define “virus” — what is meant by the word virus?
    Topic: Introduction, Characteristics & Basic Definitions • 2 marks marks

    Virus is a Latin word which means poison or venom. Viruses are noncellular particles made up of genetic material and proteins that can invade living cells.

  2. What are viruses?
    Topic: Introduction, Characteristics & Basic Definitions

    Viruses are complicated assemblies of molecules, including proteins, nucleic acids, lipids, and carbohydrates. Viruses can only replicate themselves by infecting a host cell and therefore cannot reproduce on their own.

  3. Write down the general characteristics / properties of viruses. (3–5 marks)
    Topic: Introduction, Characteristics & Basic Definitions

    - A virus is a microscopic infectious agent that can only replicate inside the living cells of a host organism. - Viruses have a simple structure, consisting of genetic material (DNA or RNA) enclosed in a protein coat called a capsid. Some viruses also have an outer envelope made up of lipids. - Viruses are considered to be non-living as they lack the ability to carry out metabolic functions and reproduce on their own; they are non-cellular. - Viruses are highly specific, targeting specific host cells based on the interaction between viral proteins and host receptors. - Viruses can infect a wide range of organisms, including plants, animals, and even bacteria (known as bacteriophages). - Viral infections can cause a wide range of diseases in their hosts, from mild illnesses to severe infections. - Viruses can be transmitted through various routes such as respiratory droplets, bodily fluids, contaminated surfaces, or through insect vectors. - Some viruses may have spikes to help attach to the host cell. - Vaccines and antiviral medicines have been developed to prevent and treat viral infections, but their use varies depending on the specific virus.

  13. Differentiate between capsid and envelope.
    Topic: Virus Structure — Capsid, Envelope & Symmetry

    Capsid: The capsid (coat) protein is the basic unit of structure. Virus particles contain the viral genome packaged in a protein coat called the capsid. Its functions are to: protect viral nucleic acid; interact specifically with the viral nucleic acid for packaging; interact with the vector for specific transmission; interact with host receptors for entry into the cell; allow for release of nucleic acid upon entry into a new cell; and assist in processes of viral and/or host gene regulation. Envelope: In some viruses, the capsid is covered by an envelope — a lipid bilayer, derived from the host cell membranes, that contains viral proteins (lipids, proteins and carbohydrates). Spikes are projections from the surface of the envelope by which viruses attach to host cells (e.g. the ability of influenza virus to clump red blood cells is associated with spikes). Viruses whose capsids are not covered by an envelope are known as non-enveloped viruses. The envelope is mostly host-specific, derived from the host cell membranes, and helps the virus to enter the host.

  14. Compare the symmetrical properties of the kinds of viral symmetry.
    Topic: Virus Structure — Capsid, Envelope & Symmetry • 5 marks marks

    Symmetry refers to the way in which capsomere units are arranged in the viral capsid. Two kinds of symmetry are known in viruses, which relate to the two primary shapes, i.e. rod and spherical shape of virus. Rod-shaped viruses have helical symmetry and spherical-shaped viruses have icosahedral symmetry.

  15. What is the Baltimore classification? Give its classes.
    Topic: Classification, Host Range, Tropism & Origin • 5 marks marks

    The Baltimore classification system, developed in the 1970s (1971) by Nobel laureate David Baltimore, categorizes viruses based on the type of nucleic acid genome and the replication strategy of the virus. Its seven classes are: - Class I: double-stranded DNA viruses (e.g. Adeno, Herpes) - Class II: single-stranded DNA viruses (e.g. Parvo) - Class III: double-stranded RNA viruses (e.g. Reo) - Class IV: positive-sense single-stranded RNA viruses (e.g. Polio, Picorna) - Class V: negative-sense single-stranded RNA viruses (e.g. Ortho) - Class VI: single-stranded RNA viruses that reverse transcribe (Retro) - Class VII: double-stranded DNA viruses that reverse transcribe (Hepadna)

  16. What is the host range of a virus? Discuss it.
    Topic: Classification, Host Range, Tropism & Origin • 5 marks marks

    - Host range refers to the spectrum of host cells that the virus can infect — the spectrum of host cells in which a virus can multiply. - Most viruses infect only specific types of cells in one host species. - Host range is determined by the specific attachment site on the host cell's surface and the availability of host cellular factors. - Most viruses are host specific and bind to specific receptors on cells; in animal viruses, receptors on the plasma membrane form hydrogen bonds with the viral surface. - Viruses that infect bacteria are called bacteriophages, or phages. - Viral host range includes: plants, vertebrates, invertebrates, protists, bacteria and fungi.

  17. Define viral tropism of cells and tissues, with an example.
    Topic: Classification, Host Range, Tropism & Origin

    In a general sense, viral tropism refers to the ability of a given virus to productively infect a particular cell (cellular tropism), tissue (tissue tropism) or host species (host tropism). For example, if a particular virus can productively infect rabbits but no other vertebrate hosts such as humans, that virus would be said to have a host tropism limited to rabbits. Similarly, if the same virus could productively infect rabbit macrophages but not any other rabbit cells, that virus would be said to have a cellular tropism limited to macrophages.

  18. What are the theories of the origin / evolution of viruses? (3–5 marks)
    Topic: Classification, Host Range, Tropism & Origin

    - First theory: They may have originated and evolved with the other life forms. - Second theory: They may have arisen from segments of the cellular nucleic acids which acquired the ability to replicate at the expense of the host cell. - Third theory (Regressive theory): Viruses arose from free-living organisms which gradually lost genetic information, until they became totally dependent on the biosynthetic pathways of their host cells.

  19. Write the steps of the animal virus infection (life) cycle.
    Topic: Viral Replication & Effects on the Host Cell • 5 marks marks

    Viral replication involves six steps: attachment, penetration, uncoating, replication (gene expression), assembly and release. - During attachment and penetration, the virus attaches itself to a host cell and injects its genetic material into it. - During uncoating, replication, and assembly, the viral DNA or RNA incorporates itself into the host cell's genetic material and induces it to replicate the viral genome. - During release, the newly created viruses are released from the host cell, either by causing the cell to break apart, waiting for the cell to die, or by budding off through the cell membrane.

  20. Describe the mechanism of viral attack (entry) on the host cell.
    Topic: Viral Replication & Effects on the Host Cell • 5 marks marks

    Viral entry pathways: A virus can fuse either directly with the plasma membrane (receptor-mediated fusion) or after being swallowed into an endosome. Which of these routes is followed depends on the type of virus. In fusion with the plasma membrane, the virus binds to a protein in the cell membrane; the function of this cellular protein (a receptor for the virus) is perverted to induce a conformational change in the viral fusion protein, leading to fusion. For a virus that is triggered within an endosome, the endosome's acidic conditions induce fusion. In either case, the viral genome passes through a fusion pore into the cytosol, and infection is initiated.

  21. Describe uncoating of viruses.
    Topic: Viral Replication & Effects on the Host Cell • 3 marks marks

    During virus entry, capsid shells are primed and disassembled in a complex, tightly regulated, multi-step process termed uncoating. - Uncoating means removal of the capsid from the virus. - It exposes the viral genome in the host cell (in the cytoplasm, with the help of cellular enzymes); the protein coat around the genome gets disintegrated. - It is a vital, important step: it makes the genome ready to take over the host machinery and activates the cascade of steps required for viral replication.

  22. What are the effects of an attack of viruses on host cells? (2–3 marks)
    Topic: Viral Replication & Effects on the Host Cell

    When viruses attack host cells, they replicate inside the host to produce new viral particles and use the host's metabolism, which can damage or destroy the host cell. The process can trigger host defence mechanisms that may cause tissue damage, contributing to the symptoms of the disease. Consequently, the damage can cause pathological effects, which could result in inflammation, tissue damage, cell death, and organ dysfunction, having grave implications for the host's health. Possible effects of the viral genome on the host cell are: - Genetic alterations - Physical damage - Oncogenesis

  23. What is the role of proteins and glycoproteins in the life cycle of a virus?
    Topic: Viral Replication & Effects on the Host Cell • 5 marks marks

    Proteins and glycoproteins have essential roles in the life cycle of viruses. They facilitate the attachment and recognition of host cells, assist in the penetration of the viral genome into the host cell, and interact with the host cell machinery to initiate the replication and transcription of viral genomes. Furthermore, the assembly of new viral particles, genome packaging, and release of new viral particles is also reliant on viral proteins and glycoproteins. Proteins and glycoproteins are involved at every stage of the viral life cycle, playing crucial roles in the process.

  24. What is a bacteriophage? Explain the lytic and lysogenic cycles.
    Topic: Bacteriophages • 3 marks marks

    A bacteriophage, also known informally as a phage, is a virus that infects and replicates within bacteria and archaea. Lytic cycle: The phages take over the machinery of the cell to make phage components. They then destroy, or lyse, the cell, releasing new phage particles. Lysogenic cycle: The phage infects a bacterium and inserts its DNA into the bacterial chromosome, allowing the phage DNA (now called a prophage) to be copied and passed on along with the cell's own DNA; lysogenic phages incorporate their nucleic acids into the chromosome of the host cell and replicate with it as a unit without destroying the cell.

  27. Describe the replication of T-phages.
    Topic: Bacteriophages

    Replication stages: 1. Attachment, 2. Penetration, 3. Uncoating, 4. Biosynthesis, 5. Release. Attachment: To specific receptors — teichoic acid, lipids, proteins, flagella — on specific bacterial hosts. Penetration: The capsid is left outside. Biosynthesis: Bacterial ribosomes translate viral proteins; proteins modify bacterial RNA polymerase; proteins for new virions are made. Assembly: In T4, helper proteins are required; base plates are assembled first, tails are built afterwards, heads are constructed separately; the whole process takes about 15 minutes. Release: By lysis (tailed phages, using endolysin), extrusion (enveloped), budding, or secretion.

  29. What is viral recombination? Also define intramolecular recombination.
    Topic: Viral Genetics — Mutation, Recombination, Reversion & Suppression • 3 marks marks

    Viral recombination: The exchange or transfer of genetic material between different but closely related viruses infecting the same cell. It can also occur between virus and host; recombinants contain new genetic information. Intramolecular recombination: Usually in DNA viruses — dissociation and re-establishment of covalent bonds within nucleic acids, by a template-switching mechanism.

  30. What is complementation? Also write its types.
    Topic: Viral Genetics — Mutation, Recombination, Reversion & Suppression

    Complementation is the assignment of mutants to functional groups resulting in production of one or both parent viruses. One of the viruses provides a functional gene product, which allows functional analysis of unknown mutations. Types of complementation: - Allelic (intragenic) complementation: different mutants have defects in the same protein. - Non-allelic (intergenic) complementation: results from mutants with defects in different genes.

  31. What is suppression of a viral genome? How is viral suppression done / define informational suppression.
    Topic: Viral Genetics — Mutation, Recombination, Reversion & Suppression

    Suppression of a viral genome is the inhibition of a mutant phenotype by a second suppressor mutation, which may be either in the viral genome or in the host cell. Informational suppression: The mechanism is different from the suppression of chain-terminating amber mutations by host-encoded suppressor tRNAs, which is also referred to as informational suppression.

  32. Write down the ways of reversion.
    Topic: Viral Genetics — Mutation, Recombination, Reversion & Suppression

    Mutant viruses can revert by three ways: - True reversion: Back mutation of the original mutation; gives the wild-type genotype or phenotype. - Intragenic reversion: A compensatory mutation, which occurs in the same originally mutated gene, restoring it. - Extragenic reversion: A suppressor mutation in a different gene — a virus gene or a host gene.

  33. Define genetic reactivation.
    Topic: Viral Genetics — Mutation, Recombination, Reversion & Suppression

  34. Define the following terms: mutation, pseudotype, recombinant, codon, anti-codon, ribosomes, plasmid, transcription, translation.
    Topic: Viral Genetics — Mutation, Recombination, Reversion & Suppression

    - Mutation: A permanent inheritable change in a single gene that results in the existence of two or more alleles occurring at the same locus. - Pseudotype: The viruses that have acquired the genotype of one parent and the phenotype of another. - Recombinant: A new combination of genes not found together on the chromosome in either parent; a recombinant results from crossing over. - Codon: A sequence of three bases in DNA or RNA that codes for a single amino acid; it enables specific proteins to be made by specific genes. - Anti-codon: A sequence of three bases in tRNA that is complementary to a codon in mRNA; it enables tRNA to sequence amino acids in the order specified by mRNA. - Ribosomes: Complexes of rRNA and protein in the cytoplasm that serve as platforms for translation of mRNA into protein. - Plasmid: Extrachromosomal circular DNA in bacteria, which replicates independently and encodes a product. - Transcription: Synthesis of single-stranded RNA by RNA polymerase using DNA as a template; the process in the nucleus whereby DNA is transcribed into mRNA. - Translation: The process of translating the codon sequence in mRNA into polypeptides with the help of tRNA and ribosomes.

  46. Define transposons. Also write their types and properties.

  49. What is fluorescence? State the principle of the fluorescence microscope.
    Topic: Microscopy • 2 marks marks

    Fluorescence is the ability of substances to absorb short wavelengths of light (ultraviolet) and give off light at a longer wavelength (visible). Principle of the fluorescence microscope: The specimen is illuminated with light of a specific wavelength, which is absorbed by the fluorophores, causing them to emit light of longer wavelengths. Fluorescent dyes are used for specimen staining; examples of fluorescent stains are DAPI and Hoechst.

  50. Phase-contrast microscope: principle and applications.
    Topic: Microscopy • 5 marks marks

    Phase-contrast microscopy (PCM) is an optical microscopy technique that converts phase shifts in light passing through a transparent specimen into brightness changes in the image. Phase shifts themselves are invisible, but become visible when shown as brightness variations. Principle: When light passes through cells, small phase shifts occur. These phase shifts are converted into changes in amplitude, which can be observed as differences in image contrast. Applications: cell culture; live cell imaging — living cells can be observed in their natural state without previous fixation or labelling.

  51. What is differential interference contrast (DIC / Nomarski) microscopy?
    Topic: Microscopy

    DIC, also known as Nomarski interference contrast microscopy, is used to enhance the contrast in unstained, transparent samples. DIC works on the principle of interferometry to gain information about the optical path length. It is a more expensive, label-free microscopy technique with high sensitivity to thin cellular material, even when it is located within thick tissue. It is useful for rendering contrast in transparent samples and gives brilliant pseudo-3D relief shading images. (In the Spring 2022 paper, a DIC/Nomarski microscope was the answer for viewing transparent samples.)

  52. Differentiate between scanning electron microscopy (SEM) and transmission electron microscopy (TEM).
    Topic: Microscopy • 5 marks marks

    SEM: An electron gun produces a finely focused beam of electrons called the primary electron beam. These electrons pass through electromagnetic lenses and are directed over the surface of the specimen. The primary electron beam knocks electrons out of the surface of the specimen, and the secondary electrons thus produced are transmitted to an electron collector, amplified, and used to produce an image on a viewing screen or photographic plate. The image is called a scanning electron micrograph. TEM: A focused beam of electrons from an electron gun passes through the ultrathin section of the specimen. The beam is focused on a small area of the specimen by an electromagnetic condenser lens. Instead of being placed on a glass slide, the specimen is usually placed on a copper mesh grid.

  53. What is scanned probe microscopy? Write its principle, types and limitations.
    Topic: Microscopy • 5 marks marks

    Scanned probe microscopy forms images of surfaces using a physical probe that scans the specimen; it was founded in 1981. Principle: The interaction between the sharp probe and the surface provides a 3D topographic image of the surface at the atomic scale. The two most popular modes (types) are: - Scanning Tunnelling Microscopy (STM): measures the electrical current between the tip and the specimen. - Atomic Force Microscopy (AFM): measures the electrostatic force between the tip and the specimen. Image formation: The tip is scanned over the surface, a value is recorded, and it is displayed as a heat map. Limitations: The detailed shape of the probe is difficult to determine sometimes; slower in acquiring images; the maximum image size is smaller; cannot examine buried solid–solid or liquid–liquid interfaces.

  54. Which dyes are used in TEM?
    Topic: Microscopy • 3 marks marks

    Uranyl acetate, lead citrate, and osmium tetroxide are commonly used dyes in Transmission Electron Microscopy (TEM) to enhance contrast and visualize biological specimens. Contrast can be greatly enhanced by using a “dye” that absorbs electrons and produces a darker image in the stained region — lead citrate, osmium tetroxide, tungsten, uranyl acetate and uranium (stains). These metals can be fixed onto the specimen (positive staining) or used to increase the electron opacity of the surrounding field (negative staining).

  61. Describe embryonic inoculation (I/E, I/C and I/V inoculation).
    Topic: Cultivation of Viruses — Cell Culture, Eggs & Isolation • 3 marks marks

    Embryos are inoculated and incubated for a period of one to six days, during which they are observed twice a day for evidence of death of the embryo. Some viruses, such as equine encephalomyelitis virus, kill the embryo within 24 hours, while others kill the embryos in 2 to 3 days. Some viruses readily multiply within the embryonic tissue and are seldom lethal. Embryo death is accompanied by collapse of the large blood vessels of the chorioallantois, so that they are difficult to see upon candling the egg, whereas they are quite distinct while the embryos are alive. Normal movements of the embryo are no longer observed if the embryo is moribund or dead. If the embryo is not dead after 5 or 6 days of incubation, it is killed or examined for lesions. These inoculations of embryos are not widely used.

  62. Which type of centrifuge is best for the isolation of viruses?
    Topic: Cultivation of Viruses — Cell Culture, Eggs & Isolation

    Ultracentrifuge. Ultracentrifuges are typically considered to be the best type of centrifuge for virus isolation, as they have the capability to separate viral particles from other contaminants at high speed and with high precision. These centrifuges typically operate at speeds of up to 100,000 rotations per minute (rpm) and have capacities ranging from a few millilitres to several litres. Additionally, ultracentrifuges are capable of separating individual viral particles based on differences in size, density, and buoyant density, which can be important for the purification of virus particles from complex biological mixtures. Thus, ultracentrifuges are widely accepted as the gold standard for virus isolation and purification.

  64. What is the virus neutralization test? Describe it.
    Topic: Serological Tests & Diagnosis • 5 marks marks

    It is a serological test that detects the presence of viral neutralizing antibodies. The antibodies bind to viral particles; these antibodies block viral infectivity and prevent viral infections. Procedure points (from the Fall 2021 / Spring 2022 solved papers): - Used to confirm the identity of an unknown viral isolate - Use of a known dilution of specific antiserum mixed with multiple dilutions of virus; a wide range of dilutions is used - Equal volumes of viral dilution and immune serum are used - Observation of the virus to produce cytopathic effects - Inoculation of 5 to 6 animals, with an equal number of controls - Observation of animals for signs, lesions or deaths

  65. What is the principle of the fluorescence antibody technique (FAT)?
    Topic: Serological Tests & Diagnosis • 3 marks marks

    The principle is based on the observation that virus proteins (antigen) are present in the tissues, e.g. rabies virus present in nervous tissues. The Fluorescence Antibody Technique (FAT) is based on the use of fluorescent-labelled antibodies that specifically bind to the antigen of interest. The fluorescent tag allows for the visualization of the bound antibody–antigen complex, which is detected using a fluorescence microscope. The technique is highly specific and sensitive, allowing for the rapid and accurate detection of a wide range of pathogens in biological samples.

  66. What are the uses of the immunofluorescence / fluorescence antibody technique? (2–5 marks)
    Topic: Serological Tests & Diagnosis

  68. What is the complement fixation test (CFT)?
    Topic: Serological Tests & Diagnosis • 5 marks marks

    The complement fixation test is an immunological test used to detect the presence of either specific antibody or specific antigen on the basis of complement fixation. Complement is a system of serum proteins that react with antigen–antibody complexes; if this reaction occurs on a cell surface, it will result in the formation of trans-membrane pores and therefore destruction of the cell. Short procedure: Serum is separated from the patient. Patients naturally have different levels of complement proteins in their serum, so the complement proteins in the patient's serum are first destroyed (by heating, which destroys complement but none of the antibodies) and replaced by a known amount of standardized complement proteins (frequently obtained from guinea pig serum). The antigen of interest is added to the serum. Then sheep red blood cells (sRBCs) which have been pre-bound to anti-sRBC antibodies are added. Result interpretation: If the patient's serum contains antibodies against the antigen of interest, they bind to the antigen to form antigen–antibody complexes, and the complement proteins react with these complexes and are depleted; thus, when the sRBC–antibody complexes are added, no complement is left and there is no lysis. If no antibodies against the antigen of interest are present, the complement is not depleted and it reacts with the sRBC–antibody complexes, lysing the sRBCs and spilling their contents into the solution, turning the solution pink.

  89. Describe the replication of retroviruses.
    Topic: Specific Virus Groups & Families • 5 marks marks

    Once inside the host cell cytoplasm, the virus uses its own reverse transcriptase (RT) enzyme to produce DNA from its RNA genome — the reverse of the usual pattern, thus “retro” (backwards). The new DNA is then incorporated into the host cell genome by an integrase enzyme; in this integrated state the virus is referred to as a provirus. The host cell then treats the viral DNA as part of its own genome, transcribing and translating the viral genes along with the cell's own genes, and the proteins produced are required to assemble new copies of the virus. It is difficult to detect the virus until it has infected the host; infection will persist indefinitely. Early steps: Retroviral particles must bind specifically to their target cells (infection requires that the cell have a surface protein that can serve as a receptor for the envelope protein of the retrovirus), cross the plasma membrane, reverse-transcribe their RNA genome, uncoat the cores, find their way to the nuclear membrane, penetrate into the nucleus and hijack the cellular machinery. When a retrovirus infects a cell, its molecules of reverse transcriptase are carried into the cell attached to the viral RNA molecules; the reverse transcriptase synthesizes DNA copies of the RNA, and this dsDNA is integrated into the host chromosome with the help of the viral enzyme integrase. Later stages: Transcription, translation (the inserts are transcribed by the host's enzymes into fresh RNA molecules, which re-enter the cytosol, where some are translated by host ribosomes), assembly, maturation, budding and release.
