  1. Write the names of the five kingdoms in the five-kingdom system of classification.
    Topic: Introduction, Classification and Microbial Diversity • 5 marks marks

    In 1969, the five-kingdom classification was proposed by Robert Whittaker: 1. Monera: Bacteria 2. Plantae: Plants 3. Animalia: Animals 4. Fungi: Yeasts, molds and mushrooms 5. Protista: These are unicellular eukaryotes. Organisms that do not fit into any other category are placed in Protista. They are larger than prokaryotes. They include algae, protozoa, slime molds and water molds.

  2. Describe the taxonomic hierarchy used for grouping microbes.
    Topic: Introduction, Classification and Microbial Diversity • 5 marks marks

    Microbes are placed in groups based on similarities that they share with each other. All organisms can be grouped into a series of subdivisions that make up the taxonomic hierarchy. A bacterial species represents a monophyletic and genomically coherent cluster of individual organisms that show a high degree of overall similarity with respect to many independent characteristics, and is diagnosable by a discriminative phenotypic property. A genus consists of various species; these species differ from each other in certain ways, although they are related by descent. Related genera make up a family. A group of similar families constitutes an order, and a group of similar orders makes up a class. Related classes, in turn, make up a phylum. All phyla that are related to each other make up a kingdom, and related kingdoms are grouped into a domain. Domain > Kingdom > Phylum > Class > Order > Family > Genus > Species

  4. Write three differences between prokaryotes and eukaryotes.
    Topic: Introduction, Classification and Microbial Diversity • 3 marks marks

    1. A prominent (true) nucleus is found in eukaryotes while it is absent in prokaryotes. 2. Membrane-bound organelles are found in eukaryotes and are absent in prokaryotes. 3. No histones are found in prokaryotes, while histones are present in eukaryotes. 4. Examples: prokaryotes are bacteria and archaea; all organisms found in domain Eukarya are eukaryotes.

  7. Explain the salient features of viruses, virions, viroids and prions.
    Topic: Introduction, Classification and Microbial Diversity • 5 marks marks

    Viruses: They are acellular structures that consist of a protein capsid enclosing either DNA or RNA (but never both), and sometimes may contain an extra membrane called an envelope. Viroids: They are composed only of circular single-stranded (ss) RNA. Virusoids: They contain circular ssRNA that need helper viruses for replication and encapsidation. They are also called satellite viruses. Prions: These are basically infectious proteins formed inside the body. They behave like organisms as they can be transmitted from one individual to another. They are extremely resistant to heat.

  8. Write the characteristics of algae.
    Topic: Introduction, Classification and Microbial Diversity • 3 marks marks

    These are eukaryotes. Their cell wall consists of cellulose. These are photosynthetic and produce oxygen. They are usually unicellular, but multicellular algae are also common. Seaweeds and pond scum are some of the examples. Algae are simple eukaryotic cells; some are unicellular, others are multicellular (thallus), but they lack tissues such as roots, stem and leaves typically seen in plants. Algae absorb nutrients from water through their surfaces and are mostly photoautotrophs; a few are chemoheterotrophs. They are responsible for about 80% of atmospheric O2 on the face of the earth. The four groups of algae include blue-green algae, green algae, brown algae and red algae. The body of a multicellular alga such as seaweed is called a thallus, which consists of branched holdfasts (which anchor the alga to rock), stem-like hollow stipes and leaf-like blades. There is no vascular tissue in algae.

  11. What is resolution? What is the resolving power of a light microscope?
    Topic: Microscopy and Staining • 2 marks marks

    Resolution is the ability of the lenses to distinguish between two closely lying objects as separate. The light microscope resolving power is 0.2 um. In the formula, n = refractive index (1.5 for immersion oil). Resolution (d) is directly proportional to the wavelength of the light used. As resolution is the smallest distance between two closely lying points, use of light with a smaller wavelength gives better resolution. That is why blue filters are used for illumination, as blue light has the shortest wavelength in the visible spectrum.

  12. What is refractive index?
    Topic: Microscopy and Staining • 2 marks marks

    It is the ratio of the velocity of light in a vacuum to its velocity in a specified medium. Refractive index is the ability of a medium to bend the light. The refractive index of glass is 1.5.

  13. Write the functions of the illuminator, condenser and objective lens of a microscope.

  14. Differentiate between phase contrast and dark field microscopy.
    Topic: Microscopy and Staining • 3 marks marks

    Dark field microscopy: The arrangement of the condenser in dark field microscopy throws light rays at an oblique angle so that if the specimen is not present on the slide, no image is formed. However, if organisms are present on the glass slide, they reflect the light into the objective lens and we see the cells as bright objects in a dark background, much like stars in the sky at night. The beauty of this microscope is that one does not have to stain the organisms to see them. Phase contrast microscopy: This instrument is built on the concept of phases of light waves. The light ray that passes through an organism gets retarded by about 1/4 of its wavelength compared with the light that passes outside the organism (through the glass slide only). A phase plate inserted into the body of the microscope (above the objective lens), thicker in the center than at the ends, further retards the retarded light ray, which on recombination creates contrast and makes the cells visible. This method also does not involve staining.

  15. Describe five types of microscopy.
    Topic: Microscopy and Staining • 5 marks marks

    1. Dark field microscopy: This microscope makes use of a special condenser that provides illumination from the sides only. The condenser throws light rays at an oblique angle, so if the specimen is not present no image is formed; if organisms are present, they reflect light into the objective lens and appear as bright objects in a dark background. Organisms do not have to be stained to be seen. 2. Phase contrast microscopy: Built on the concept of phases of light waves. Light passing through an organism is retarded by about 1/4 of its wavelength; a phase plate further retards it, and the recombined light creates contrast which makes the cells/organisms visible. This method also does not involve staining. 3. Fluorescence microscopy: This microscope is equipped with UV light as the source of illumination. The light falls over the slide (having the specimen) and creates fluorescence. Fluorochromes are substances that absorb short-wavelength light and emit longer-wavelength (visible) light. Fluorochromes are attached to antibodies specific to organisms, so the presence of an organism in a specimen can be determined using fluorochrome-tagged antibodies. 4. Transmission electron microscope (TEM): The electron microscope uses an electron beam instead of light rays. Since the electron beam has a much shorter wavelength, the resolution of EM is 0.1 nm compared with the light microscope's 200 nm. Focusing of the electron beam is done not by glass lenses but by electromagnets that act like lenses. The image of the specimen is formed on a fluorescent screen like a TV screen. 5. Scanning electron microscope (SEM): A modified version of the electron microscope. The electron beam is allowed to fall onto the specimen and the image is formed from the reflected electrons, which are collected and assembled into an image by the detector.

  16. Labelled diagram of an electron (transmission) microscope — name its main parts.
    Topic: Microscopy and Staining • 5 marks marks

    This is a diagram question in the source papers; the main parts to label in a transmission electron microscope are: electron gun (source of the electron beam), condenser electromagnets (condenser lenses) that focus the beam onto the specimen, specimen stage/holder, objective electromagnetic lens, projector/intermediate electromagnetic lens, and the fluorescent viewing screen (or photographic plate) on which the image is formed, all enclosed in a vacuum column. Unlike a light microscope, focusing is done by electromagnets instead of glass lenses, and the image is formed on a fluorescent screen like a TV screen.

  17. What are fluorochromes?
    Topic: Microscopy and Staining • 2 marks marks

    Fluorochromes are substances that absorb short-wavelength light and emit longer-wavelength light (visible light spectrum). Fluorochromes are used in fluorescent microscopy. They are attached to antibodies that are specific to organisms. So, if an organism is present in the specimen, we can use specific antibodies to determine the presence of those organisms by using fluorochrome-tagged antibodies.

  18. Describe the physical and chemical methods of making a smear on a slide.
    Topic: Microscopy and Staining • 3 marks marks

    Staining starts with making a smear using a glass slide. - Using a platinum loop, a colony or a drop of broth culture can be smeared into a thin film on a glass slide for making a smear. - The specimen is spread into a thin film (smear). - The smear is air-dried. - The smear is fixed (attached) to the slide before staining. - Heating the slide is one way of fixing the smear (physical method). - Methyl alcohol can also be used for fixing (chemical method).

  19. Write down the advantages of staining.
    Topic: Microscopy and Staining • 2 marks marks

    - Most organisms appear colorless when seen under a microscope. - Staining emphasizes certain structures of the organisms. - Staining is just coloring with a dye. - Staining increases the visibility of microbes because it increases contrast. - Shape, size and arrangements of the organisms can be readily seen. - Purity or contamination of a culture can be determined. - Differentiation and classification of microbes is possible; for example, microbes can be categorized into Gram-positive or Gram-negative groups. - Structures such as flagella, capsule and spores of bacteria can be detected with staining. - It gives quick results when examining infections, is simple and cost-effective, helps in determining appropriate treatments for infection, and is a key procedure in identifying bacteria.

  22. What is acid-fast staining? Which bacteria are stained by it?
    Topic: Microscopy and Staining

    Some bacteria, such as Mycobacterium, have a waxy material in their cell wall. The acid-fast stain is a laboratory test that determines if a sample of tissue, blood, or other body substance is infected with the bacteria that cause tuberculosis (TB) and other illnesses. Some of the sample is placed on a glass slide, stained, and heated. The cells in the sample hold onto the dye. The slide is then washed with an acid solution and a different stain is applied. Bacteria that hold onto the first dye are considered "acid-fast" because they resist the acid wash. These types of bacteria are associated with TB and other infections.

  23. Write the functions of the cell membrane in bacteria.
    Topic: Bacterial Cell Structure • 5 marks marks

    The cell membrane is composed of phospholipid molecules arranged tail to tail with heads facing away from each other, so the lipid molecules appear as a bilayer. All membranes enclosing the organelles in the cell or the cell itself look like bilayer structures; this is the basic design of the plasma membrane. Proteins and carbohydrates are also inserted into the plasma membrane. Functions: - Selective permeable barrier - Passive and active transport - Respiration in microbes (bacteria) - Photosynthesis in microbes - Lipid synthesis - It regulates the transport of substances in and out of the cell - It receives chemical messengers from other cells; cell mobility, secretion and absorption of substances - Cell wall parts are transported by a molecule called bactoprenol, which is present in the plasma membrane - The plasma membrane has many receptors with which various ligands can bind and initiate signals for gene expression etc. Note: cell membrane lipids can be disrupted by alcohols, quaternary ammonium compounds and polymyxins, so these compounds are used as disinfectants or for controlling microbial growth.

  24. Write the characteristics of integral proteins.
    Topic: Bacterial Cell Structure • 3 marks marks

    Integral proteins are inserted in the membrane or embedded in the membrane. Integral proteins are not static in position in the membrane; they can diffuse laterally and change positions in the membrane from time to time. So, the plasma membrane is like a water pond and integral proteins are like plastic bags thrown in the pond, which swim and move with air currents. Integral proteins act as channels or carriers in facilitated diffusion. Integral proteins are called transporters or permeases.

  25. What is the function of the cell wall in bacteria?
    Topic: Bacterial Cell Structure • 5 marks marks

    The cell wall is the most important layer in bacteria. It is a rigid layer just outside the plasma membrane and the most important structure in prokaryotes. - It provides shape to the organism. - It provides protection from osmotic lysis. - It is involved in pathogenicity (the ability of the organism to cause disease). - Several antibiotics act on it. Each bacterium is enclosed by a rigid cell wall composed of peptidoglycan, a protein-sugar (polysaccharide) molecule. The wall surrounds the cytoplasmic membrane, protecting it from the environment. It also helps to anchor appendages like the pili and flagella, which originate in the cytoplasmic membrane and protrude through the wall to the outside. The strength of the wall keeps the cell from bursting when there are large differences in osmotic pressure between the cytoplasm and the environment. Peptidoglycan is thick in Gram-positive cells and thin in Gram-negative cells.

  26. What are L-form bacteria?
    Topic: Bacterial Cell Structure • 2 marks marks

    Some cells, called L-form organisms, are naturally found without the cell wall. Normally they have a cell wall, but under certain conditions they may lack a cell wall. These cells can be created when grown in the presence of penicillin, which inhibits cell wall synthesis.

  27. Write the three parts of lipopolysaccharides (LPS).
    Topic: Bacterial Cell Structure • 3 marks marks

    The lipopolysaccharide molecule consists of 3 parts: 1. Lipid A: endotoxin — causes fever, vasodilation and shock. 2. Core polysaccharide: provides structural support. 3. O side chain: antigenic, much like teichoic acid in Gram-positive bacteria.

  28. What do you know about the types of teichoic acid?
    Topic: Bacterial Cell Structure

    Teichoic acids are polymers of glycerol or ribitol phosphate found in the cell wall of Gram-positive bacteria. They are of two main types: (1) wall teichoic acids, which are covalently attached to peptidoglycan in the cell wall, and (2) membrane (lipoteichoic) acids, which are anchored to the plasma membrane and pass through the peptidoglycan layer. Teichoic acids give the Gram-positive cell wall its negative charge, help in ion transport, and are antigenic.

  29. What is glycocalyx? What is a capsule?
    Topic: Bacterial Cell Structure • 2 marks marks

    Glycocalyx is the outermost layer, viscous and gelatinous in nature, that surrounds the cell. It is composed of polysaccharide and polypeptide, or both. If the glycocalyx is organized and firmly attached to the cell, it is called a capsule. - The capsule plays an important role in virulence (degree of pathogenicity). - It can also be a part of the vaccine against the bacteria to which it belongs. - It can be demonstrated by the negative staining technique. - Capsulated organisms make glistening colonies. Examples: Bacillus anthracis, Streptococcus pneumoniae and Klebsiella. The size of the capsule depends upon the culture conditions. Capsules are mostly water-soluble, and capsular organisms usually make the broth viscous and stringy.

  30. What is a flagellum? Give its functions.
    Topic: Bacterial Cell Structure • 2 marks marks

    A long filamentous structure that propels bacteria is called a flagellum. It helps bacteria to move (motility).

  31. Write the parts of a flagellum.
    Topic: Bacterial Cell Structure • 2 marks marks

    A flagellum consists of the following 3 parts: - Filament: made of a protein called flagellin, which makes the H antigen that can be used for serovar identification in Gram-negative bacteria. - Hook: made of a different protein. - Basal body: the most complex part.

  32. Define pili and give their functions.
    Topic: Bacterial Cell Structure • 5 marks marks

    Pili are hair-like structures composed of pilin, usually one to ten in number. - Longer than fimbriae - Used for attachment to host cells and to bacteria - Used for DNA transfer from one bacterium to another: conjugation (sex pili) - Also function in twitching motility - Gliding motility is also a function of the pili

  33. What are pili and what are their types?
    Topic: Bacterial Cell Structure • 5 marks marks

    Pili are hair-like appendages composed of the protein pilin, usually one to ten in number, longer than fimbriae, used for attachment and for DNA transfer during conjugation. Their types are: (1) common (adhesive) pili, present in larger numbers, which help the bacterium attach to host cells and surfaces; and (2) sex pili, usually one or a few in number, which form the conjugation tube through which DNA (e.g. the F plasmid) is transferred from a donor to a recipient cell.

  34. What are fimbriae? Write their location and function.
    Topic: Bacterial Cell Structure • 3 marks marks

    Fimbriae are hair-like small appendages on Gram-negative bacteria, present around the entire cell surface. Fimbriae help bacteria attach to surfaces before they can secrete biofilm. Fimbriae also help in attachment, e.g. in Neisseria gonorrhoeae.

  35. Define ribosomes and give their composition in prokaryotes.
    Topic: Bacterial Cell Structure • 2 marks marks

    Ribosomes are non-membranous organelles present in both prokaryotes and eukaryotes and are involved in protein synthesis. Their composition in prokaryotes is: 70S ribosomes (when the two subunits are combined into one unit) — the 50S subunit contains one rRNA and the 30S subunit contains two rRNAs. Several antibiotics act on ribosomes and inhibit protein synthesis; for example, streptomycin attaches to the 30S subunit.

  36. Describe the structural functions of ribosomes, cell wall and flagella.
    Topic: Bacterial Cell Structure • 3 marks marks

    Ribosomes: Composed of proteins + ribosomal RNA. Prokaryotes have 70S ribosomes (50S subunit: one rRNA; 30S subunit: two rRNAs). Several antibiotics act on ribosomes and inhibit protein synthesis; streptomycin attaches to the 30S subunit. Cell wall: The most important layer in bacteria; a rigid layer just outside the plasma membrane and the most important structure in prokaryotes. It provides shape to the organism, protection from osmotic lysis, and is involved in pathogenicity. Several antibiotics act on it. Flagella: Long filamentous structures that propel bacteria. A flagellum consists of 3 parts: filament (made of flagellin, the H antigen), hook (a different protein) and basal body (the most complex part).

  37. What are inclusions? Write their types.
    Topic: Bacterial Cell Structure • 3 marks marks

    Inclusions are reserve deposits of nutrients. Bacteria accumulate nutrients when nutrients are plentiful. Concentrating nutrients in the form of inclusions avoids the increase in osmotic pressure due to accumulation of nutrients in one place. Not all organisms accumulate them, so inclusions vary from bacterium to bacterium. They can also serve as markers for bacterial identification, as some are limited to specific organisms. Important inclusions are: - Metachromatic granules - Polysaccharide granules - Lipid inclusions - Sulfur granules

  38. Write the characteristics of metachromatic granules.
    Topic: Bacterial Cell Structure • 2 marks marks

    Metachromatic granules are also called volutin. They stain red with certain dyes such as methylene blue; that is why they are called metachromatic (they stain in a different color — methylene blue gives blue color but the color on these granules is red). They are large inclusions and contain inorganic phosphates, which are used in ATP synthesis. They are characteristic of Corynebacterium diphtheriae, which can be identified by the presence of these granules.

  39. Write the characteristics of magnetosomes.
    Topic: Bacterial Cell Structure • 2 marks marks

    - Inclusions of iron oxide - Surrounded by invaginations of the plasma membrane - Present in Gram-negative bacteria - Act like a magnet; bacteria can stick to iron-containing rocks for nutrition - Decompose H2O2, which is toxic for cells

  40. What is an endospore?
    Topic: Bacterial Cell Structure • 2 marks marks

    An endospore is a resistant, dormant structure formed within a bacterial cell to pass unfavorable conditions. - Specialized resting cells; highly durable, dehydrated forms of bacteria, formed inside a bacterium. - Survive extremes of temperature; resistant to heat; need to be autoclaved in order to be killed. - Survive lack of water; can survive in high salt concentrations. Clostridium spores can survive in honey, and that is one reason honey is not recommended for infants, because spores can cause tetanus in babies. - Survive radiation danger and toxic environments. - Mostly formed by Gram-positive bacteria: genus Clostridium and genus Bacillus. One Gram-negative bacterium, Coxiella burnetii, also has it. - Formation of spores takes place within a vegetative cell and the process is called sporulation or sporogenesis; it is initiated when nutrients become unavailable.

  41. What is sporulation (sporogenesis)?
    Topic: Bacterial Cell Structure • 2 marks marks

    Formation of spores takes place within a vegetative cell, and the process is called sporulation or sporogenesis. It is initiated when nutrients become unavailable.

  42. Write the names and features of the five layers of an endospore.
    Topic: Bacterial Cell Structure • 5 marks marks

    1. Exosporium: a thin, delicate outermost covering of the spore. 2. Spore coat: the 2nd layer, underneath the exosporium. It is thick and composed of several protein layers. It is resistant to chemicals and contains enzymes for germination. Germination of spores into vegetative form occurs when the environment becomes favorable for growth. 3. Cortex: the 3rd layer from outside in. It has peptidoglycan in it. 4. Spore cell wall (core wall): surrounds the protoplast or spore core. 5. Spore core: contains the nucleoid and ribosomes.

  43. Write the stages of germination of a spore.
    Topic: Bacterial Cell Structure • 3 marks marks

    A spore germinates when it finds a conducive environment for its growth. Germination has 3 phases or stages: 1. Activation: it prepares the spore for germination. Heat can activate the spore when appropriate moisture and nutrients are present in the environment. 2. Germination: the spore starts swelling and losing its coats etc. It becomes metabolically active. 3. Outgrowth: new components are made.

  44. Write the three shapes of bacteria and the subgroups of spiral bacteria.
    Topic: Bacterial Cell Structure • 3 marks marks

    1. Coccus: circular or spherical in shape. 2. Bacillus: rod-shaped bacteria. 3. Spiral: curved-shaped bacteria. Spirals are further divided into 3 subgroups: - Vibrio: curved rods - Spirillum: helical but rigid - Spirochete: helical but flexible Spirochetes move by axial filaments which are enclosed by the outer membrane.

  45. Write down the types of bacteria (bases of classification).
    Topic: Bacterial Cell Structure • 3 marks marks

    Bacteria can be classified on different bases: photosynthetic, saprotrophic and heterotrophic on the basis of mode of obtaining food; aerobic and anaerobic on the basis of oxygen requirement; Gram-positive and Gram-negative on the basis of cell wall composition; fermentor and non-fermentor on the basis of fermentation-carrying-out ability, and so on.

  46. What are chemotaxis and phototaxis?
    Topic: Bacterial Cell Structure • 2 marks marks

    Movement of an organism in response to a chemical is called chemotaxis. Movement of an organism in response to a light stimulus is called phototaxis.

  47. Define culture, culture medium and inoculum.

    - Culture: this term is used to define growth of microbes. In other words, microbial growth in the lab is called a culture. - Culture medium: a nutrient material that supports the growth of microbes in the lab. A culture medium is composed of different nutrients that are essential for microbial growth. - Inoculum: microbes introduced into a culture medium that initiate growth of the organism; a small amount of material containing bacteria, viruses or other microorganisms that is used to start a culture.

  48. Name the types of culture media on the basis of function.
    Topic: Microbial Growth and Culture

  49. What is agar? Write its characteristics.
    Topic: Microbial Growth and Culture • 2 marks marks

    Agar is the solidifier of a medium. It is derived from a seaweed. It is not digested/degraded by bacteria during growth. It melts at about 100 C and solidifies at about 43 C, so it is used for making plates and slants.

  50. Why is solid medium better than liquid medium for the growth of bacteria?
    Topic: Microbial Growth and Culture • 2 marks marks

    Solidification of the medium is done for purifying organisms from each other, as solid medium provides a surface for individual colonies to grow well separated from each other. A well-isolated colony is assumed to be the progeny of a single cell. Purification of organisms is done by streaking the organisms. Secondly, solid media are used to study the colony characteristics.

  51. What is a buffer? Which substances are used as buffers in culture media?
    Topic: Microbial Growth and Culture • 2 marks marks

    Buffers are used to maintain the desired pH. A buffer is a solution that can maintain a nearly constant pH if it is diluted, or if relatively small amounts of strong acids or bases are added. Peptones and amino acids are used as buffers; phosphates are also used for buffers. Three buffers used for controlling the pH of a culture medium are: 1. Peptones, 2. Amino acids, 3. PBS (phosphate buffer).

  52. What are the physical requirements for the growth of bacteria?
    Topic: Microbial Growth and Culture • 3 marks marks

    The physical requirements for bacterial growth are: 1. Temperature 2. pH 3. Osmotic pressure

  53. Describe the four phases of the bacterial growth curve.
    Topic: Microbial Growth and Culture • 3 marks marks

    There are four distinct phases of the growth curve: 1. Lag phase: cells prepare for growth in this phase. No growth is observed during this period; however, cells are metabolically very active. 2. Log phase: organisms multiply exponentially or logarithmically. Generation time becomes constant during this phase, so the log graph shows a straight line. Cells are in the most active stage. For commercial applications such as vaccine production, cells have to remain in this phase to reproduce most efficiently. This phase is also used to determine the generation time. 3. Stationary phase: also called a period of equilibrium, as microbial deaths equal the production of new cells. Organisms start dying, but the dead organisms are replaced by new ones because replication is still going on, so the overall number of organisms does not change. 4. Death phase: the number of deaths exceeds the number of new cells formed, so the overall number decreases. It is also called the logarithmic decline phase. The reason is that nutrients are depleted and waste products, which are toxic to the cells, accumulate, suppressing growth and killing the cells.

  54. Differentiate between direct count and viable count.
    Topic: Microbial Growth and Culture • 2 marks marks

    Direct microscopic count: This method uses a hemocytometer, which takes a specific volume of sample, and the number of microbes can directly be counted under a light microscope. The number of microbes in one ml of original sample may be determined by multiplying with the appropriate number. No incubation is required, so this method is quicker, and it gives a total count (viable as well as dead microbes). There is an automated version of this method where the cells are counted electronically; the instrument is called a Coulter counter. Viable count: only living (viable) cells are counted, using plate methods — the pour plate method and the spread plate method — in which viable cells form colonies that are counted.

  55. What is passive transport? Name three passive movements.
    Topic: Microbial Growth and Culture • 2 marks marks

    Passive transport is the movement of substances with the concentration gradient, i.e. substances move from a higher concentration to a lower concentration. The gradient provides the force for movement of molecules, and this happens with no energy expenditure. Passive movement is divided into simple diffusion and facilitated diffusion (in facilitated diffusion, integral proteins act as channels or carriers, called transporters or permeases, and no energy is required). Three passive movements: 1. Simple diffusion, 2. Facilitated diffusion, 3. Osmosis.

  56. What is osmotic pressure? What is plasmolysis?
    Topic: Microbial Growth and Culture • 3 marks marks

    Osmotic pressure is the force caused by a solution passing through a semi-permeable surface by osmosis, which is equal to the force required to resist the solution from passing back through the surface. An example of osmotic pressure is the process used to filter water. Plasmolysis: if cells are placed in hypertonic solutions, water leaves the cells, shrinking the cells and damaging them. This process is called plasmolysis — the process in which cells lose water in a hypertonic solution.

  57. What are microaerophiles? Give their characteristics.
    Topic: Microbial Growth and Culture • 2 marks marks

    Microaerophiles need oxygen because they cannot ferment or respire anaerobically. However, they are poisoned by high concentrations of oxygen. They gather in the upper part of the test tube but not at the very top.

  58. Describe the types of microbes based on oxygen requirement.
    Topic: Microbial Growth and Culture • 5 marks marks

    Oxygen is needed for aerobic respiration. NADH formed during the Krebs cycle is oxidized via the electron transport chain, which generates 36 or 38 ATP molecules in organisms that use O2 as the final electron acceptor. Based on the use and concentration of oxygen, microbes are classified into five groups: 1. Obligate aerobes: oxygen must be present for their growth; they do not grow if O2 is not present. In a tube of nutrient agar their growth remains confined to the surface only. Example: Mycobacterium tuberculosis. 2. Facultative anaerobes: these organisms prefer aerobic respiration; however, if oxygen is not available, they can use anaerobic respiration or fermentative modes for generating ATP. They are present throughout the tube, but more growth is seen at the top (close to the surface) because they prefer using oxygen if available. 3. Obligate anaerobes: these organisms do not use oxygen, and they cannot grow in the presence of oxygen, because oxygen creates toxic compounds which can kill these cells. Strict anaerobes do not have a system to get rid of these toxic compounds. Their growth remains confined to the bottom of the tube, which has little oxygen. 4. Aerotolerant aerobes (anaerobes): these organisms do not use oxygen but are also not bothered by its presence; they have a better system to dispose of toxic oxygen compounds. They grow evenly throughout the tube of medium. 5. Microaerophiles: need oxygen at low concentration but are poisoned by high concentrations of oxygen.

  59. Name five bacteria which need oxygen for growth.
    Topic: Microbial Growth and Culture • 3 marks marks

    1. Nocardia asteroides 2. Mycobacterium tuberculosis 3. Bacillus anthracis 4. Pseudomonas aeruginosa 5. Neisseria gonorrhoeae

  60. Classify bacteria on the basis of temperature requirements.
    Topic: Microbial Growth and Culture • 5 marks marks

    Organisms are basically classified into three groups based on temperature requirements: 1. Psychrophiles: cold-loving. Strict psychrophiles grow at about 15 C; psychrotrophs have an optimum temperature of 20-30 C and are the food-spoilage bacteria that can spoil food during refrigeration. 2. Mesophiles: 25-40 C. These are the ones that cause diseases in animals and humans. Optimum is 37 C, the body temperature of humans and animals. 3. Thermophiles: 50-60 C, heat-loving, important in organic compost piles. They are further divided into hyperthermophiles, which grow optimally at 80 C and live in hot springs.

  61. How do bacteria get nitrogen?
    Topic: Microbial Growth and Culture • 2 marks marks

    Bacteria get nitrogen: - By decomposing proteins - From NH4+ ions - From nitrates - Using gaseous N2 (nitrogen-fixing bacteria)

  62. What is phosphorylation?
    Topic: Microbial Metabolism • 2 marks marks

    The addition of an inorganic phosphate group to a chemical compound is called phosphorylation, e.g. addition of P to ADP for the synthesis of ATP during metabolic reactions.

  63. Explain oxidative phosphorylation and photophosphorylation (mechanisms of phosphorylation).
    Topic: Microbial Metabolism • 3 marks marks

    Oxidative phosphorylation: a. Electrons captured from foods are transferred to coenzymes such as NAD+ or FAD etc. b. These electrons then pass through a series of electron carriers and ultimately to the last electron acceptor molecules such as O2 or other inorganic compounds (nitrate, sulphate, carbonate etc.), through a system called the electron transport chain. c. During these events, ATP is generated by chemiosmosis. Photophosphorylation: This occurs in plants and chlorophyll-containing microbes or photosynthetic cells. In these cells, light energy is finally converted into ATP. An electron transport chain is also involved here.

  64. Write the three steps of respiration of glucose. Differentiate aerobic and anaerobic respiration.
    Topic: Microbial Metabolism • 3 marks marks

    Respiration of glucose takes place in three general steps: 1. Glycolysis: oxidation of glucose to pyruvic acid. 2. Krebs cycle: oxidation of acetyl CoA to CO2. 3. Electron transport chain: coenzymes that carry electrons from the Krebs cycle or glycolysis are oxidized to create ATP. Respiration is further divided into two types: - Aerobic respiration: if in the electron transport chain the final electron acceptor is oxygen, it is called aerobic respiration. - Anaerobic respiration: if the final electron acceptor is any inorganic compound other than oxygen, it is called anaerobic respiration. This final electron acceptor may be a nitrate ion, a sulfate ion, or a carbonate ion.

  65. Describe the process of fermentation.
    Topic: Microbial Metabolism • 3 marks marks

    The initial steps of glucose oxidation by fermentation are the same as they occur in glycolysis for respiration. However, when pyruvate is generated through glycolysis, electrons are also captured by NADH, and this NADH needs to be regenerated into NAD+ for recycling. If the final electron acceptor is one of the end products, such as ethanol, lactic acid, acetic acid etc., the process is called fermentation. Both anaerobic respiration and fermentation do not use oxygen; however, during fermentation there is no electron transport chain involved.

  66. Define oxidation and reduction. What is the difference between them?
    Topic: Microbial Metabolism • 2 marks marks

    Oxidation = loss of electrons, or gain of oxygen, or loss of hydrogen. Reduction = gain of electrons, or loss of oxygen, or gain of hydrogen. Difference: Oxidation is the process of loss of electrons, whereas reduction is the process of gain of electrons. Oxidation can also be the gaining of oxygen atoms, whereas reduction is the loss of oxygen atoms. Oxidation and reduction reactions are always coupled.

  67. What is a coenzyme? Give examples.
    Topic: Microbial Metabolism • 2 marks marks

    Coenzymes are organic non-protein molecules that bind with the protein molecule (apoenzyme) to form the active enzyme (holoenzyme). Coenzymes such as NAD, NADP, FAD, FMN etc. are used as carriers of electrons in oxidation-reduction reactions.

  68. What is catalase? Write its protocol/reaction.
    Topic: Microbial Metabolism • 3 marks marks

    When H2O2 is produced, it gives rise to peroxide anions which are toxic to the cells. Cells have evolved to deal with this toxic H2O2 by an enzyme called catalase, which breaks hydrogen peroxide down into water and oxygen (2H2O2 -> 2H2O + O2). In the catalase test, a colony is mixed with a drop of hydrogen peroxide; the appearance of bubbles (oxygen) shows the organism is catalase-positive.

  69. Explain the methods of microbial control in detail.
    Topic: Control of Microorganisms • 5 marks marks

    These methods can be broadly classified into four groups: Physical, Mechanical, Chemical and Biological. Physical methods — Heat: the cheapest and most easily available source to control microbial growth. Heat denatures enzymes. Thermal Death Point is the lowest temperature at which all cells in a culture are killed in 10 min (specific for a species). Thermal Death Time is the minimum time during which all cells in a culture are killed at a given temperature; higher temperatures take less time. Dry heat forms: hot-air oven, flaming (platinum loop in a Bunsen burner flame), and incineration (burning to ashes). Moist heat: autoclaving (15 psi), boiling at 100 C, Tyndallization (100 C for 3 consecutive days, for sugar solutions that can be degraded by autoclaving) and pasteurization (usually below 100 C). Pasteurization employs low heat to kill pathogenic and food-spoiling bacteria in milk but does not kill thermoduric organisms; milk can be pasteurized at 63 C for 30 min, high-temperature short-time 72 C for 15 sec, or ultra-high-temperature 140 C for 4 sec. Other physical methods: low temperature inhibits growth by slowing chemical reactions (refrigeration at 2-8 C, deep-freezing, lyophilization/freeze-drying); high pressure denatures proteins (used for preserving juices and wines); desiccation prevents metabolism; osmotic pressure causes plasmolysis (food stored in high salt or sugar). Filtration is also a physical method — air is filtered using HEPA filters and liquids using membrane filters. Radiation: ionizing radiation (gamma rays, X-rays, high-energy electron beams; wavelength less than about 1 nm) ionizes water, generating highly reactive hydroxyl radicals that damage DNA and cause mutations; it sterilizes medical plastic supplies, medicines and meat products. Non-ionizing radiation (e.g. UV light, wavelength longer than 1 nm; most useful at 260 nm, absorbed by DNA) causes thymine dimers that inhibit correct replication of DNA; it controls organisms in air and on surfaces but cannot penetrate deep into fluids. Ethylene oxide gas sterilizes heat-sensitive items such as disposable plastics, petri dishes, syringes, sutures and catheters; it is a strong alkylating agent that reacts with functional groups of DNA and proteins. Chemical methods: factors influencing the efficacy of a chemical disinfectant include its concentration, presence of organic matter, pH (disinfectants are more effective at acidic pH) and time of exposure. Alcohols (ethanol, isopropanol) denature proteins and dissolve lipids; 70% alcohol is more effective than 100% because alcohols require water; they kill vegetative bacteria and fungi but not spores, and are used in hand sanitizers. Heavy metals (silver, zinc, mercury, copper) have biocidal/antiseptic activity at very low concentration (oligodynamic action) and denature proteins — e.g. 1% silver nitrate, silver-sulfadiazine on burns, copper sulfate for green algae in ponds, zinc chloride in mouthwashes. Surface-active agents (surfactants) decrease surface tension: soaps and detergents cause mechanical removal of germs (degerming); quaternary ammonium compounds (quats, e.g. benzalkonium chloride) are cationic detergents that affect the plasma membrane, inhibit enzymes and denature proteins, but do not kill endospores and mycobacteria. Chemical food preservatives include sodium benzoate, sorbic acid, calcium propionate, and sodium nitrate/nitrite for meat; the antibiotics nisin and natamycin prevent spoilage of cheese. Aldehydes (formaldehyde and glutaraldehyde) are the most effective antimicrobials; they inactivate proteins by forming covalent cross-links with functional groups (-NH2, -OH, -COOH, -SH).

  70. Describe the dry heat methods of sterilization.
    Topic: Control of Microorganisms • 3 marks marks

    Forms of dry heat include: 1. Dry heat (hot-air oven) 2. Flaming (platinum loop sterilization in the flame of a Bunsen burner) 3. Incineration (burning to ashes) Similarly, moist heat can be grouped into autoclaving (15 psi), boiling at 100 C, Tyndallization (100 C for 3 consecutive days, to sterilize sugar solutions that can be degraded by autoclaving) and pasteurization (usually at a temperature lower than 100 C).

  71. Why is 70% alcohol more effective than 100% alcohol?
    Topic: Control of Microorganisms • 2 marks marks

    Ethanol and isopropanol are alcohols. Alcohols denature proteins and dissolve lipids. Alcohols require water for being more effective; this is the reason that 70% alcohol is more effective than 100%. Alcohols can effectively kill the vegetative forms of bacteria and fungi but not spores. Alcohols are not very effective on wounds. They are commonly used in hand sanitizers.

  72. Define antisepsis, sepsis and sterilization.
    Topic: Control of Microorganisms • 3 marks marks

    - Antisepsis: removing pathogens from living tissue. - Sepsis: microbial contamination; a toxic inflammatory condition arising from the spread of an organism from a focus of infection. - Sterilization: the complete removal or destruction of all microbial life (including endospores) from an object or habitat. - Sanitization: lowering microbial counts on eating utensils (to safe public health levels).

  73. Define thermal death point and thermal death time.
    Topic: Control of Microorganisms • 2 marks marks

    Thermal Death Point: the lowest temperature at which all cells in a culture are killed in 10 min. It is a specific temperature for a specific species of organism. Thermal Death Time: the minimum time during which all cells in a culture are killed at a given temperature. It varies from temperature to temperature for the same organism; obviously, higher temperatures take less time to kill the organisms than low temperatures.

  74. Write the uses of ionizing radiation for microbial control.
    Topic: Control of Microorganisms • 2 marks marks

    Ionizing radiation includes gamma rays, X-rays, or high-energy electron beams. Ionizing radiation possesses a wavelength shorter than that of non-ionizing radiation, less than about 1 nm. These short wavelengths cause ionization of water, generating highly reactive hydroxyl radicals which are damaging to the cells, as they bind to DNA and result in mutations. Medical plastic supplies, medicines and meat products can be sterilized by radiation.

  75. Write the characteristics of antibiotic chemicals used as food preservatives.
    Topic: Control of Microorganisms • 2 marks marks

    Although antibiotics are used to kill organisms in disease states, some antibiotics are not very effective for this purpose; however, these antibiotics can be used in food to prevent food spoilage. Nisin and natamycin prevent the spoilage of cheese.

  76. What is an F+ cell?
    Topic: Microbial Genetics and Identification • 2 marks marks

    In E. coli, the fertility factor (F factor) plasmid was the first plasmid observed to be transferred from one organism to another; hence, bacteria that have this plasmid are called F+ cells, to differentiate them from those that do not have one (F-). However, once F- cells acquire the F+ plasmid, they also become F+.

  77. What is an Hfr cell? Write the function of Hfr in conjugation.
    Topic: Microbial Genetics and Identification • 2 marks marks

    If the F factor gets integrated into the chromosome of the cell, the bacterium becomes a high frequency of recombination (Hfr) cell. When conjugation occurs between an Hfr cell and an F- cell, the Hfr cell's chromosome (with its integrated F factor) replicates and a parental strand of the chromosome is transferred to the recipient cell. Since this transfer starts from the middle of the F factor and is usually not complete, the whole chromosome is not transferred, but some genes are transferred and become integrated into the genomic DNA of the recipient cell. The F- cell remains F- because the F factor is not transferred completely. Because the genes are transferred in a definite order with time, conjugation can be used for gene mapping on the bacterial chromosome.

  78. What are vertical and horizontal gene transfer? Write the types of lateral (horizontal) gene transfer.
    Topic: Microbial Genetics and Identification • 3 marks marks

    Vertical gene transfer: the normal way of transferring genes from parents to offspring. This happens when a cell divides; each daughter cell receives exactly what its parent cell has. Horizontal (lateral) gene transfer: when genes are transferred from cell to cell within the same species — or across different species of organisms — the process is called horizontal gene transfer. It involves a donor and a recipient cell. The recipient cell incorporates the received DNA into its own genome, which becomes a recombinant molecule (recombinant DNA), and the cell is called a recombinant cell. Three processes are known by which genes can be transferred horizontally from one cell to another: 1. Transformation 2. Conjugation 3. Transduction

  79. Briefly explain conjugation, transformation and transduction.
    Topic: Microbial Genetics and Identification • 3 marks marks

    Conjugation: the process by which one bacterium transfers genetic material to another through direct contact. One bacterium serves as the donor and the other as the recipient. Gene transfer can be mediated by plasmids (extra-chromosomal circular DNA fragments that replicate independently of the bacterial chromosome). Conjugation requires direct cell-to-cell contact and the cells have to be of opposite mating types. Transduction: the process by which foreign DNA is introduced into a bacterial cell by a virus or viral vector — the transfer of a gene from one bacterium to another via a virus (bacteriophage). When a bacteriophage infects a bacterium such as E. coli, it replicates inside it and produces a protein coat (capsid) in which viral DNA is packed before the virus is released; bacterial DNA may be carried along to the next bacterium. Transformation: a process of horizontal gene transfer by which some bacteria take up foreign genetic material (naked DNA) from the environment. Naked DNA in solution is transferred from one bacterium to another. Bacteria able to take up free extracellular genetic material are termed competent cells.

  80. Define gene, chromosome, genome and phenotype.
    Topic: Microbial Genetics and Identification • 5 marks marks

    Gene: a segment of DNA that codes for a specific product (a polypeptide/protein or RNA) and determines a particular character of the organism. Chromosome: the large DNA molecule (in bacteria, usually a single circular double-stranded DNA) that carries the genes of the cell in a definite order. Genome: the complete set of genetic information (all the DNA/genes) present in a cell or organism. Phenotype: the observable characteristics or traits of an organism, produced by the expression of its genes (genotype) in a given environment.

  81. Differentiate between a clone and a strain.
    Topic: Microbial Genetics and Identification • 2 marks marks

    Clone: a population of cells derived from a single cell that are genetically identical. Strain: a genetic variant of a clone is called a strain.

  82. How can bacteria be identified? Describe the methods of identification of bacteria.
    Topic: Microbial Genetics and Identification • 5 marks marks

    Microbes, especially (disease-causing) bacteria, can be identified by three methods: A. Classical or conventional methods: differential staining of the sample, culturing the sample onto nutrient agar, blood agar and MacConkey's agar, purifying the culture (colonies expected to be involved in the disease) and detection of various enzymes of metabolic pathways. Classically, such methods used to take a long time (3 days at least); however, rapid identification methods are now available which use preformed media for testing the presence of metabolic enzymes. B. Serological methods: these involve antibody-antigen interactions. Antibodies are produced by B lymphocytes against foreign antigens such as bacteria and their toxins. Antibodies are very specific in their interaction — antibodies made against E. coli do not bind Staphylococci and vice versa. The main advantage of serological methods is speed and economy: they are quick (about an hour) and can work directly on the sample, which does not have to be cultured and purified. In all serological tests, either the serum or the antigen should be known to us. C. Nucleic acid based methods: identification based on the DNA/RNA of the organism.

  83. What is Western blotting? What is it used for?
    Topic: Microbial Genetics and Identification • 5 marks marks

    Western blotting is a technique used for the detection of antigen in the serum. Microbial proteins can be separated on SDS-PAGE by electrophoresis, and the presence of these proteins can be detected by enzyme-tagged antibodies specific to those proteins. A color band will be seen where the specific protein (antigen) is present on the gel. The proteins in the gel are first transferred to a paper strip before they can be detected by specific antibodies.

  84. Write the difference between precipitation and agglutination tests.
    Topic: Microbial Genetics and Identification • 2 marks marks

    Agglutination is a process in which soluble antibodies interact with a particulate antigen. Precipitation is a process in which soluble antibodies interact with a soluble antigen. Examples of agglutination tests: blood grouping (antibodies with particulate RBCs), Rose Bengal Plate Agglutination Test (for diagnosing brucellosis in animals), Widal test (Salmonella cells and Salmonella antibodies) and Coombs test. Agglutination tests are qualitative tests used to detect the presence of antibodies in serology laboratories and blood banks. Examples of precipitation tests: the agar gel diffusion test, in which antigens and antibodies diffuse from wells in agarose gel and a precipitation line forms where they meet; and precipitation of proteins, used to isolate a specific protein from cells using protein-specific antibodies. The precipitation test is used to quantify both antigen and antibody.

  85. Describe endospore staining.
    Topic: Microscopy and Staining

    Endospores appear as clear halos in Gram-stained smears, so a special staining technique is used for them. Malachite green is used as the primary stain; the smear is heated to steaming for about 5 minutes so that the stain penetrates the spore. The slide is then washed with water and counterstained with safranin. The endospores appear green while the vegetative cells appear pink/red.

  86. What are the methods used for viable counting of microbes?
    Topic: Microbial Growth and Culture

    Viable counting determines the number of living cells in a sample that are capable of multiplying and forming colonies. Its main methods are: 1. Plate count: the sample is plated on solid medium and each viable cell grows into a colony. It is done by the pour plate method (sample mixed with molten agar) and the spread plate method (sample spread over the surface of set agar). 2. Membrane filtration: a known volume of liquid is passed through a membrane filter which traps the bacteria; the filter is then placed on culture medium and the colonies that grow are counted. 3. Most Probable Number (MPN): a statistical dilution method used for samples whose cells do not form distinct colonies on plates.

    Topic: Microbial Growth and Culture • 3 marks marks

    Bacteria obtain sulphur from: • Sulfate ions • Hydrogen sulfide • Sulfur-containing amino acids

  88. Name the chemicals that cause damage (disruption) of the plasma membrane.
    Topic: Control of Microorganisms

    Phenol and phenolics were the first widely used antiseptics, and they kill microbes by disrupting (damaging) their plasma membranes. Alcohols and quaternary ammonium compounds also act against microbes by damaging the cell membrane.
